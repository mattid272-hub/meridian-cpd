## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- Explain how surface condensation forms, using relative humidity, dew point and surface temperature.
- Identify the common thermal bridges at junctions in UK housing and the mould patterns they tend to produce.
- Distinguish condensation from penetrating damp, rising damp and plumbing leaks using visual evidence and simple checks.
- Explain the limits of moisture meters on masonry, including the effect of salts and calibration for timber, and what that means for your report.
- Record findings and refer appropriately, without going beyond an assessor's competence.

## Introduction

Every energy and retrofit assessor walks into homes with mould on the walls. The householder often asks what it is and what to do. The landlord may want a cause named in the report. The retrofit design that follows may make the problem better or worse. This course is about reading what you see well enough to record it accurately, to recognise which kind of moisture problem is likely, and to know when to stop and refer.

It does not train you to carry out a damp survey. The government's damp and mould guidance for landlords says that assessment of damp and mould "should be carried out by appropriately qualified contractors", and that landlords "should not assume they will be able to diagnose the problem themselves in all instances" (Understanding and addressing the health risks of damp and mould in the home, MHCLG and others, updated 1 April 2026, "Identifying and addressing damp and mould in your property"). The same caution applies to assessors. Your job is to recognise and record. Naming a cause with certainty is someone else's job.

### Where this course applies

This course is written for England and Wales. Most of the building physics applies anywhere, but the regulatory sources differ by nation:

## Section 1: Where Moisture in Homes Comes From

### Four sources, one symptom

Mould and damp staining are symptoms. The government's damp and mould guidance names four kinds of damp that produce them ("Understanding damp and mould"):
- Condensation damp "happens when moisture generated inside the home cools and condenses onto colder parts of the buildings (for example window frames, corners and low points on walls behind sofas or wardrobes)". The guidance adds: "This is the most common form of damp."
- Penetrating damp "is water that gets into the building from outside due to defects in the walls, roofs, windows or floors."
- Rising damp "is moisture from the ground that rises up through parts of the buildings in contact with the ground (walls and floors); it is usually found in older properties and is often misdiagnosed."
- Traumatic damp "can be caused by leaking water from waste and heating pipes, overflowing baths or sinks, burst pipes or defective water storage vessels inside the building." It "can also originate from outside the property", for example from another building or from flooding.

Hold on to the phrase "often misdiagnosed". It is the government's own warning, in guidance written for landlords, that this category is easy to get wrong.

The guidance is also clear that the type of damp does not change the landlord's position: "Irrespective of the type of damp (condensation, penetrating, rising or traumatic), landlords are legally responsible for addressing damp and mould" in the circumstances it describes ("Understanding damp and mould"). For an assessor, this means the cause matters for what the landlord should do next, not for whether the problem should be reported.

### Moisture made inside the home

The guidance describes the inside source plainly: "Moisture from normal occupant activities, such as showering, drying laundry and cooking, builds up in the air around a home if it cannot be removed." It gives no figure for how much water a household produces each day, and this course does not quote one. Figures for daily moisture production circulate widely, but none was found in a primary source opened for this course.

Two points from the guidance shape how you should write about this:
- It says "it is totally unreasonable to blame damp and mould in the home on 'lifestyle choices'". It identifies the usual fundamental causes as building deficiencies, inadequate ventilation, inadequate heating and poor energy efficiency.
- It says tenants should not be expected to reduce moisture if their home does not allow them to.

So a report that says "mould caused by tenant drying clothes indoors" is both outside your competence and at odds with government guidance. A report that says "clothes drying on radiators in bedroom 2 at time of visit; no extract fan in that room" records facts.

### Moisture from outside and below

Penetrating and rising damp bring water in from outside the occupied space. The guidance's external checks for landlords include "damaged or blocked pipes, gutters or downpipes", "broken seals around windows or ill-fitting windows that do not close fully", "visible structural or facade defects, such as cracks in render or the foundation, missing or broken roof finishes" and a "bridged (compromised) damp proof course" ("Internal and external checks"). Each of these is something an assessor can see and record without any instrument.

On rising damp specifically, the guidance says it "can be identified through visual inspection; however chemical testing is the most appropriate way of confirming it", and that "often it is due to defective damp proof courses and membranes" ("Understanding damp and mould"). Chemical testing is not part of an energy or retrofit assessment. That is a referral point, covered in Section 7.

### Moisture already in the fabric

Approved Document C defines two forms of condensation (England edition, paragraph 0.12):
- Surface condensation: "Deposition of liquid water from a vapour, occurring on visible surfaces within the building."
- Interstitial condensation: deposition of liquid water from a vapour "occurring within or between the layers of the building envelope."

Paragraph 0.6 explains why both matter: "Surface condensation from the water vapour generated within the building can cause moulds to grow", and "Interstitial condensation may cause damage to the structure."

You can see surface condensation. You usually cannot see interstitial condensation; you see its consequences later, if at all. That difference matters for retrofit, because insulation changes where in a wall the cold, and so the condensation risk, sits. Section 3 returns to this.

### How common is damp?

The English Housing Survey is the main source for England. Its 2022 to 2023 headline report says (Chapter 4, Dwelling condition, "Damp"):
- "In 2022, 4% or 1 million dwellings had a problem with damp."
- "Damp problems were more prevalent in private rented dwellings, with 9% reported to have a problem in 2022, compared to 5% of social renters and just 2% of owner occupiers."
- "In 2022, 7% of local authority dwellings and 4% of housing association dwellings had damp."
- After "a sizable reduction" between 1996 and 2011, "incidences of damp have increased since 2019 from 3%".

The survey's definition is narrower than everyday usage. A home "is considered to have damp or a problem with damp if the surveyor records damp which is significant enough to be taken into consideration when making their HHSRS assessments", so "minor issues of damp are not recorded". The report also warns that 2022 data combines measured and modelled data and that "comparisons with previous years should be considered with caution". It reports a single damp measure, not separate figures for each type.

The government's Ethnicity facts and figures service, using the same survey, reports that "in the 2 years to March 2023, an average of 4% of households in England had damp in at least one room" (Housing with damp problems, published 30 April 2025). It defines a home with damp as having rising damp, penetrating damp, or "serious condensation or mould".

The damp and mould guidance gives a wider range: "Estimates of the number of homes in England with damp and mould range from 4% to 27% of homes, or 962,000 to 6.5 million households", depending on how damp and mould is measured ("Introduction"). The lower end reflects surveyor-recorded damp of the kind the HHSRS takes into account; higher estimates use broader measures.

This course did not find an equivalent current figure for Wales in a source it opened. Do not apply the English percentages to Welsh housing.

### Why this matters to assessors

On a typical working week you will see more mould than the 4% figure suggests, because the survey counts only damp significant enough for an HHSRS assessment. Most of what you see will be minor surface mould around windows and in corners. Some of it will be serious. Recording both accurately, without inflating or dismissing either, is the skill this course builds.

## Section 2: Condensation Mechanics

### Relative humidity and dew point

Two definitions do most of the work. Both come from the World Meteorological Organization's humidity calibration training unit, which draws on the WMO Guide to Instruments and Methods of Observation (WMO-No. 8):
- Relative humidity is the ratio between the partial vapour pressure of the water vapour in the air and the saturation vapour pressure "over a flat surface of liquid water" at the same temperature and pressure, expressed as a percentage.
- Dew-point temperature is "the temperature to which a humid air must be cooled for water vapour to condense into liquid water."

The key is that saturation vapour pressure depends strongly on temperature. Warm air can hold much more water vapour than cold air. Take a given quantity of vapour and cool it, and its relative humidity rises until, at the dew point, it reaches 100% and water starts to come out.

The same training unit gives the WMO approximation for saturation vapour pressure over water, valid from minus 45°C to plus 60°C (citing WMO-No. 8, Volume I, 2023 edition, Annex 4.B):

e_w(t) = 6.112 × exp(17.62 × t / (243.12 + t)), with t in °C.

You never need to use this on site. It is given so the worked numbers below can be checked. Because relative humidity is a ratio, the units of e_w cancel out in every calculation below.

### Worked example: dew point in a living room

Using that formula, a room at 20°C has the following dew points (course calculation):
- At 50% RH, the dew point is about 9°C.
- At 60% RH, the dew point is about 12°C.
- At 70% RH, the dew point is about 14°C.

So in a room at 20°C and 60% RH, any surface colder than about 12°C will collect liquid water. The guidance's own examples of where condensation forms are "window frames, corners and low points on walls behind sofas or wardrobes". A lintel soffit or a cold reveal can be another.

### Mould does not need liquid water

Here is the point many householders, and some reports, miss. Mould can start on a surface that never looks wet. What matters is the relative humidity at the surface, not in the middle of the room.

Air touching a cold surface is cooled to that surface's temperature. Its vapour content stays about the same as the room air's, but its saturation vapour pressure falls. So the relative humidity right at the surface is higher than the room reading. This follows directly from the WMO definition above.

Course calculation for a room at 20°C (surface RH = room vapour pressure divided by saturation vapour pressure at the surface temperature):

| Surface temperature | Room at 50% RH | Room at 60% RH | Room at 70% RH |
|---|---|---|---|
| 18°C | 57% | 68% | 79% |
| 16°C | 64% | 77% | 90% |
| 15°C | 69% | 82% | 96% |
| 14°C | 73% | 88% | Condenses |
| 12°C | 83% | Condenses | Condenses |
| 10°C | 95% | Condenses | Condenses |

Read across the 15°C row. A room at a comfortable 20°C and an unremarkable 60% RH puts the air at a 15°C surface at about 82% RH. Nothing is visibly wet. But that surface may well grow mould.

### The critical surface humidity for mould

How high does surface humidity have to be? Two sources opened for this course address it.

Approved Document F Volume 1 (2021 edition, England) sets a performance criterion for moisture: "there should be no visible mould on the inner surfaces of the external walls of a properly heated dwelling with typical moisture generation" (Appendix B, paragraph B2). It notes that "Mould can grow whether the dwelling is occupied or unoccupied", so the humidity criterion "should be met at all times" (B3). Paragraph B10 says the moisture criteria are likely to be met if, during the colder months, the moving average "surface water activity of the internal surfaces of external walls" is always below the values in Table B2:

| Moving average period | Surface water activity (Table B2) |
|---|---|
| 1 month | 0.75 |
| 1 week | 0.85 |
| 1 day | 0.95 |

For new dwellings, paragraph B11 gives room air limits that are likely to meet those criteria (Table B3):

| Moving average period | Indoor air relative humidity (Table B3) |
|---|---|
| 1 month | 65% |
| 1 week | 75% |
| 1 day | 85% |

Surface water activity is a fraction from 0 to 1. Read it as the relative humidity at the surface: 0.75 corresponds to 75%. (Approved Document F does not spell out that equivalence; it is this course's explanation.) The structure of the tables is the useful lesson. Short spikes are tolerated; sustained high surface humidity is not. A bathroom that hits 90% for an hour after a shower and dries out is a different case from a bedroom wall that sits at 80% for weeks.

A UCL research paper on the hygrothermal performance of construction details, by Mumovic and others, treated 80% surface RH as "the point at which surface mould growth may become problematic", citing BS 5250:2002 (conclusions; the copy read is undated, with references from 2004). This course could not open BS 5250:2002 or its 2021 replacement to confirm the figure.

Put those together and you have a working concept: there is a critical surface humidity, in the region of 75% to 80% sustained over weeks, above which mould becomes likely. The exact figure depends on the source, the averaging period and the surface. Do not quote a single number as a hard threshold in a report.

### Surface temperature is the lever

Combine the table above with that critical range and you can see why surface temperature is the variable that decides most cases. At 20°C and 60% RH:
- a surface at 18°C sits at about 68%: comfortably safe;
- a surface at 16°C sits at about 77%: borderline;
- a surface at 15°C sits at about 82%: likely to support mould if sustained;
- a surface at 12°C is at the dew point: visibly wet.

Course calculation: for a 15°C surface to stay at or below 80% surface RH, a room at 20°C must stay at or below about 58% RH. For a 10°C surface, the room must stay at or below about 42% RH, which is hard to sustain in an occupied home in winter.

So the colder a surface, the drier the room must be to keep it free of mould. That is the bridge between condensation and thermal bridging.

### The temperature factor (fRsi)

Designers express how cold a junction runs using a temperature factor, written fRsi. PAS 2035:2023 lists "critical temperature factors (fRsi) at the corners, junctions and edges of building envelopes" and "assessing the risk of surface condensation and mould growth using temperature factors" among the calculation techniques in clause 4.3.3. It says Retrofit Assessors, Retrofit Designers, Retrofit Coordinators and Retrofit Evaluators "should be familiar" with those techniques (clause 4.3.6). Familiarity is not a duty to calculate.

The usual definition expresses the internal surface temperature as a fraction of the way from the outside air temperature to the inside air temperature:

fRsi = (internal surface temperature − external air temperature) ÷ (internal air temperature − external air temperature)

This course could not open the documents that formally define fRsi (BRE IP1/06 and BS EN ISO 13788), so treat this as a working statement of the idea and check the formal definition, including the surface resistance conventions, in those documents before using it in design work.

Course calculation with that formula, inside 20°C and outside 0°C:
- fRsi 0.9 gives a surface at 18°C.
- fRsi 0.75 gives a surface at 15°C.
- fRsi 0.6 gives a surface at 12°C.
- fRsi 0.5 gives a surface at 10°C.

A higher fRsi means a warmer surface. A perfectly insulated junction would approach 1; a junction with no insulation and a highly conductive path to outside falls towards 0.

### The 0.75 figure: what the opened sources say

The figure 0.75 is widely associated with dwellings. Here is exactly what the sources opened for this course say:
- PAS 2035:2023, clause 8.2.16: construction details shall "maintain the continuity of the three-dimensional insulated envelope". Published good-practice details "can be used". For any other detail "it shall be shown by calculating it in accordance with IP1/06" that "the temperature factor (fRsi) of each detail is not less than 0.75."
- Approved Document L Volume 1 (2021 edition incorporating 2023 amendments, England), paragraph 4.18(a): one route to limiting thermal bridging in new dwellings is to "Use construction joint details calculated by a suitably competent person following the guidance in the Building Research Establishment's BR 497 and the temperature factors set out in the Building Research Establishment's Information Paper 1/06." Approved Document L does not itself state the value.
- Approved Document C (England, 2013 amendments) does not use the term temperature factor or the figure 0.75 in the text read for this course. Its surface condensation provisions for floors and walls are framed differently (Section 3).

So for retrofit work under PAS 2035, 0.75 is a clause 8.2.16 requirement for any detail that is not a published good-practice detail. For new dwellings in England, Approved Document L points to IP1/06's temperature factors, which this course could not open.

Note also the arithmetic above. At fRsi 0.75, with 20°C inside and 0°C outside, the surface sits at 15°C. In a room at 60% RH that surface is at about 82% RH. The design threshold is not a promise that no mould will ever grow; it rests on assumed internal and external conditions set out in IP1/06, which this course could not check. A home with high moisture production and little extract can grow mould on a junction that meets 0.75.

### What you can take to site

You will not calculate fRsi on a visit. But the concept explains what you see:
- Mould concentrated on one junction, with clean wall either side, points to a locally colder surface.
- Mould spread across the whole of an external wall, worst low down and behind furniture, points to high room humidity combined with a generally cold wall.
- Mould on internal walls well away from external surfaces is less typical of condensation and is worth a closer look for another cause.

Those are patterns, not conclusions. Section 4 shows how to use them.

## Section 3: Thermal Bridging at Junctions

### What a thermal bridge is

PAS 2035:2023 defines a thermal bridge as an area where insulation is discontinuous, thinner or less effective, "leading to locally increased heat loss" and "locally reduced internal surface temperature" (clause 3.36). Approved Document L Volume 1 puts it the same way: "Thermal bridges occur when an area of a building has significantly higher heat transfer than the surrounding parts. Breaks in insulation, reduced insulation or more conductive materials can contribute to thermal bridge effects" (paragraph 4.16).

PAS 2035:2023 also defines thermal by-pass: "unintended penetration or circulation of external air on the warm side of the insulation layer in a construction", which renders the insulation ineffective (clause 3.37). By-pass is not the same as bridging, but it has the same symptom: a surface colder than the design intended.

The consequence for moisture is spelled out in Approved Document L: gaps in insulation "can have a significant impact on heat loss and thermal bypass and create a risk of condensation and mould" (paragraph 4.14).

### Where the bridges are: the junction checklist

Approved Document L paragraph 4.17 lists, for new dwellings, the junctions where continuity of insulation must be detailed. It is written for new build, but it is also the clearest official list of where thermal bridges sit in UK housing. Paragraph 4.19 tells those working in existing dwellings that "care should be taken to reduce unwanted heat loss through thermal bridging" and to "Follow the guidance in paragraph 4.17 where appropriate."

The junctions named in paragraph 4.17 are:
- Ground floor to external wall: wall-to-floor junctions "should be detailed to achieve continuity of insulation" (4.17(f)). In masonry, external or cavity wall insulation "should extend below the damp-proof course (where applicable) and be at least the equivalent of one full block height (215mm) below the underside of the floor structure/slab" (4.17(f)(ii)).
- Intermediate floors: floor-to-wall junctions "should be detailed to ensure that insulation in the external wall is continuous" (4.17(g)).
- Windows: "designs should minimise thermal bridging" (4.17(h)). For lintels, "consider using independent lintels with an insulated cavity closure between the inner and outer lintel" (4.17(h)(i)). "Insulated cavity closers should be used for all construction types", and "insulated plasterboard should be used in reveals to abut jambs and should be considered within reveal soffits" (4.17(h)(ii)).
- Roofs at eaves and gables: "continue the insulation across the wall-to-eaves and wall-to-gable junctions" (4.17(i)). For roofs insulated at ceiling level, "loft insulation at the eaves should extend beyond the wall insulation without any reduction in thickness due to the pitch of the roof" (4.17(i)(ii)).
- Gables and party walls: "At gables and party walls, insulation should extend to the wall; if the space between the wall and joist is less than 100mm, perimeter insulation may be required" (4.17(i)(ii)).
- Party walls generally: paragraph 4.16 says thermal bridging, "including at the party wall", should be reasonably limited.

PAS 2035:2023 clause 8.2.15 requires the retrofit design, where fabric measures are included, to include details "for the corners, junctions and edges of all affected building elements", including the combinations marked in its Figure D.1. This course did not have the text of Annex D in full and does not reproduce Figure D.1.

### The mould pattern each junction tends to produce

What follows describes typical patterns. The sources above establish where the bridges are and that bridges lower surface temperature. The descriptions of how mould tends to present at each are this course's practical reading of that physics, not quotations. Treat them as reasons to look closely, never as proof.

**Window reveals, heads and sills.** Where the wall's insulation stops short of the window frame, or the frame sits forward of a cavity closer, the reveal is colder than the wall face. Mould tends to appear as a band down the jamb, along the head, or in the corners where reveal meets frame. Condensation on the glass often runs down and wets the sill and the bottom of the reveal, so the bottom corners are frequently worst. Approved Document L's advice to use insulated plasterboard in reveals (4.17(h)(ii)) exists for this reason.

**Lintels.** A steel or concrete lintel can conduct heat across the cavity. The soffit above the window, and a strip of wall directly above the opening, may show a line of mould or a darker strip of dust (pattern staining) that follows the lintel's length. Approved Document L's reference to "common leaf lintels" whose "base plate should not be continuous" (4.17(h)(i)) describes the detail that causes this.

**Floor perimeter.** At the junction of a ground floor and an external wall, heat can leak down through the wall below floor level. Look for mould along the skirting line of external walls, worst in corners and behind furniture. The government guidance's own example of where condensation forms includes "low points on walls behind sofas or wardrobes" ("Understanding damp and mould"). Furniture against a cold wall cuts off the warm air that would otherwise reach it, lowering the surface temperature further.

**External corners.** At an external corner, two cold faces meet and the inside surface area is small compared with the outside area losing heat. Mould tends to start in the vertical line of the corner and, very often, in the top or bottom corner where a third surface (ceiling or floor) also meets. Top corners of rooms under a cold roof edge combine this with the eaves junction.

**Eaves and ceiling edges.** Where loft insulation stops short of the wall head, or is compressed at the eaves, the ceiling perimeter on external walls runs cold. Mould tends to show as a band along the ceiling edge on external walls, often darkest at the corners. Approved Document L's warning about insulation "without any reduction in thickness due to the pitch of the roof" (4.17(i)(ii)) targets exactly this.

**Intermediate floors.** On upper floors, the zone where floor joists or a concrete slab meet the external wall can be a bridge, particularly in timber frame where "the intermediate floor structure breaches the external wall insulation" (Approved Document L, 4.17(g)). Inside, look at the ceiling-wall junction of the room below and the floor-wall junction of the room above.

**Party walls.** A party wall at a gable or in the loft can bypass insulation; paragraph 4.17(i)(ii) addresses the gap between the wall and the nearest joist. At ceiling level in top-floor rooms, a cold strip along the party wall junction is possible where the loft insulation does not reach the wall.

### Why retrofit insulation can move or worsen cold spots

This is the heart of the retrofit risk. Insulation that is not continuous does not only leave the gap as cold as before; it changes how the room behaves around the gap.

PAS 2035:2023 recognises the problem directly. Clause 8.2.17 says: "If a stage-by-stage approach to insulation is chosen", the design must "include provisions for mitigating the risks relating to cold surfaces", including condensation and mould. Clause 8.2.16 requires details that "maintain the continuity of the three-dimensional insulated envelope".

The mechanisms, as this course reads them from those clauses and the physics in Section 2:
- **The junction becomes the coldest surface in the room.** Before insulation, the whole wall may have been cold, and moisture spread across it. After insulation, the main wall warms but an uninsulated reveal, floor edge or eaves stays cold. That junction becomes the place where surface humidity is highest, and mould can concentrate there.
- **Airtightness raises room humidity.** Approved Document F says "Energy efficiency measures carried out on existing dwellings might reduce infiltration and cause the dwelling to become under-ventilated" (paragraph 3.6). Replacing windows "is likely to increase the airtightness of the dwelling" (paragraph 3.15). Higher room humidity pushes every cold surface closer to its critical humidity.
- **Interstitial risk moves.** Adding insulation changes the temperature profile through the wall. PAS 2035:2023 notes that a standard BS EN ISO 13788 assessment "might not be sufficient" for some walls, such as solid walls with internal insulation in exposed locations (clause 4.3.3, note 1). Clause 8.2.10 requires the design to "include provision for the management of moisture within the construction, in line with BS 5250", and its note says "The materials specified should match the moisture strategy of the existing building."

The government's damp and mould guidance makes the same connection for landlords: "some energy efficiency interventions may reduce overall ventilation rates", and "If ventilation is not addressed, this can increase the risk of damp and mould, poor indoor air quality and overheating in warmer weather" ("Reducing the risk of damp and mould developing").

### Traditional buildings

Approved Document L warns that energy efficiency of historic and traditional dwellings "should be improved only if doing so will not cause long-term deterioration of the building's fabric or fittings", particularly for buildings "with a vapour permeable construction that both absorbs moisture and readily allows moisture to evaporate", such as "wattle and daub, cob or stone and constructions using lime render or mortar" (paragraph 0.10).

For assessors this has two practical consequences. First, recording construction accurately (solid stone, lime render, cement repairs) is a moisture finding in its own right, because it tells the designer what moisture strategy the wall has. Second, where cement render, impermeable paint or raised ground levels have been added to a traditional wall, the wall's ability to dry may have changed. Record what you see. Do not speculate on cause.

### Ventilation and the Approved Document F link

Approved Document F says "Without adequate ventilation, mould and internal air pollution might become hazardous to health" (paragraph 1.1). Building work "should not reduce the ventilation provision of the dwelling unless it can be demonstrated that the ventilation provision after the work is carried out meets the minimum standards of requirement F1(1)" (paragraph 3.6). When carrying out energy efficiency measures, "an assessment should determine what, if any, additional ventilation provision is needed" (paragraph 3.7), and "Following BSI PAS 2035 is considered to be an adequate means of demonstrating compliance with paragraph 3.6" (note to paragraph 3.7).

The minimum intermittent extract rates in Table 1.1 of Approved Document F Volume 1 (2021) are a useful reference when you record extract fans:

| Room | Minimum intermittent extract rate |
|---|---|
| Kitchen, cooker hood extracting to outside | 30 l/s |
| Kitchen, no cooker hood or one not extracting to outside | 60 l/s |
| Utility room | 30 l/s |
| Bathroom | 15 l/s |
| Sanitary accommodation | 6 l/s |

You will not usually measure flow on an energy assessment. But recording "extract fan present, not running when switched on" or "no extract in bathroom" is evidence the designer and coordinator need.

### Approved Document C: the floor and wall provisions

Approved Document C (England) requires that the walls, floors and roof "shall adequately protect the building and people who use the building" from harmful effects including "interstitial and surface condensation" (Requirement C2(c)). For floors, paragraph 4.5 says "All floors should not promote surface condensation or mould growth, given reasonable occupancy conditions", and paragraph 5.2(f) applies the same words to walls.

The guidance on how to meet this is in paragraph 4.22 (floors) and paragraph 5.36 (walls). Both say the requirement will be met if the thermal transmittance (U-value) "does not exceed 0.7W/m2K at any point" and the junctions between elements are designed to "Accredited Construction Details, or follow the guidance of BRE IP17/01". The Wales edition's paragraph 4.22 is worded the same way on the U-value but refers to "robust construction details" instead of Accredited Construction Details.

Note what Approved Document C does and does not say. It sets a maximum U-value "at any point" of 0.7 W/m²K as a surface condensation safeguard. It does not, in the text read for this course, use the temperature factor or the figure 0.75. And it cites IP17/01, an earlier BRE information paper, where Approved Document L and PAS 2035 cite IP1/06. If you see a report or a training slide saying "Approved Document C requires fRsi of 0.75", that is not what the England 2013 text opened for this course says.

## Section 4: Telling the Twins Apart

### Why the twins get confused

Condensation, penetrating damp, rising damp and leaks can all leave wet patches, staining and mould. They can coexist in the same room. And the treatments are very different: an extract fan does nothing for a leaking gutter, and injecting a chemical damp proof course does nothing for condensation. The government's guidance warns that rising damp in particular "is often misdiagnosed" ("Understanding damp and mould").

You are not there to name the cause. But a good record of the pattern, the location and the context lets the person who does name it get it right first time. And it lets you decide when a referral is needed.

### The comparison table

The definitions in the first column are quoted from the government's damp and mould guidance. The other columns are this course's practical reading of what each definition implies about appearance and context. They are indicators to record, not tests that settle the cause.

| | Condensation | Penetrating damp | Rising damp | Leak (traumatic damp) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Definition (guidance) | Moisture generated inside "cools and condenses onto colder parts of the buildings" | Water "from outside due to defects in the walls, roofs, windows or floors" | Moisture "from the ground that rises up through parts of the buildings in contact with the ground" | Leaking water from "waste and heating pipes, overflowing baths or sinks, burst pipes or defective water storage vessels" |
| Where it tends to show | Cold surfaces: glass, reveals, corners, behind furniture, external walls at low level and ceiling edges | Any height on an external wall, ceiling or chimney breast, often below a visible external defect | Low on walls in contact with the ground | Near pipes, wet rooms, tanks, radiators; can be on internal walls and ceilings below wet rooms |
| Typical appearance | Mould spotting or bloom, water droplets on glass, damp to touch in cold weather | A defined patch, possibly with tide marks, which may spread outwards | A band low on the wall, possibly with tide marks, salt deposits or blown plaster | A defined patch, often with a clear edge, growing from a point |
| When it is worse | Cold weather, morning, after cooking, bathing or drying laundry | After rain, especially wind-driven rain on exposed elevations | Relatively steady, though it may vary with ground water | When the fitting is used, or constantly if a supply pipe is leaking |
| Outside clues | None specific; the fabric may be sound | "damaged or blocked pipes, gutters or downpipes", cracks in render, "missing or broken roof finishes", poor window seals | "bridged (compromised) damp proof course", raised ground or paths | Overflow pipes running, stained soffits below |
| Mould? | Common | Possible | Possible | Possible, especially if long-standing |

The quoted phrases in the "Outside clues" row come from the guidance's list of external checks ("Internal and external checks"). Note the absence of tide marks, salts or rising damp heights from the guidance: it does not describe these, and this course does not give a height figure.

### The one detail that usually separates them

If you remember only one thing per pair, use these. They are reasoning from the definitions, not rules in a source.

- **Condensation versus penetrating damp:** Does it follow the weather outside or the life inside? Condensation follows cold surfaces and indoor moisture; it is usually worst in the coldest corners and behind furniture regardless of which wall faces the weather. Penetrating damp follows rain and a defect; it tends to sit below or beside the external problem and to change after wet weather.
- **Condensation versus rising damp:** Is it confined to the bottom of the wall? Condensation at a floor perimeter often sits right at skirting level and in corners, and comes with mould. Rising damp, as the guidance defines it, rises from the ground through walls in contact with it. The guidance says chemical testing is the most appropriate way to confirm it. A band low on the wall that is not obviously linked to cold, furniture or moisture-producing rooms is a referral.
- **Condensation versus a leak:** Is there a source point? A leak usually grows from somewhere: a pipe run, a joint, a wet room above. Condensation is usually diffuse across cold surfaces. A patch on an internal wall or a ceiling below a bathroom is a leak until shown otherwise.
- **Penetrating damp versus rising damp:** Height and outside evidence. Penetrating damp can appear at any height and usually has an external defect nearby. Rising damp, by definition, is confined to parts of the building in contact with the ground.

### Simple checks an assessor can make

None of these needs specialist equipment. All of them produce facts you can record.

**Inside:**
- Where exactly is the mould or staining? Note room, wall, orientation, height above floor, and whether it is on an external, party or internal wall.
- Is it on a junction (reveal, lintel soffit, corner, skirting, ceiling edge) or across a field of wall?
- Is there furniture against it? Move nothing heavy; note what is there.
- Is there water on the glass? Is the sill wet?
- What is in the room: laundry drying, a tumble dryer, a fish tank, a bath or shower with the door shut?
- Are there extract fans in the kitchen and bathroom? Do they run? Are trickle vents present, open or closed, or painted shut? The guidance says trickle vents "should be controllable by the tenant, rather than permanently open" ("Reducing the risk of damp and mould developing").
- Is the heating working and in use? The guidance's internal checks include "ineffective or broken heating systems" ("Internal and external checks").
- Are there signs of a leak: staining below a wet room, around a tank, a radiator valve or a pipe run?

**Outside:**
- Gutters and downpipes: blocked, overflowing, broken, discharging onto the wall?
- Render and pointing: cracked, missing, bulging?
- Roof coverings and flashings: missing, slipped, damaged?
- Window seals and sills: gaps, missing drips?
- Ground level against the wall: above the damp proof course line, or paths bridging it?

**In the loft (if your scope includes it and access is safe):**
- Is insulation continuous to the eaves, or does it stop short or compress?
- Is there mould on the underside of the roof deck or on rafters?
- Do bathroom or kitchen extract ducts terminate in the loft rather than outside?

### Mixed causes

A home can have more than one moisture source at once. A wall with a blocked gutter may also be a cold wall in a poorly ventilated room. A record that lists everything you saw, without forcing a single cause, serves the landlord and the designer best. The government guidance itself tells landlords that "Simply removing surface mould will not prevent the damp and mould from reappearing" ("Identifying and addressing damp and mould in your property"): if you record only the mould and not the context, the underlying causes may not be found.

## Section 5: Instruments and Their Limits

### The question meters are asked to answer

On many visits someone will ask for, or expect, a moisture meter reading. The instrument is quick, the number looks authoritative, and a high reading appears to settle the question. On masonry and plaster it usually does not. The best primary source this course could open on this is a 2022 Historic England research report that tested handheld moisture meters against weighed samples.

### The Historic England evidence

Historic England Research Report 2/2022, "A Comparative Evaluation of Methods to Monitor Moisture in Historic Porous Masonry Materials", by Heather Viles, Hong Zhang and Scott Orr, tested resistance, capacitance and microwave handheld meters, along with invasive methods, on blocks of limestone, sandstone, brick and lime mortar under controlled wetting and drying, and compared each against weight measurements. The findings that matter for assessors:

- **Calibrated for timber.** One of the resistance meters tested gives its scale as a "wood moisture equivalent", and the report's description of it notes that "readings above 30% are relative" (Table 1). The scale is set up for wood. On masonry the number is not the moisture content of the wall.
- **Salts change the reading.** "All the measurement methods showed a strong effect of salinity" (section 1.4.7). Readings at the same moisture level "differed greatly depending on whether or not the water was saline". The conclusions call this "an important and predictable influence" on several methods, including the resistance meters (section 1.5).
- **Materials cannot be compared.** "The clear conclusion is that it is not advisable to directly compare meter readings from different building materials" (section 1.5). A reading on brick and a reading on mortar are not on the same scale.
- **Surface only.** The resistance meters "are only sensitive to moisture contents in Clipsham limestone near the surface (<5mm)" (section 2.4.3).
- **Narrow useful range.** One resistance meter in resistance mode "is only effective for c 0–2% moisture contents on Portland limestone" (section 1.4.3).
- **Semi-quantitative at best, in a laboratory.** Most techniques gave "good semi-quantitative estimates of moisture levels" under controlled conditions (summary), and the report notes its findings apply to its laboratory conditions (foreword). Under wetting, "Most devices gave more variable (noisier) results" (section 2.4.1).
- **Know the material.** "knowledge of material types is very important for interpreting the output from different moisture measurement techniques" (section 1.4.8).

The report studied historic masonry materials. It did not test modern gypsum plasters, cement renders or plasterboard, and this course has no primary source on meter behaviour on those materials. The principles about calibration, salts and surface sensitivity are general features of how the meters work, but treat any extension to other materials with caution.

### Why salts matter so much in damp walls

Rising damp and long-term penetrating damp carry dissolved salts into the wall. When water evaporates at the surface, the salts stay behind. The government guidance names chemical testing as "the most appropriate way of confirming" rising damp ("Understanding damp and mould"). Putting that together with the Historic England finding: a wall that once was wet can keep giving high resistance meter readings after it has dried, because the salts conduct. And a high reading on a wall with salt contamination tells you little about how much water is in it now.

This course could not open a primary source that quantifies how long such readings persist or how hygroscopic salts behave in plaster. It states the principle only.

### What this means for your report

- Do not report a moisture meter reading on masonry or plaster as a moisture content. If you record one at all, record it as a relative reading on the meter's own scale, with the meter type, the material, the location and the height.
- Do not use a single high reading as evidence of rising damp. The guidance says confirming rising damp is a matter for chemical testing, and the Historic England report shows salts drive readings.
- Comparisons between locations on the same material, taken the same day with the same meter, can help you describe a pattern ("readings higher at 200 mm than at 1 m on the same wall"). Comparisons across materials cannot.
- On timber, such as skirtings and floor joists you can see, a resistance meter is working on the material it was set up for, but this course has no opened primary source on timber reading thresholds and does not give one.

### Other instruments

A thermo-hygrometer reading of room temperature and relative humidity is meaningful in the sense of Section 2, but a single spot reading is a snapshot. Approved Document F's criteria are moving averages over a day, a week and a month (Tables B2 and B3). One reading during a visit tells you the conditions at that moment.

Infrared thermometers and thermal imaging cameras show surface temperature patterns, which can make a thermal bridge visible. This course could not open a primary source on their use or limits for damp investigation, so it does not set out a method. If you use them, record the date, time, indoor and outdoor temperatures and the settings, and describe the images as showing relative surface temperatures, not moisture.

The damp and mould guidance lists "consistently high relative humidity, as assessed using a moisture meter or environmental monitors" among internal checks for landlords ("Internal and external checks"). "Consistently" is the key word: it implies monitoring over time, not a reading on the day. The same guidance warns that "Smart sensors should not be misused as tools to blame tenants" ("Reducing the risk of damp and mould developing").

## Section 6: Health Context and Landlord Duties

### Why damp and mould matter for health

The government's guidance states: "Damp and mould primarily affect the airways and lungs, but they can also affect the eyes and skin." It says "Everyone is vulnerable to the health impacts of damp and mould" ("Health effects of damp and mould"). It names people at increased risk, including:
- "people with a pre-existing health condition (for example allergies, asthma, COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), cystic fibrosis, other lung diseases and cardiovascular disease)";
- "people of all ages who have a weakened immune system";
- "people living with a mental health condition";
- "pregnant women, their unborn babies and women who have recently given birth";
- children and young people, "whose organs are still developing";
- "older people";
- "people who are bedbound, housebound or have mobility problems" ("People at increased risk from damp and mould").

This matters to your record because PAS 2035:2023 clause 7.3.2 k) requires "an appraisal of occupancy, including the number of occupants and any special considerations". Where a household includes people in those groups, and there is mould, record both facts in the same place in your report, factually, with consent where personal information is involved.

### Landlord duties, briefly

The legal duties fall on landlords, not on assessors. In outline, from the guidance and the legislation opened for this course:
- The Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018 requires that rented properties "are free of hazards, including damp and mould", where the hazard is serious enough that "the dwelling is not reasonably suitable for occupation in that condition" (guidance, "Legal standards on damp and mould in rented homes").
- The HHSRS is "a risk-based evaluation tool" that covers "mould and all types of dampness" (same section).
- In England, the Hazards in Social Housing (Prescribed Requirements) (England) Regulations 2025, SI 2025/1042, "apply to lessors of social homes in England" (regulation 1(4)) and came into force on 27 October 2025 (regulation 1(2)). The significant hazard category is limited to cases where "the risk of harm is associated with exposure to damp, mould or fungal growth" (regulation 3(2)(a)(i)). This is the government's Phase 1 of Awaab's Law.

MER-ALL-017 covers Awaab's Law in depth, including the timescales, the Phase 2 draft, private renting and the Welsh position under the Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016. It is not repeated here. The essential point from that course: the Awaab's Law sources place no duty on assessors, but your written report can be the moment a social landlord "becomes aware" of a potential hazard. Prompt, dated, factual reporting is good practice.

### Mould removal is not your advice to give

The guidance says "most tenants could reasonably be expected to remove condensation and very small amounts of mould" with a suitable cleaner, but "larger areas of mould should only be addressed by qualified professionals" ("Identifying and addressing damp and mould in your property"). It prefers mould removal products to bleach. If a householder asks you what to do, you can point them to their landlord, or, if they own the home, to get professional advice. You should not advise on cleaning extensive mould.

## Section 7: Recording, Photographing and Referring

### What an assessor should say

The safe vocabulary is simple. You recognise and record. You describe what is "possible" or "likely". You advise the client to get professional advice. That is consistent with the damp and mould guidance's own position that assessment "should be carried out by appropriately qualified contractors" and that misdiagnosis is a known risk.

Write findings that someone who has never seen the house could act on:
- **Location:** room, wall, orientation, height, external or internal wall.
- **Extent:** approximate area or length, and whether it is spotting, a band or a patch.
- **Pattern:** at a junction, across a field of wall, below a wet room, at low level.
- **Context:** heating on or off, room temperature and RH if measured, extract fans present and working, trickle vents, furniture, laundry, outside defects seen.
- **Construction:** wall type, any insulation, render, ground levels.
- **Likely category, if you state one:** "pattern consistent with possible condensation at a cold junction" is acceptable. "Rising damp" as a conclusion is not.

Words to avoid in findings: "confirmed", "definitely", "caused by", "rising damp" as a finding, "tenant lifestyle", and any instruction to treat, inject, tank, strip or open up anything.

### Photographs

PAS 2035:2023 clause 7.4.1 says the whole-dwelling assessment, "including the ventilation assessment, shall be recorded and reported to the Retrofit Designer". MER-ALL-017 notes that the same clause asks for photographs of identified defects. Good practice for moisture:
- A wide shot showing the room and where on the wall the problem sits.
- A close-up showing the pattern.
- A shot showing what is outside that wall, if visible: gutter, downpipe, render, sill.
- A shot of extract fans and trickle vents, open or closed.
- Date and time, and a scale reference where useful.

Do not move heavy furniture, lift floor coverings, or remove panels to get a photograph. Note that an area was not inspected and why.

### Never tell a householder to open up walls

Do not tell a householder or landlord to remove plaster, drill the wall, inject a damp proof course, apply tanking, strip linings, or lift floors. Those decisions belong to a qualified professional who has investigated the cause. The guidance's statement that rising damp "is often misdiagnosed" is a direct warning about the cost of getting this wrong. If something needs opening up to be understood, your recommendation is: get professional advice from an appropriately qualified surveyor.

The guidance names some of the qualifications landlords might look for in a damp surveyor: "CSRT - Certificated Surveyor in Remedial Treatment", "CRDS - Certificate in Remedial Damp Surveying" and "CSDTB/CSDB (Chartered Surveyor in Timber and Dampness in Buildings)" ("Types of professional advice"), among others. Naming what the guidance lists is fine. Recommending a particular firm or product is not.

### PAS 2035 touchpoints

For Retrofit Assessors and Coordinators working under PAS 2035:2023, the moisture findings feed formal requirements:
- **Condition.** The assessment shall include "an appraisal of the dwelling's heritage, architectural features, structure, construction and condition" (clause 7.3.2 b)).
- **Defects, condensation and mould.** It shall identify "the location and severity of any existing construction defects or structural defects or leaks" and "any condensation and/or mould growth in the dwelling" (clause 7.3.2 d)).
- **Ventilation.** It shall include "an inspection and review of the existing ventilation following the checklist provided in Table C.1" (clause 7.3.2 f)). MER-ALL-017 notes that the published text it reviewed contains no Table C.1.
- **Services.** "confirmation that the systems are working correctly (or otherwise)" (clause 7.3.2 j)).
- **Occupancy.** "an appraisal of occupancy, including the number of occupants and any special considerations" (clause 7.3.2 k)).
- **Defect categories.** Defects are reported in two categories: "defects that need to be repaired before any retrofit work can proceed" and "defects that are recommended but not an essential prerequisite to retrofit" (clause 7.4.2).
- **Design response.** Where "there is evidence of condensation and/or mould growth in the dwelling in the retrofit assessment", the Retrofit Designer must confirm whether ventilation is adequate (clause 8.2.13).

Notice that clause 7.3.2 d) asks for location and severity. It does not ask the assessor to establish the cause. Clause 7.4.2's categories are where your judgement matters: active penetrating damp from a broken gutter is likely to be something that needs repair before wall insulation proceeds; whether it is, in a given case, is for the designer and coordinator. Record clearly enough for them to decide.

### Energy assessments outside PAS 2035

On an EPC visit, moisture is not part of the energy rating. MER-ALL-017 covers what an EPC does and does not establish. But if you see serious mould, a leak near electrics, or anything suggesting a structural problem, you can and should tell the client in writing that you noticed it and recommend professional advice. Keep it factual, keep it brief, and keep a dated copy.

### Referral triggers

These are situations where you stop describing and recommend professional advice, in writing. They are this course's recommended practice, not legal requirements.

- **Gas.** Any smell of gas, a gas appliance you are concerned about, or signs of combustion problems: follow your organisation's gas safety procedure and the emergency arrangements in force. Do not investigate.
- **Electrical.** Water near or tracking through electrical fittings, sockets or consumer units: recommend urgent advice from a qualified electrician; warn the occupant not to use affected fittings.
- **Structural.** Bulging walls, significant cracking, sagging ceilings, rot in structural timbers, or a ceiling that is wet and heavy: recommend professional advice from a structural engineer or building surveyor.
- **Active leak.** Running or dripping water: tell the occupant and, if rented, the landlord, promptly.
- **Possible rising damp, or a low-level band you cannot account for.** Recommend a specialist damp survey; the guidance says chemical testing is the most appropriate way of confirming rising damp.
- **Extensive mould.** The guidance says larger areas "should only be addressed by qualified professionals".
- **Mould in a home with vulnerable occupants.** Record both facts; for a social home in England, report promptly in writing to the landlord (MER-ALL-017).

### Grant-funded insulation: the TrustMark route

Some of the moisture you see will be in homes that have had insulation fitted under government schemes. If a householder tells you they had external wall insulation installed under ECO4 or the Great British Insulation Scheme (GBIS), there is a free route they should know about.

TrustMark's leaflet says that for homes with "external wall insulation fitted under one of the Government's current Energy Company Obligation (ECO) schemes", TrustMark "will arrange a free audit of your home". At the audit "a qualified professional from TrustMark will inspect the installation and explain the findings to you", and "An audit will focus on safety and technical standards". If issues are found, "TrustMark will arrange for these issues to be put right at no cost to you". The leaflet gives the contact as "0333 555 1234 (Option 1)" and onsitebookings@trustmark.org.uk. TrustMark's web page for the offer is titled "Free Find & Fix Audit Offer" and says: "If you have had work done under ECO4 or GBIS, see the next steps to bring the work up to the required standards" (page updated 23 October 2026).

What the sources opened for this course do and do not say:
- The free audit is described for external wall insulation under ECO4 or GBIS. The leaflet and page do not mention internal or cavity wall insulation.
- For other complaints about TrustMark-registered businesses, TrustMark's general process has three stages, in order: raise it with the business; escalate to the business's Scheme Provider; then contact the Dispute Resolution Ombudsman, generally after a final response or 12 weeks, and within 12 months of the first complaint (TrustMark, "Complaints process"). That page does not say whether those stages are free.
- TrustMark's own role page says "Our role is not to investigate specific consumer complaints", and that consumers "are not entitled to compensation specifically under the TrustMark Scheme" ("TrustMark roles and responsibilities").

Your role is to signpost, not to judge the installation. "The householder reports external wall insulation installed under ECO4; mould observed at window reveals on insulated elevations; householder advised of TrustMark's free audit offer for ECO4 and GBIS external wall insulation" is a complete, safe record.

### A worked record

A mid-terrace solid-walled house, ground floor rear room, January visit. The record might read:

"Rear reception, north-facing external wall. Black mould spotting approx. 1.2 m long along skirting line, extending approx. 300 mm up the wall, worst in the external corner and behind a sofa positioned against the wall. Similar spotting in the top corner at ceiling level, same wall. Window reveals and head show mould bands both sides. Water on the inside of the glass at time of visit. Room 17°C, 68% RH at 10:30 (single spot reading). No trickle vents. Kitchen extract present, did not run when switched on. Outside: rear gutter appears intact; no render cracks seen from ground level; ground level below internal floor level. Pattern consistent with possible condensation at cold junctions. Low-level band not seen on internal walls. Recommend professional advice on ventilation and on the cold junctions before any wall insulation is designed. Occupants include an infant (recorded with consent)."

Notice what it does not say. It does not say "condensation caused by drying clothes". It does not say "no rising damp". It does not give a meter reading as a moisture content. It describes, it locates, it gives context, it uses "possible", and it refers.

## Key Takeaways

The points to carry away:
- The government's guidance names four kinds of damp: condensation, penetrating, rising and traumatic. Condensation is "the most common form of damp"; rising damp "is often misdiagnosed".
- In England, 4% of dwellings (1 million) had a damp problem in 2022 on the English Housing Survey's HHSRS-based definition: 9% of private rented, 5% of social rented and 2% of owner-occupied homes.
- Mould needs high surface humidity, not liquid water. A cold surface raises the humidity of the air touching it. At 20°C and 60% RH, a 15°C surface sits at about 82% RH.
- Approved Document F expresses its mould criterion as surface water activity below 0.75 averaged over a month, 0.85 over a week and 0.95 over a day.
- PAS 2035:2023 clause 8.2.16 requires any detail that is not a published good-practice detail to be shown, by calculation to IP1/06, to have an fRsi not less than 0.75. Approved Document C (England, 2013) frames surface condensation as a maximum U-value of 0.7 W/m²K at any point plus junction details; it does not state 0.75 in the text read.
- Thermal bridges sit at reveals, lintels, floor perimeters, intermediate floors, eaves, gables and party walls. Discontinuous insulation can make a junction the coldest surface in the room, and airtightness work can raise room humidity.
- Moisture meters on masonry are set up for timber and strongly affected by salts. Readings on different materials cannot be compared. Do not report a meter reading on masonry as a moisture content or as evidence of rising damp.
- Recognise and record; say "possible" or "likely"; recommend professional advice. Never tell a householder to open up walls. For external wall insulation under ECO4 or GBIS, signpost TrustMark's free audit.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** A room is at 20°C and 60% RH. Using the WMO saturation vapour pressure formula, roughly what is the relative humidity of the air at a wall surface at 15°C?

A) 60%
B) 68%
C) 82%
D) 100%, with visible condensation

**Correct answer: C.** The cooler surface raises the relative humidity of the air touching it; at 15°C it is about 82%, while 100% is reached at about 12°C (Section 2).

---

**Q2.** According to PAS 2035:2023 clause 8.2.16, what must be shown for a construction detail that is not taken from published good-practice guidance?

A) That its U-value does not exceed 0.7 W/m²K at any point
B) That its temperature factor (fRsi), calculated in accordance with IP1/06, is not less than 0.75
C) That it has been signed off by building control
D) That its psi value is below the RdSAP default

**Correct answer: B.** Clause 8.2.16 requires fRsi not less than 0.75 by calculation to IP1/06; the 0.7 W/m²K figure is Approved Document C's floor and wall provision (Sections 2 and 3).

---

**Q3.** A ceiling in the room below a first-floor bathroom has a stained patch with a defined edge that has grown since last month. Which is the most likely category to record?

A) Condensation
B) Rising damp
C) Possible leak (traumatic damp), to be referred
D) Penetrating damp through the roof

**Correct answer: C.** A defined patch below a wet room points to a possible leak from waste or supply pipes, which the guidance calls traumatic damp; the assessor records it and refers (Sections 1 and 4).

---

**Q4.** What did Historic England Research Report 2/2022 find about handheld moisture meters on masonry?

A) Resistance meters give accurate moisture contents on all masonry
B) Salinity had a strong effect on all methods, and readings from different materials should not be compared directly
C) Salts have no effect on resistance meter readings
D) Meter readings can confirm rising damp without other tests

**Correct answer: B.** The report found "a strong effect of salinity" on all methods and concluded "it is not advisable to directly compare meter readings from different building materials" (Section 5).

---

**Q5.** During a retrofit assessment, a householder says the external wall insulation on their home was installed under ECO4, and you see mould at the window reveals. What is the appropriate response?

A) Tell the householder the installation is defective and should be stripped
B) Record the observation factually, recommend professional advice, and tell the householder about TrustMark's free audit for ECO4 and GBIS external wall insulation
C) Take moisture meter readings and report the moisture content of the wall
D) Say nothing, because moisture is outside the scope of the assessment

**Correct answer: B.** The assessor recognises and records, does not tell anyone to open up walls, and signposts the free TrustMark audit described for external wall insulation under ECO4 or GBIS; PAS 2035 clause 7.3.2 d) requires condensation and mould to be recorded (Section 7).
