## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- Recognise intermittent extract, continuous mechanical extract, MVHR, single-room heat recovery, positive input ventilation (PIV) and passive stack on site, and name their main parts.
- Describe the faults that government-funded field studies found in these systems, and the limits of those studies.
- Tell signs that may point to under-ventilation from signs that point elsewhere, without attributing damp or mould to occupants.
- Record ventilation to the level PAS 2035:2023 clause 7.3.2 f) asks for, so the Retrofit Designer can decide what is needed.
- State what Approved Document F and RdSAP 10 say about PIV, and what no source opened for this course shows about it.
- Recognise when fans and an open-flued combustion appliance share a home, and refer under your organisation's gas safety procedure.

## Introduction

Part 1 of the Ventilation Series covered how homes are ventilated and what Approved Document F (ADF) expects. This part goes on site: what each system looks like on the ceiling, in the loft and on the window frame, how it tends to fail, and what you write down.

Three facts frame the course. ADF lists only three example systems, and some you will meet are not among them. Field studies found faults in a large share of new homes; as PAS 2035:2023 puts it, "the existence of a ventilation system is not proof" of adequate ventilation (Annex C, C.1). And your role is to recognise and record. Measuring flow, commissioning and deciding on upgrades belong to others (Part 3).

This course does not repeat other Meridian courses. **MER-RA-008** covers the clause 7.3.2 f) items, Table 3.1, Diagram 3.1, clause 8.2.13 and Annex C. **MER-DEA-003** covers every RdSAP ventilation entry. **MER-ALL-018** covers moisture physics, the condensation "twins" and instruments. Where those topics touch this one, the course points to them.

### Where this course applies

This course is written for England and Wales.
- **England:** ADF Volume 1, 2021 edition, "takes effect on 15 June 2022" and is the edition in use. The 2026 edition takes effect on 24 March 2027 for most work (24 September 2027 for higher-risk building work). This course cites the 2021 edition unless it says otherwise. The 2026 edition renumbers several Section 1 paragraphs (for example, 1.20 becomes 1.16 and the note to 1.34 moves to 1.30); Part 3 covers the changes.
- **Wales:** ADF Volume 1, 2022 edition, took effect on 23 November 2022. A 2026 Welsh edition takes effect on 4 March 2027 (Welsh Government web page); it was not opened. The 2022 Welsh text was checked for the paragraphs this course relies on most. They carry the same numbers and requirements as England, except where the course says otherwise.
- **PAS 2035:2023** and **RdSAP 10** apply in both nations.
- **The field studies** were carried out in England (MHCLG/AECOM, seven developments; NHBC Foundation NF52, one development) and studied new homes, not retrofitted ones. The survey in the Wider view panel covered England, Wales and Scotland.

### How the sources were handled

Every figure and quotation comes from a document opened for this course. Where a source could not be opened, the course says so and does not fill the gap. The gaps that matter:

- **Manufacturers' instructions** vary by product. No product figures are given, and no costs.

Some numbers are calculated, not quoted. Each one shows its inputs.

## Section 1: Intermittent Extract Fans

**In short:** Intermittent fans run only when switched on or triggered. ADF expects extract in kitchens, utility rooms, bathrooms and WCs at set minimum rates, mounted high and within 400 mm of the ceiling. In most homes in the field study, fans did not reach those rates, many isolators were off and some greasy shutters did not open. Record each fan's room, type, control and whether it runs, and do not adjust settings.

### What ADF expects

ADF 2021 says extract to the outside "should be provided" in kitchens, utility rooms, bathrooms and sanitary accommodation (1.17). Minimum intermittent rates are in Table 1.1:

| Room | Minimum intermittent extract rate |
|---|---|
| Kitchen, cooker hood extracting to outside | 30 l/s |
| Kitchen, no hood, or hood not extracting to outside | 60 l/s |
| Utility room | 30 l/s |
| Bathroom | 15 l/s |
| WC (sanitary accommodation) | 6 l/s, or purge ventilation instead |

The note to Diagram 1.2 matters on site: "A recirculating cooker hood on its own does not provide" ventilation that complies with Part F. Record a hood with no duct to outside as recirculating.

Points you can check by eye:
- **Position:** fans and terminals (not cooker hoods) "as high as is practicable", and no more than 400 mm below the ceiling (1.20).
- **Controls:** automatic controls should have a manual override (1.50). Humidity controls "should not be used for sanitary accommodation" (1.36).
- **Run-on:** in a room with no openable window, the fan should run "for at least 15 minutes after the room is vacated" (1.51). Installers should check run-on timers are set to at least 15 minutes (4.6).

Wales 2022 reads the same for Table 1.1 and paragraphs 1.20, 1.36, 1.51 and 4.6(c).

### Ducts and noise

The duct run matters as much as the fan. ADF 2021 says "Rigid ducts should be used wherever possible" (1.76). Flexible duct should be used only for final connections, no more than 1.5 m long, pulled taut so the full diameter is kept (1.77, 1.78). Ducts should be short with few bends (1.79), and each terminal should have at least 90% of its duct's free area (1.80). Wales 2022 has the same at 1.76, 1.77 and 1.80.

ADF asks for resilient mountings where structure-borne noise could be a problem (1.5), though there is "no requirement to undertake noise testing" (note to 1.7).

### How they fail: the field evidence

The best field evidence is a government-funded study of 80 new homes on seven developments in England, visited from November 2015 to February 2016 (AECOM for MHCLG, *Ventilation and Indoor Air Quality in New Homes*, September 2019). Fifty-five had natural ventilation with intermittent fans.
- "Only 2 of the 55 homes visited met the guidance in ADF" for both trickle vents and extract rates (p. 2).
- Only 9 of the 55 met the minimum extract fan flow rates (p. 2): 16% (calculated: 9 ÷ 55).
- In the homes the report labels "System 1", about 40% of bathroom and WC fans had their isolators off (p. 33).
- Some kitchen fans with backdraught shutters were greasy enough that the shutters did not open on their own (p. 19).
- Some fan models had dip switches for their settings; none had been correctly set to "ducted mode" where the fan was ceiling mounted (p. 19).
- The report linked some bathroom under-performance to the resistance of ducting and external terminals (p. 19).
- Residents turned fans off because of noise (p. 30).

These were new homes, not retrofits. Still, a fan that exists is not a fan that works.

### Refurbished kitchens and bathrooms

When a kitchen or bathroom is refurbished, ADF 3.30 says existing fans, including hoods that extract to outside, "should be retained or replaced", with checks that retained devices work. If the room had none, none need be added unless the work makes the building less compliant (3.31). Replacing a fan on the existing cabling need not be notified to building control (3.32).

### What you record

PAS 2035:2023 clause 7.3.2 f) 1) asks you to "record the position and type of any fans", with make and model "where possible", and the reasons if not. Clause 7.3.2 j) asks for confirmation that services "are working correctly (or otherwise)". NOTE 5 says fans should be checked to BSRIA guidance and inadequate air movement reported.

For each fan, record: room and position; type (wall, ceiling with duct, inline in the loft, cooker hood extracting or recirculating); make and model or why not; control and any manual override; whether it runs when the householder switches it on, and runs on; isolator position as found; any visible duct (rigid or flexible, length, kinks); visible grease or a shutter that stays closed.

A run check is not a flow measurement. ADF flow measurement needs a calibrated device with a proprietary hood (4.10), covered in Part 3. Where a fan runs but you cannot say whether it meets the rate, write "running; flow not measured". Do not change a fan's settings, isolator or cover.

Callouts: "Table 1.1: bathroom 15 l/s", "1.20: max 400 mm below ceiling", "1.77: flexible max 1.5 m". Caption: "A running fan is not a measured fan."

## Section 2: Continuous Mechanical Extract

**In short:** Continuous extract runs all the time at a low rate and boosts when needed, from one central unit with ducts or from separate room fans. ADF expects it to meet the whole-dwelling rate, to run without anyone switching it on, and to have trickle vents in habitable rooms but none in wet rooms. In the field study, one of 25 such homes met ADF and over half the fans were not running. Record type, controls and whether each fan runs.

### What it looks like

ADF 2021 describes continuous extract as a central extract system, individual room fans, or both (1.60). PAS 2035 and RdSAP call these centralised and decentralised.
- **Centralised:** one fan unit, for example in a loft or cupboard, with ducts to valves in each wet room.
- **Decentralised:** a separate fan in each wet room running continuously at low speed. It can look like an intermittent fan. Clues: it runs with the light off and has a boost control or sensor rather than an on/off switch.

ADF 2021 1.46 NOTE says "An intermittent extract fan may be replaced" with a decentralised continuous fan, so a new-looking bathroom fan may be either type. Check the label.

### What ADF expects

- **Rates.** Total continuous extract should be at least the whole-dwelling rate in Table 1.3 (1.62). Paragraph 1.24 says that rate should meet both 0.3 l/s per m² of floor area and the Table 1.3 figure for the number of bedrooms, so the higher governs. Each wet room should reach the Table 1.2 high rate (1.63): kitchen 13 l/s, utility room 8, bathroom 8, WC 6.
- **Controls.** Continuous fans "should be set up to operate without occupant intervention" (1.35). For upgrades under PAS 2035 Annex C, the rate "shall not be controlled solely by light switches or manual switches" (C.3.6).
- **Background ventilators.** None in wet rooms; at least 4000 mm² in each habitable room; at least as many ventilators as bedrooms plus two (1.64). Wales 2022 1.64 is the same.

Note one inconsistency: ADF's checklist for existing homes (Table D1) asks for 5000 mm² per habitable room under continuous extract, while 1.64 says 4000 mm², in both the 2021 and 2026 England editions. Record the areas you find; the designer applies the standard.

### Worked numbers

Calculated from ADF figures:
- **Whole-dwelling rate, three bedrooms, 90 m²:** Table 1.3 gives 31 l/s; 0.3 × 90 = 27 l/s; the higher governs, 31 l/s. At 120 m², 0.3 × 120 = 36 l/s governs.
- **Same kitchen, two systems:** an intermittent fan with no hood should reach 60 l/s (216 m³ an hour) while it runs. A continuous system's kitchen high rate is 13 l/s (46.8 m³ an hour), with a lower rate running all day. The rates come from different tables, so a small continuous fan is not "under-sized" for being smaller.
- **Background ventilators, one three-bedroom, two-storey house with living room, dining room and kitchen:** natural ventilation (Table 1.7), 8000 mm² in each of five habitable rooms and the kitchen plus 4000 mm² in the bathroom = 52,000 mm²; continuous extract (1.64), 4000 mm² in each of five habitable rooms = 20,000 mm², none in kitchen or bathroom; MVHR (1.72), none.

### How it fails: the field evidence

The MHCLG/AECOM study included 25 homes with decentralised continuous extract:
- "Only one of the 25 homes visited met the guidance" for both extract and trickle provision (p. 2).
- The report found "over 50% of System 3 continuous fans were not operational" (pp. 30, 33), "System 3" being its label for these homes.
- On two of the three developments with continuous extract, trickle ventilators were fitted in the same rooms as the extract fans (p. 2).

The report does not say why each fan was off. Do not guess either: record "not running at visit" and anything the householder tells you, as reported.

### What you record

As in Section 1, plus: centralised or decentralised and where any central unit is; whether each fan or valve is running; boost control or sensor; background ventilators by room, noting any in wet rooms. ADF 4.13 says sufficient information "must be given" to the owner, which should include design flow rates and maintenance requirements. If you are shown it, note where it is kept. RdSAP entry is in MER-DEA-003 Sections 3 and 4.

**Visual:** Worked-numbers chart. Background ventilator area for one three-bedroom house: natural 52,000 mm², continuous extract 20,000 mm², MVHR 0. Kitchen: intermittent 60 l/s against continuous high rate 13 l/s. Footer: "1 of 25 continuous extract homes met ADF."

## Section 3: MVHR and Single-Room Heat Recovery

**In short:** MVHR supplies air to living rooms and bedrooms and extracts from wet rooms through one unit with a heat exchanger. ADF expects no trickle vents with it. A field study of ten homes found fans wired to run on boost, very dirty filters, blocked fly screens, uncomfortably cold supply air and noise. Record the unit's location, the valves in each room, visible duct insulation and whether it runs. Single-room units serve one room only.

### What it looks like

You may find a box-shaped unit in a loft, cupboard or utility room with four duct connections: intake and exhaust to outside, supply and extract to the house. Round valves sit on ceilings or high on walls: supply in habitable rooms, extract in wet rooms.

ADF 2021 expects a supply to each habitable room (1.67), terminals placed to avoid draughts (1.68), the Table 1.2 high rate from each wet room (1.70), and a design that avoids moist wet-room air "recirculating to the habitable rooms" (1.71). Background ventilators "should not be installed with" MVHR (1.72), and Table D1 asks whether they have been "removed or sealed shut". Wales 2022 has the same at 1.70 to 1.72.

PAS 2035:2023 C.3.3 NOTE 4 adds points you can see. MVHR "was primarily developed for new dwellings" and needs careful design in existing homes, "especially small ones". Fan boxes, heat exchangers and ductwork "should not be installed in unheated spaces", and cold-side ducts should be insulated and vapour sealed.

For new work from 24 March 2027, the 2026 England edition adds filter access without tools (1.75) and duct insulation levels (1.83); Part 3 covers them.

### How it fails: the field evidence

The NHBC Foundation studied MVHR in ten new zero-carbon homes on one development (A. Dengel and M. Swainson, BRE, *Assessment of MVHR systems and air quality in zero carbon homes*, NF52, August 2013):
- "All of the MVHR fan units inspected had been wired" to run in boost mode constantly (p. 19).
- "The exhaust air filters were found to be very dirty", and some filters were fitted the wrong way round (p. 19).
- Fly screens were "nearly totally blocked" in some homes. In one, supply flow fell "from 28 l/s to less than 5 l/s" (pp. 24, 25), a fall of more than four-fifths (calculated: (28 − 5) ÷ 28 = 82%).
- Commissioning engineers had closed valves very tightly to limit cold supply air "dumping" into rooms, which the report calls "of considerable detriment to the efficiency" (p. 20).
- Duct heat losses were "very significant", and in cold weather bedroom supply air became "uncomfortably cold" (pp. 28, 30).
- "53% (23 of 43) of the rooms measured" failed the CIBSE Guide A noise limits before remedial work; 3 of 38 failed after (p. 37).
- Occupants "could not ascertain if they were actually running correctly" (p. 37). Poor comfort "may lead to an occupant turning the MVHR system off" (p. 29).

Ten homes on one development is a small sample, but every fault listed can be seen or heard without instruments.

### Single-room heat recovery ventilators

A single-room heat recovery unit is a through-wall fan with a small heat exchanger. ADF allows one in a new wet room, at the Table 1.2 high rate with 50% of that as its continuous rate, and no background ventilator in that room (3.25, 3.26; Wales 2022 3.26 the same). PAS 2035 clause 7.3.2 f) 1) lists them as a type to record, and C.3.7 says several single-room units "shall not be used as a whole-dwelling system" under Annex C. Record each one in its room. Do not record several as whole-house MVHR.

### What you record

Unit location (inside or outside the heated envelope); make and model or why not; valves room by room, supply or extract, noting any visibly closed or taped over; any background ventilators and whether open, closed or sealed; visible filter door and any date label (do not open the unit); visible ducts in the loft, insulated or not; outside terminals as seen from the ground; whether it runs and the control position (normal, boost, off); noise the householder reports, as reported. RdSAP needs the duct insulation level where ducts are inside the heated envelope (convention 7.03d; see MER-DEA-003 Section 4).

**Visual:** Cutaway house map. Loft: MVHR unit, uninsulated supply duct, blocked intake fly screen ("28 l/s to under 5 l/s"). Bedroom supply valve screwed shut; control on boost; living-room trickle vent circled ("1.72"). Inset: single-room unit, "one room only".

## Section 4: PIV and Passive Stack

**In short:** PIV pushes air into the home from a fan unit, either from the loft through a ceiling diffuser or from a unit drawing outside air. ADF does not list it among its example systems, so it is treated case by case. PAS 2035 asks you to record it with make and model. Passive stack has no fan: ducts rise from wet rooms to the roof. Record what you find, keep both separate from extract, and leave suitability to the designer.

### PIV: what the primary sources say

- **Approved Document F.** Table 1.6 lists three example systems: natural ventilation (less airtight homes), continuous mechanical extract and MVHR. PIV is not one of them. "Positive input" does not appear in the 2021 or 2026 England editions, and the parts of the Welsh 2022 edition read for this course do not mention it. ADF 1.11 says other systems "may be acceptable if they can be shown to meet an equal level of performance". Outside Table 1.6, "expert advice should be sought". ADF sets no airtightness limit or band for PIV.
- **PAS 2035:2023.** Clause 7.3.2 f) 1) names "positive input ventilation (PIV)" among the fan types to record. Annex C accepts other systems "if they can be shown to meet an equal level of performance" and ventilation effectiveness, "and extract moisture at source"; expert advice "shall be sought" (C.2.2 c), C.3.3 c)).
- **RdSAP 10.** The specification (v4.0, section 4.2) lists "Positive input – from loft (treated as natural)" and "Positive input – from outside". Convention 7.03a describes mechanical systems as "input-only, extract-only or balanced", and 7.03e ranks the two PIV types last when two systems are present. MER-DEA-003 Section 3 covers the entry.

What the sources do not give is evidence of how well PIV performs. The BRE work from 2000 could not be opened, and no newer independent primary study was found. So this course makes no claim that PIV lowers humidity, reduces condensation or improves air quality, and none that it does not. Treat any such claim you meet, including in sales material, as unverified.

### PIV on site

You may see a fan unit in the roof space feeding a ceiling diffuser below (RdSAP "from loft"), or a unit on or through an external wall drawing outside air (RdSAP "from outside"). PIV is an input system, so it does not extract from kitchens and bathrooms. Keep recording extract in those rooms separately; ADF 3.30 still expects existing kitchen and bathroom fans to be kept or replaced in a refurbishment.

Record: type and location of unit and diffuser; make and model or why not; whether it runs and the control position (do not open the unit); any roof-space condensation, since PAS 7.3.2 d) asks for the location and severity of any condensation; and any open-flued appliance (Section 7).

Do not record a judgement that PIV is suitable or unsuitable for the home's airtightness. No source opened gives a basis for one. That decision belongs to the Retrofit Designer.

### Passive stack

Passive stack uses ducts with no fan, rising from grilles in wet-room ceilings to terminals at roof level, and moves air by stack effect and wind.
- It is not a Table 1.6 system in the 2021 or 2026 England editions.
- Wales 2022 3.30 says that in a kitchen or bathroom refurbishment, existing "passive stack ventilators should be retained or replaced". England 2021 3.30 does not mention them.
- RdSAP convention 7.03a: where vents (not air bricks) are present and mechanical ventilation "cannot be confirmed", enter the number of vents as passive stack.

A wet-room ceiling grille may be passive stack, centralised extract or MVHR. Trace the duct in the loft where safe, and listen for a fan. If you cannot confirm it, record "ceiling vent, system not confirmed".

Columns: "Could be"; "What tells them apart" (four-connection unit: MVHR; roof terminal, no fan: passive stack; loft fan with one duct: loft-fed PIV; no on/off switch: likely decentralised extract; heat exchanger label: single-room unit); "Record as". Footer: "If you cannot confirm it, say so."

## Section 5: Trickle Vents, Undercuts and Purge

**In short:** Trickle vents supply air with natural ventilation or continuous extract, but not with MVHR. ADF says they are intended to be left open; the damp and mould guidance says tenants should be able to control them. In the field study, only 29% were open on average. Record each vent's room, marked area, and whether it was open or closed as you found it. Check door undercuts, and window openings for purge.

### What ADF expects

ADF calls trickle vents "background ventilators" and sizes them by equivalent area (1.15), which should be marked where it is easy to see from inside (1.16). For natural ventilation, Table 1.7 sets 8000 mm² for habitable rooms and kitchens in homes with more than one floor (10,000 mm² single-storey), 4000 mm² for bathrooms, and no minimum for utility rooms and WCs. Wales 2022 Table 1.7 is the same.

Also check:
- Rooms with external walls should have them (1.52). A night-latch position does not count (note to 1.52).
- Similar areas on each façade (1.53); noise-attenuating vents near sustained loud noise (1.54).
- At least 500 mm from a fan in the same room (1.55).
- At least five in habitable rooms and kitchens, four in a one-bedroom home (1.57).
- At least 1700 mm above the floor, "to reduce cold draughts", but within reach (1.34).

### Open or closed: two texts side by side

- ADF 2021: background ventilators "are intended to normally be left open" (note to 1.34), and the owner's information should say they "should be left open" (4.16(b)). The 2026 England edition drops "normally" (note to 1.30). Wales 2022 has the 2021 wording.
- Damp and mould guidance (MHCLG, DHSC and UKHSA, updated 1 April 2026): "All replacement windows are required to have trickle vents", and they should be "controllable by the tenant, rather than permanently open".

These do not conflict: ADF also expects ventilation to be controllable (1.33). The design assumes the vent is open in normal use, and the occupant can close it. You record what you find.

The field evidence shows why "as found" matters. In the MHCLG/AECOM study, "only 29% of trickle ventilators were open on average" (p. 25). Residents closed them against road noise, and they were hidden by closed curtains at night (p. 3).

### Replacement windows

Where old windows had background ventilators, ADF 3.14 says new ones should too, no smaller and controllable. Where they had none, 3.15 says ventilation should be "no worse than it was before the work", for example 8000 mm² in habitable rooms and kitchens and 4000 mm² in bathrooms. Wales 2022 3.15 gives the same areas and adds that none should be installed with continuous supply and extract. Replacing more than 30% of windows is a major measure in Table 3.1; MER-RA-008 Section 3 covers the category.

### Undercuts and purge

ADF 1.25 asks for a free area equal to "a 10mm undercut in a 760mm wide door": 10 mm above a fitted floor finish, 20 mm above an unfinished floor. That is 7,600 mm² (calculated: 10 × 760). Wales 2022 1.25 is the same. New flooring can close the gap, so record doors with none.

Each habitable room should have purge ventilation able to give at least four air changes an hour (1.26, 1.27), through openings such as windows or external doors, or by mechanical extract (1.28). Table 1.4 sets hinged or pivot window openings at 1/10 of floor area for 15° to 30° and 1/20 for 30° or more; below 15° "are not suitable" (1.31). Wales 2022 is the same. Worked example (calculated): a 12 m² bedroom needs 12 ÷ 20 = 0.6 m² at 30° or more, or 12 ÷ 10 = 1.2 m² if restrictors hold it between 15° and 30°.

### What you record

PAS 2035 clause 7.3.2 f) 2) asks you to "record the size and location of all background ventilators where present". For each: room and façade; marked equivalent area or "not marked"; open or closed as found; damage, paint-over or covering. For each habitable room, the purge opening (type, rough size, angle, restrictor). For each internal door, undercut or none.

**Visual:** Traffic light. Green: marked trickle vent, open; door undercut; casement opening wide. Amber: unmarked vent closed behind curtains; new carpet, no door gap; window restrictor: "record as found; designer". Red: no vent, mould at reveal and ceiling corner: "record location and severity; designer check (PAS 8.2.13 b))".

## Section 6: Recognising Possible Under-Ventilation

**In short:** Condensation and mould can point to under-ventilation, but also to leaks, rising or penetrating damp and cold spots. Government guidance lists switched-off or blocked fans and consistently high humidity among root causes, and calls blaming "lifestyle choices" "totally unreasonable". A government survey found airing and drying habits had no apparent effect on damp. Record what you see, where and how severe, and what the ventilation is doing. Leave the cause to the designer.

### What to look for

The damp and mould guidance (updated 1 April 2026) lists internal root causes including "consistently high relative humidity, as assessed using a moisture meter or environmental monitors" and "damaged, blocked, absent or switched off mechanical ventilation". It notes that "some energy efficiency interventions may reduce overall ventilation rates". ADF 3.6 agrees that energy efficiency measures "might reduce infiltration" and leave a home under-ventilated. PAS 2035 C.1 says many homes improved for energy efficiency "are not adequately ventilated".

Patterns that may fit under-ventilation:
- Condensation or mould in more than one room.
- Fans absent, isolated or not running (Sections 1 to 3).
- Trickle vents absent, closed or painted shut (Section 5).
- Past energy efficiency work, such as new windows, insulation or a sealed chimney, with no change to ventilation.

PAS 2035 7.3.2 d) NOTE 2 says "A small amount of condensation" around bathroom sinks or showers, or around window edges in extreme weather, "is normal". Record it; do not over-read it.

### The twins

Some signs that look like under-ventilation come from a roof or gutter leak, penetrating or rising damp, a plumbing leak or a cold bridge. MER-ALL-018 Section 4 ("Telling the Twins Apart") covers how to tell these apart. For ventilation the point is simpler: no ventilation system stops water getting into the building, so record any possible water ingress and refer it for repair first.

### Do not attribute it to occupants

The guidance is direct: "it is totally unreasonable to blame damp and mould in the home on 'lifestyle choices'", and "Approaches that attach blame to occupants' behaviour are unjust and counterproductive."

The evidence supports this. BEIS's *Energy Follow Up Survey: thermal comfort, damp and ventilation* (final report, 2021, on the 2017 survey) found 27% of households reported some damp or mould. Ten per cent reported no bathroom ventilation while showering or bathing, 83% dried clothes indoors in winter, and 47% reported never ventilating the room while doing so. Yet there was "no apparent influence" of airing, bathroom ventilation or indoor drying on the prevalence of damp, and main bedrooms "were colder in homes with damp" (p. 7).

That is a survey finding, not proof about any one home, but it is a good reason to record the building, not the household.

Tenants may worry about "running costs", safety, noise or losing heat, the guidance notes. Record any reason given neutrally: "reported: switched off because of noise". Noise is a design and installation matter (ADF 1.5 to 1.7).

### Instruments and where it goes

A thermo-hygrometer reading is a snapshot. No source opened for this course sets a humidity level to treat as a pass or fail on site; MER-ALL-018 Section 5 covers instruments. Record any reading with time, room, weather and whether windows, fans and heating were on.

PAS 2035 clause 8.2.13 b) says that where the assessment finds "evidence of condensation and/or mould growth", the Retrofit Designer shall confirm whether the existing ventilation is adequate. Your record is what the designer uses. ADF Table D1 Note 1 likewise asks for "a visual check for mould or condensation". MER-RA-008 Section 3 covers the trigger. For rented homes, MER-ALL-017 covers what an assessment does and does not establish under Awaab's Law.

**Visual:** Twin-to-rule-out grid. Rows: mould in ceiling corners and behind a wardrobe; tide mark and salts at skirting; damp below a gutter, worse after rain; streaming bedroom windows; stain on a ceiling below the bathroom. ## Section 7: Combustion Appliances, Fans and the Referral Route

**In short:** Fans might cause combustion gases to spill from an open-flued appliance into the room, even where the fan and appliance are in different rooms. ADF says appliances "must operate safely whether or not fans are running". Your job is to recognise and record any open-flued appliance and any fans in the same home, and refer through your organisation's gas safety procedure or to a Gas Safe registered engineer. Never test, adjust or advise on the appliance yourself.

### What ADF says

ADF 2021 0.17: "Ventilation fans might cause combustion gases to spill from open-flued appliances." The gases might fill the room rather than going up the flue, "even if the combustion appliance and fan are in separate rooms". ADF 0.18 says Approved Document J should be followed when installing and testing ventilation appliances, and "Combustion appliances must operate safely whether or not fans are running." The 2026 England edition keeps both at 0.17 and 0.18. In Wales 2022 the same guidance is at 0.14 and 0.15.

ADF also says that where a combustion appliance is installed, automatic ventilation controls must meet Part J (note to 1.37), and that Part J "must be considered" for building work in a kitchen or bathroom with a combustion appliance (note before 3.30). Approved Document J was not opened, so this section covers recognition and referral only.

### What you recognise and record

- Any open-flued appliance (gas, oil or solid fuel boiler, fire or stove taking air from the room, with a flue or chimney): location and fuel.
- Every fan, including extracting cooker hoods, continuous extract, MVHR and PIV, and which rooms they are in relative to the appliance.
- Any proposed measure that adds or upgrades a fan, or makes the home more airtight.
- Chimneys and flues for the RdSAP count (convention 9.01; MER-DEA-003 Section 2).
- What the householder tells you about the appliance, as reported.

### Referral

Where an open-flued appliance shares a home with any extract fan, cooker hood or other powered ventilation, or a measure would add one:
- Follow your organisation's gas safety procedure.
- For gas appliances, refer to a Gas Safe registered engineer; for oil or solid fuel, to a competent engineer for that fuel.
- Record that you referred, to whom and when.
- If the householder reports fumes or you have any immediate concern, follow your organisation's emergency procedure straight away.

Do not run fans to "see what happens", do not light or adjust the appliance, and do not tell the householder it is safe or unsafe.

### Pulling the record together

Across Sections 1 to 7, the record that serves the designer holds: every fan and system by room, type, make and model (or why not), control and whether it ran (PAS 7.3.2 f) 1), j)); every background ventilator by room, marked area and position as found (f) 2)); undercuts, purge openings and anything sealed; condensation and mould by location and severity, and possible water ingress (d)); open-flued appliances and referrals made; past and proposed measures (e)); and householder reports, attributed and neutral.

Write "possible" or "likely" where you cannot confirm. Do not write that a system is compliant, adequate or inadequate: the designer decides under clause 8.2.13.

Branches: open-flued appliance with any fan → "Gas safety procedure / Gas Safe registered engineer or competent engineer for the fuel" (red side box: "Fumes or immediate concern: emergency procedure"); water ingress → "Repair first"; condensation, mould or Category B/C → "Retrofit Designer (8.2.13)". ## Wider View: Ventilation Provision and Use Across Great Britain

*Outside research, credited: C. Van Rooyen and T. Sharpe (2024), "Ventilation provision and use in homes in Great Britain: A national survey", Building and Environment 257, 111528 (University College London and University of Strathclyde; open copy on Strathprints).*

YouGov ran the authors' online questionnaire on 17 June 2022 for adults in England, Wales and Scotland; 1,861 responses were analysed (p. 3). As reported:
- 71% of homes "did not have this minimum ventilation provision": extract fans in both wet rooms plus trickle vents on some or all windows (p. 4).
- 33% reported no bathroom ventilation and 18% no kitchen ventilation system (p. 5).
- Of those with cooker hoods, only 56% said the hood extracted to outside (p. 5).
- Of households with mechanical ventilation, 41% had "never maintained" any part of it, including cleaning inside (p. 5).
- PIV was reported by 2% and MVHR by 3% (p. 5).
- Barriers to using mechanical ventilation included electricity cost (19%) and noise (13%); 45% reported none (Table 4).
- In homes built after 1991, 9% of those with mechanical ventilation and trickle vents reported mould or damp, against 17% without. Before 1991 it was 23% in both groups (p. 7).

Caveats: responses are self-reported and "were not validated" (p. 9); the summer timing may affect recall of winter; the sample is "not strictly representative" (p. 3); no significance test is reported for the mould comparison; and trickle vent counts differ within the paper, so no trickle vent share is quoted.

**What it means for your work:** A householder's description of their ventilation may be incomplete, and homes missing basic extract appear common. Check what is there yourself. A system that has never been maintained may be present but not working, so record whether it runs. The older-homes result is a reminder that mould has more than one cause: record the fabric and heating as carefully as the fans, and leave the cause to the designer.

---

## Key Takeaways

- ADF lists three example systems: natural ventilation (less airtight homes), continuous mechanical extract and MVHR. Other systems "may be acceptable" if shown to perform equally, with expert advice.
- A system that exists is not a system that works. In the MHCLG/AECOM study, 2 of 55 naturally ventilated homes and 1 of 25 continuous extract homes met ADF.
- Faults you can see: isolators off, fans not running, greasy shutters, flexible duct beyond ADF's limits, closed trickle vents, MVHR on constant boost, dirty filters, blocked fly screens.
- Background ventilators go with natural ventilation and continuous extract (not in wet rooms with continuous extract), and not with MVHR.
- PIV is not an ADF example system. PAS 2035 asks you to record it and RdSAP has two PIV methods. No independent performance evidence was opened, so make no claims about it.
- Never attribute damp or mould to "lifestyle". Record the building, the ventilation and possible water ingress, and leave the cause to the designer.
- Where any fan shares a home with an open-flued appliance, refer under your organisation's gas safety procedure or to a Gas Safe registered engineer. Never test or adjust.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** A single bathroom ceiling fan runs with the light off, has no pull-cord and has a boost button. How should you record it?

A) As an intermittent fan that has been left on
B) As likely decentralised continuous extract, with make and model from the label (or why not), running at the visit
C) As MVHR, because it runs all the time
D) As passive stack, because it is in the ceiling

**Correct answer: B.** ADF 1.46 NOTE allows an intermittent fan to be replaced by a decentralised continuous fan, and continuous fans run without occupant intervention (1.35). PAS 2035 7.3.2 f) 1) asks for type, position and make and model. Write "likely" unless the label confirms it (Sections 2 and 4).

---

**Q2.** A home has MVHR, and every living room and bedroom window has an open trickle vent. Which statement matches ADF 2021?

A) This is correct, as MVHR needs trickle vents for supply
B) Background ventilators "should not be installed with" MVHR; record them as found and pass the record to the designer
C) The assessor should tape the trickle vents shut
D) Trickle vents only matter in single-storey homes

**Correct answer: B.** ADF 1.72 says background ventilators should not be installed with MVHR, and Table D1 asks whether they have been "removed or sealed shut". You record; you do not alter (Section 3).

---

**Q3.** A landlord asks whether PIV would suit a three-bedroom semi with condensation. What does the primary evidence opened for this course support?

A) PIV suits homes with an air permeability between 3 and 7 m³/(h·m²)
B) ADF lists PIV as an example system for less airtight homes
C) PIV is not an ADF Table 1.6 system; another system may be acceptable if shown to perform equally, with expert advice; record what exists and leave suitability to the Retrofit Designer
D) PAS 2035 bans PIV

**Correct answer: C.** ADF 1.11 and the note to Table 1.6 cover systems outside its examples. ADF sets no airtightness band for PIV, and PAS 2035 lists PIV as a type to record (Section 4).

---

**Q4.** A tenant says they keep the bathroom fan isolator off because the fan is noisy, and there is mould on the bathroom ceiling. What is the appropriate record?

A) "Mould caused by tenant switching off the fan"
B) "Bathroom fan isolator off at visit; reported by tenant: switched off because of noise. Mould on ceiling [location, extent]. Possible under-ventilation; refer to designer"
C) Switch the isolator on and record the fan as working
D) Nothing, as the tenant's choice is not part of the assessment

**Correct answer: B.** The damp and mould guidance calls blaming "lifestyle choices" "totally unreasonable" and lists switched-off ventilation as a root cause to record. Noise is a design matter (ADF 1.5 to 1.7). Mould triggers the designer's check under PAS 2035 8.2.13 b) (Sections 1 and 6).

---

**Q5.** A home has an open-flued gas fire in the living room, and continuous extract is proposed. What should the assessor do?

A) Run the kitchen fan and watch the fire's flame
B) Advise the householder to stop using the fire
C) Record the appliance, its location and the existing and proposed fans, and refer through the organisation's gas safety procedure or to a Gas Safe registered engineer
D) Nothing, because the fan and fire are in different rooms

**Correct answer: C.** ADF 0.17 says fans might cause gases to spill "even if the combustion appliance and fan are in separate rooms" (Wales 2022: 0.14). Testing belongs to a competent engineer. The assessor recognises, records and refers (Section 7).
