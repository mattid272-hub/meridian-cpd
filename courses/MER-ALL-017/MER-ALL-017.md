

# Awaab's Law Phase 2: What Energy Assessors Must Know
**Course ID:** MER-ALL-017 | **CPD Hours:** 1 | **Published:** October 2026

---

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the key legislative provisions of Awaab's Law Phase 2 and distinguish them from the Phase 1 requirements that applied to damp and mould alone.
- Explain the prescribed response, investigation and remedy timescales that social housing landlords must now meet for qualifying hazards, and how these timescales create direct workflow pressures on energy assessors.
- Evaluate how current EPC assessment, damp/mould survey and retrofit reporting practices must be adapted to align with landlord compliance obligations under the extended regulations.
- Apply appropriate referral and escalation procedures when on-site findings indicate a hazard that falls within the scope of the Phase 2 prescribed timescales.
- Assess the implications of the updated legal framework for professional liability, record-keeping standards and contractual relationships between assessors and registered providers.

## Introduction

Awaab Ishak died in December 2020 from a respiratory condition caused by prolonged exposure to mould in his family's housing association flat in Rochdale. The coroner's findings, published in 2022, triggered a legislative response embedded in the Social Housing (Regulation) Act 2023, which introduced what is now universally known as Awaab's Law. Phase 1 — the initial set of prescribed time limits for social housing landlords to investigate and remedy damp and mould — came into force in 2025 and applied exclusively to those two hazards. Phase 2 extends the regime.

Phase 2 broadens the scope of prescribed timescales beyond damp and mould to encompass a wider set of hazards that pose a serious risk to the health or safety of tenants. The precise categories captured under Phase 2 were confirmed following consultation, and registered providers of social housing are now subject to statutory deadlines for acknowledging, investigating and rectifying these hazards once reported by a tenant. Failure to comply carries regulatory consequences enforced by the Regulator of Social Housing and, in serious cases, the Housing Ombudsman.

For energy assessors, this is not abstract housing policy. If you carry out EPC assessments in social housing stock, if you are commissioned to undertake damp and mould surveys by a registered provider, or if you deliver retrofit assessments and coordination under PAS 2035, you are now operating inside a compliance ecosystem that runs on the clock. Landlords are contractually and legally dependent on the speed and accuracy of your reporting. Your survey findings may trigger — or fail to trigger — a statutory timescale. Your retrofit recommendations may form part of a landlord's remediation plan that must be completed within a prescribed window.

This course sets out what Phase 2 contains, how the timescales work, what they mean for the way you report and refer, and where your professional responsibilities begin and end. It is not a policy overview. It is a working briefing for assessors who need to understand the framework they are now operating within.

## Section 1: The Legislative Framework — What Phase 2 Actually Says

Awaab's Law sits within the Social Housing (Regulation) Act 2023, specifically the provisions that amended the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 by inserting new sections concerning prescribed time limits for repairs relating to specified hazards. Phase 1 was narrow by design: it targeted damp and mould as the initial prescribed hazards, reflecting the specific circumstances of Awaab Ishak's death and the systemic failures identified in the social housing sector's response to those conditions.

Phase 2 extends the prescribed hazard categories. Following the MHCLG consultation (which closed in early 2025), the government confirmed that additional hazards would be brought within scope. These are broadly aligned with the Housing Health and Safety Rating System (HHSRS) hazard profiles and include — among others — excess cold, electrical hazards, structural collapse, falls associated with disrepair, and exposure to carbon monoxide. Assessors should consult the final statutory instrument to confirm the definitive list, as the precise hazard categories and any exclusions are set out there.

The critical mechanism is the same as Phase 1: once a tenant reports a qualifying hazard to their landlord, the landlord must acknowledge the report, commence an investigation, and complete remedial works within prescribed timescales. The specific timescales confirmed for Phase 2 hazards should be verified against the relevant statutory instrument, but the framework distinguishes between emergency hazards (posing an imminent risk to life) and non-emergency but serious hazards, with correspondingly tighter or more extended deadlines.

Three points matter for assessors:

**First**, the clock starts when the tenant reports, not when you arrive on site. If a landlord commissions your survey three weeks after a tenant complaint, the statutory timescale is already running. Your turnaround is not your own to set — it is a residual portion of the landlord's compliance window.

**Second**, the duties are on the landlord, not on the assessor. You are not the duty-holder. But if your report is the investigation that the landlord relies on to satisfy the statutory requirement, your work product is embedded in their compliance. If your report is late, incomplete or ambiguous, the landlord's obligation is frustrated, and your contractual and professional exposure increases accordingly.

**Third**, the hazard categories now overlap substantially with what energy assessors routinely encounter. Excess cold is an EPC and retrofit issue. Damp and mould have always been part of your survey remit. Carbon monoxide risk intersects with heating system assessments. Phase 2 has moved the legal framework directly onto your professional territory.

## Section 2: Timescales, Triggers and the Assessor's Position in the Compliance Chain

Understanding where the energy assessor sits in the landlord's compliance chain is essential. You are typically engaged at one of three points: as a surveyor investigating a reported hazard, as an EPC assessor whose routine visit identifies a potential hazard, or as a retrofit assessor or coordinator planning or overseeing remediation works that may constitute the landlord's "remedy" under the statutory framework.

**The investigation phase.** When a landlord receives a tenant hazard report, they must commence investigation within a prescribed period. For many registered providers, particularly those without large in-house surveying teams, this investigation is outsourced — frequently to DEAs or retrofit assessors with damp and mould survey competence. If you are the commissioned investigator, your survey report is the investigation. The landlord cannot satisfy the statutory investigation timescale until your report is delivered and is of sufficient quality to inform a remediation decision. This places direct time pressure on your scheduling, site attendance and report turnaround. Any standard Service Level Agreement with a registered provider client should now be reviewed against the statutory timescales to ensure compatibility.

**The incidental discovery.** You attend a property to lodge an EPC or carry out a retrofit assessment. During the survey, you observe black mould on window reveals, significant condensation, or evidence of excess cold (perhaps linked to glazing failures — noting the thermal performance standards referenced in [L38] BS 6262-2: Glazing for Buildings: Energy, light and sound, which provides benchmarks for thermal transmittance of glazing systems). You are not there to investigate a hazard complaint. But your observation, if communicated to the landlord, may itself constitute or prompt a "report" that starts the statutory clock. If you do not communicate it, and the tenant subsequently suffers harm, your failure to flag a known hazard raises professional conduct and negligence questions. The practical position is clear: if you see it, you report it. But how you report it — and to whom — now carries legal weight.

**The remediation phase.** Where you are acting as a retrofit coordinator or assessor under PAS 2035, your medium-term improvement plan may be the landlord's intended remedy for a hazard that now has a statutory completion deadline. The phased, whole-house approach that characterises good retrofit practice may conflict with the urgency of a prescribed timescale. You may need to distinguish between interim measures (which may satisfy the immediate statutory obligation) and long-term retrofit solutions. Your reporting must make this distinction explicit so that the landlord can demonstrate compliance while the deeper work proceeds.

## Section 3: Reporting, Record-Keeping and Referral Pathways

Phase 2 does not impose new reporting formats on assessors, but it fundamentally changes the consequences of inadequate reporting. Your reports are now potential compliance evidence. They may be examined by the Regulator of Social Housing, the Housing Ombudsman, or — in the worst case — a coroner. The standard of documentation must reflect this.

**Report content.** Every survey or assessment report that touches on a Phase 2 hazard category should, at minimum, include: a clear identification of the hazard observed (using language consistent with HHSRS hazard categories where applicable); a description of severity and extent; an assessment of whether the hazard poses an imminent risk to the health or safety of occupants; a recommended course of action, distinguishing between emergency interim measures and longer-term remediation; and a clear timestamp — date of inspection, date of report completion, date of submission to the instructing landlord.

**Photographic evidence and measurement data.** Damp readings, thermal imaging where undertaken, relative humidity data, and photographic records are no longer "nice to have" — they are the evidential foundation of the landlord's compliance record. Where glazing performance is relevant to excess cold or condensation, reference to thermal transmittance values and compliance with standards such as [L38] BS 6262-2 should be included where appropriate.

**Referral pathways.** Not every hazard you identify will fall within your professional competence to assess fully. Structural defects, gas safety issues and electrical hazards require referral to appropriately qualified specialists. Your report must make clear when a referral is recommended and to whom. Under Phase 2, the referral itself may be time-critical: if you recommend a Gas Safe engineer attends urgently due to suspected carbon monoxide risk, and the landlord does not act within the prescribed emergency timescale, the landlord is in breach. Your referral recommendation — and the date you made it — becomes part of the audit trail.

**Record retention.** Maintain copies of all reports, correspondence and supporting data for a minimum period consistent with professional indemnity requirements and, ideally, aligned with the limitation periods for housing disrepair claims (typically six years, but potentially longer in personal injury cases). Ensure that records are stored securely and are retrievable — the point at which a regulator or ombudsman requests your file may be years after the original survey.

**Communication protocols.** Agree in advance with each registered provider client how hazard findings will be communicated. Email is the minimum — it provides a dated, retrievable record. Phone calls to flag urgent hazards are appropriate but must be followed up in writing. If a landlord's internal systems use a repairs portal or case management platform, ensure your submissions are logged with timestamps.

## Section 4: Practical Application — Adjusting Your Working Practice

The regulatory framework is only useful if it changes what you actually do on site and at your desk. This section addresses the practical adjustments.

**Contract review.** If you hold framework agreements or standing contracts with registered providers, review them now. Check whether the SLA turnaround times are compatible with the statutory timescales. If your contract allows 10 working days for report delivery but the landlord's investigation window is shorter, you have a misalignment that will generate compliance failures. Renegotiate where necessary — but recognise that tighter timescales may justify revised fee structures.

**On-site protocol.** Adopt a consistent hazard-flagging protocol for every social housing property you enter, regardless of the purpose of your visit. A brief checklist — covering visible damp, mould, condensation, cold surfaces, carbon monoxide alarm presence, obvious electrical or structural defects — takes minutes and protects both you and the landlord. Where you identify a potential emergency hazard, communicate it to the landlord the same day, in writing.

**Interim vs. long-term recommendations.** Train yourself to separate these clearly in every report. A landlord facing a statutory deadline needs to know what can be done immediately (dehumidifiers deployed, mould treatment applied, temporary heating provided, ventilation improved) versus what requires a retrofit programme (insulation upgrades, glazing replacement to current thermal performance standards per [L38] BS 6262-2, heating system replacement). Both matter — but only the former satisfies the immediate legal obligation.

**Professional development.** Ensure your CPD record reflects competence in hazard identification relevant to the Phase 2 categories. If you are not confident in distinguishing HHSRS hazard bands or in assessing severity, seek targeted training. Your professional credibility — and your usefulness to landlord clients — depends on it.

**Insurance.** Confirm with your professional indemnity insurer that your policy covers survey and advisory work that may form part of a landlord's statutory compliance record. The risk profile of this work has changed. Your insurer needs to know.

## Key Takeaways

- Awaab's Law Phase 2 extends statutory hazard response timescales beyond damp and mould to a broader set of hazards including excess cold, carbon monoxide risk and structural defects — all of which intersect with energy assessment and retrofit work.
- The statutory duty sits with the registered provider landlord, but your survey report is frequently the compliance evidence they rely on — making your accuracy, speed and clarity a direct factor in their legal exposure.
- Report turnaround times must be reviewed against the prescribed statutory timescales; existing SLA arrangements may no longer be fit for purpose.
- Every social housing site visit should include a brief hazard-screening protocol regardless of the primary purpose of the assessment, with same-day written notification to the landlord for emergency-level findings.
- Reports must clearly distinguish between interim measures that satisfy immediate statutory obligations and longer-term retrofit solutions that address root causes.
- Record-keeping standards must reflect the possibility that your reports will be scrutinised by regulators, the Housing Ombudsman or a coroner — timestamp everything, retain everything, store it retrievably.
- Review your professional indemnity cover and contractual terms with registered provider clients to ensure they reflect the changed risk profile created by Phase 2.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Question 1:** Under Awaab's Law Phase 2, when does the statutory timescale for a landlord to investigate a reported hazard begin?

A) When the landlord commissions a surveyor to attend the property
B) When the tenant reports the hazard to the landlord
C) When the energy assessor arrives on site to carry out the inspection
D) When the assessor submits the completed survey report

**Correct answer: B.** The statutory clock starts when the tenant makes the hazard report to the landlord. All subsequent steps — commissioning a survey, attending site, completing the investigation — must occur within the residual time available.

---

**Question 2:** An energy assessor attending a social housing property for a routine EPC assessment observes significant black mould on bedroom walls. The tenant has not formally reported this to the landlord. What is the most appropriate course of action?

A) Note it on the EPC but take no further action, as the tenant has not reported it
B) Advise the tenant to report the issue and take no further action yourself
C) Notify the landlord in writing the same day, with photographic evidence and a recommendation for further investigation
D) Complete the EPC and include a general note about property condition in the comments field

**Correct answer: C.** The assessor has a professional responsibility to flag observed hazards to the instructing landlord, particularly in the context of Phase 2 obligations. Written, dated notification with evidence protects the assessor and enables the landlord to act within statutory timescales.

---

**Question 3:** Which of the following best describes the assessor's role in a landlord's compliance with Awaab's Law Phase 2?

A) The assessor is the primary duty-holder and is directly liable for compliance failures
B) The assessor has no role in compliance — the duty sits entirely with the landlord
C) The assessor's survey report typically constitutes the investigation that the landlord relies on to satisfy the statutory requirement, making report quality and timeliness critical
D) The assessor is only relevant if they hold a specific Awaab's Law accreditation

**Correct answer: C.** While the statutory duty is on the landlord, the assessor's work product is embedded in the compliance chain. Late, incomplete or ambiguous reports can directly frustrate the landlord's ability to meet prescribed timescales.

---

**Question 4:** When reporting on a property with condensation-related damp linked to poor glazing thermal performance, which reference standard is most relevant to include in the assessment?

A) BS 5250 — Management of moisture in buildings
B) [L38] BS 6262-2 — Glazing for Buildings: Energy, light and sound
C) PAS 2030 — Installation of energy efficiency measures
D) Building Regulations Part F — Ventilation

**Correct answer: B.** [L38] BS 6262-2 provides benchmarks for thermal transmittance and condensation performance of glazing systems and is directly relevant where poor glazing performance contributes to excess cold or condensation hazards. Other standards listed may also be relevant in context, but BS 6262-2 is the specific glazing standard applicable here.

---

**Question 5:** A retrofit coordinator's whole-house improvement plan for a social housing property includes glazing upgrades, external wall insulation and a heating system replacement, with a projected completion date 18 months from the start of works. The landlord has received a tenant hazard report for excess cold. What should the retrofit coordinator's report recommend?

A) Proceed with the whole-house plan as designed; the long-term solution is the correct approach
B) Abandon the whole-house plan and install only the heating system as an emergency measure
C) Clearly distinguish between interim measures that can be implemented within the statutory timescale and the longer-term retrofit programme, enabling the landlord to satisfy immediate obligations while the deeper work proceeds
D) Advise the landlord that retrofit timescales are not subject to Awaab's Law and no adjustment is needed

**Correct answer: C.** The assessor must separate interim remediation (which can satisfy the immediate statutory duty) from the long-term retrofit solution. This enables the landlord to demonstrate compliance while ensuring that root-cause remediation still proceeds through a properly designed programme.
