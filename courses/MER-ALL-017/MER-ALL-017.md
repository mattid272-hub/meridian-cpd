# Awaab's Law Phase 2: What Energy Assessors Must Know
**Course ID:** MER-ALL-017 | **CPD Hours:** 1 | **Published:** October 2026
*© Meridian CPD 2026. All rights reserved.*

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- State the legal basis of Awaab's Law, who it binds, how it is enforced and which nation it covers.
- List the hazards and timescales that have applied to social landlords in England since 27 October 2025.
- Explain the status of the draft Phase 2 Regulations, what they would change from 30 November 2026, and what is planned for Phase 3 and private renting.
- Distinguish what an EPC, a PAS 2035 retrofit assessment and a ventilation assessment do and do not establish about damp, mould and excess cold.
- Pass on findings that may point to a hazard in a way that fits the landlord's duties, without assuming duties no source places on you.

---

## Introduction

Awaab Ishak was two when he died on 21 December 2020. The inquest, which concluded on 15 November 2022, found that he died of a severe respiratory condition caused by prolonged exposure to mould in the social home his family rented from Rochdale Boroughwide Housing (Awaab's Law consultation, January 2024). "Awaab's Law" is the government's name for the hazard duties in section 10A of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 and the regulations made under it.

This course covers England only. Section 10A applies only where "the dwelling is in England" (section 10A(1)), and the regulations "apply to lessors of social homes in England" (SI 2025/1042, regulation 1).

This edition replaces the earlier October 2026 edition. That edition presented Phase 2 as in force when it is a draft due to start on 30 November 2026, put carbon monoxide in Phase 2 rather than Phase 3, said Phase 1 covered damp and mould alone when it also covers every emergency hazard, and named the Regulator of Social Housing as enforcer when enforcement is by tenants through complaints and the courts. It also gave no timescales, and its advice to cite a glazing standard and learn HHSRS bands had no basis in the Awaab's Law sources.

The legal status statements here are correct to 14 October 2026; Phase 2 was still before Parliament on that date.

## Section 1: The Law in Force Now

**In short:** Since 27 October 2025, registered providers of social housing in England must investigate any emergency hazard except lack of space and complete any relevant safety work within 24 hours; for potential significant damp, mould or fungal growth hazards, they must investigate within 10 working days of becoming aware and make them safe within 5 working days after that, subject to the detail below. Tenants enforce this through claims for breach of covenant; Awaab's Law does not provide for fines.

### Where the duty comes from

Section 42 of the Social Housing (Regulation) Act 2023 inserted sections 10A and 10B into the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, in force from 20 September 2023 (section 42, commencement note). Section 10A implies into each qualifying lease a covenant by the landlord to comply with "all prescribed requirements" (section 10A(2)), and requires regulations setting periods for landlords to act on prescribed hazards (section 10A(3)). A lease qualifies where the dwelling is in England, the landlord is a registered provider of social housing, the dwelling is social housing other than low cost home ownership, and the fitness for human habitation covenant in section 9A applies, or would apply, to the lease (section 10A(1) and (6)). "Prescribed hazard" means a hazard prescribed under section 2 of the Housing Act 2004 (section 10A(7); section 10(2)): the Housing Health and Safety Rating System (HHSRS) hazards in Schedule 1 to the HHSRS (England) Regulations 2005.

The regulations are the Hazards in Social Housing (Prescribed Requirements) (England) Regulations 2025, SI 2025/1042, made on 23 September 2025 and in force since 27 October 2025. The government calls this Phase 1.

### What counts as a hazard

Both categories are judged by what a reasonable landlord "with the relevant knowledge" would do, meaning what it knows or ought to know "about the health and circumstances of the occupier" (regulation 3(2)). The categories are:
- Emergency hazard: an "imminent and significant risk of harm" that a reasonable landlord would take steps to make safe within 24 hours. This covers any prescribed hazard except one associated with lack of space for living and sleeping (regulation 3).
- Significant hazard: a significant risk of harm that a reasonable landlord would take steps to make safe "as a matter of urgency (but not within 24 hours)". In Phase 1 this is limited to damp, mould or fungal growth (regulation 3).

The hazard must arise from a deficiency the landlord is responsible for, not wholly or mainly from the tenant's breach of the lease (regulation 3(2)).

### The timescales

Working days exclude weekends, Christmas Day, Good Friday and bank holidays in England and Wales (regulation 2). Periods after an investigation count from the day after it is completed. The periods are:
- Emergency hazard: investigation by a competent investigator and any relevant safety work completed within 24 hours of the landlord, aware of the issue, having reasonable grounds to believe there is an emergency hazard (regulation 5).
- Potential significant hazard: investigation completed within 10 working days beginning on the day after the landlord "becomes aware", or, if the tenant asks for a physical inspection before it is completed, within 10 working days beginning on the day after that request (regulation 6).
- Written summary to the tenant within 3 working days (regulation 9).
- Safety work on a significant hazard completed within 5 working days (regulation 11).
- Supplementary preventative work begun within 5 working days or, if that is not reasonably practicable, as soon as reasonably practicable and within 12 weeks; then completed "within a reasonable period", with the home kept safe meanwhile (regulations 12 and 13).
- If the home cannot be made safe in time, suitable alternative accommodation "at no cost to the lessee" (regulation 16).

### How it is enforced

The regulations are enforceable "only through actions for breach of the covenant" (section 10A(4)), that is, by the tenant's claim, and a court may order specific performance (regulation 19). The landlord has a defence if it "used all reasonable endeavours" (section 10A(5)). Awaab's Law "does not provide for fines"; tenants can complain to the Housing Ombudsman after the landlord's own process, and the Regulator of Social Housing "does not have a role in determining individual complaints" (Phase 2 guidance, section 13).

## Section 2: Phase 2, Phase 3 and Private Renting

**In short:** Treat Phase 2 as a draft: if it is approved and made, excess cold, excess heat and other hazards join damp and mould as significant hazards from 30 November 2026, with the same timescales, and until then Phase 1 applies. Carbon monoxide is planned for Phase 3, though an imminent and significant risk is already an emergency hazard. No private landlord has Awaab's Law duties today; extending them is out to consultation until 18 December 2026.

### Status of Phase 2

Phase 2 is the draft Hazards in Social Housing (Prescribed Requirements) (England) (Amendment) Regulations 2026. legislation.gov.uk says it "has not yet been made". Parliament's procedure record shows it laid before both Houses on 13 July 2026 under the draft affirmative procedure and debated in a Lords Grand Committee on 13 October 2026, with no approval by either House recorded by 14 October. If made, it comes into force on 30 November 2026 (draft regulation 1). gov.uk adds "(subject to Parliamentary approval)". The Phase 1 guidance applies until 11:59pm on 29 November 2026, and the Phase 2 guidance cited in this course from 30 November 2026 (Awaab's Law collection page). Until the instrument is in force, the Phase 1 law summarised in Section 1 is what applies.

### What the draft would change

The draft amends only regulations 3, 4 and 21 of SI 2025/1042 (draft, contents), so the timescales stay the same. The changes are:
- More significant hazards: paragraphs 2, 3, 14A, 18A, 21, 22, 23, 23A and 29 of Schedule 1 to the HHSRS Regulations 2005 join damp and mould (draft regulation 3). These are excess cold, excess heat, domestic hygiene, falls on the level, falling on stairs, falling between levels, electrical hazards, fire and explosions, and structural collapse and falling elements (Schedule 1).
- A new categorising test: a significant hazard is one a reasonable landlord would "make safe within 15 working days" (but not within 24 hours) (draft regulation 3). This is part of the definition, not a deadline.
- A fire exclusion: fire hazards caused by a deficiency in the parts of a block regulated by the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, which the Explanatory Note describes as the structure, external walls, common parts and doors between homes and common parts, are taken out of Awaab's Law.
- For the new hazards the "relevant time" is 30 November 2026 (draft regulation 5). A concern reported after then triggers the requirements even if the landlord knew of the hazard before (Phase 2 guidance, section 12).

Excess cold and excess heat sit closest to your work. Where the risk from either is imminent and significant, it is already an emergency hazard; Phase 2 adds them at the significant level.

### The HHSRS has changed too

From 23 June 2026 SI 2026/571 inserted paragraph 4A "Indoor Air Pollutants", which includes carbon monoxide, into Schedule 1 and omitted paragraph 6 on carbon monoxide (Schedule 1, annotations). New HHSRS operating guidance for England was published the same day. But for Awaab's Law "An HHSRS assessment is not required", and there is "no specific requirement for investigators" to be HHSRS qualified (Phase 2 guidance).

### Carbon monoxide and Phase 3

Paragraph 4A is not in the draft's list, so carbon monoxide is not a Phase 2 hazard. The government's response of 25 June 2025 listed "Carbon Monoxide and fuel combustion products" under Phase 3 for 2027 (Annex A). An imminent and significant carbon monoxide risk has been an emergency hazard since 27 October 2025 (regulation 3).

Phase 3 is government policy, not law: it would extend the requirements to all remaining HHSRS hazards except overcrowding (Phase 2 guidance). The June 2025 response said 2027; the July 2026 guidance gives no date, and legislation.gov.uk listed no Phase 3 draft in October 2026.

### Private renting

No private landlord has Awaab's Law duties today. Section 60 of the Renters' Rights Act 2025 would extend section 10A beyond social housing leases, but it is "prospective" and was "not in force at Royal Assent" (legislation.gov.uk). On 9 October 2026 the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government opened a consultation, "Extending Awaab's Law to private rented tenancies and to licences", closing on 18 December 2026; it applies to England and leaves timescales to the consultation. Course MER-ALL-008 covers the Renters' Rights Act.

### Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland

In Wales, landlords under secure contracts, periodic standard contracts and fixed term standard contracts of under seven years must ensure the dwelling is fit for human habitation (Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016, section 91, in force 1 December 2022), with fitness determined under the Renting Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) (Wales) Regulations 2022. This course has not established whether Wales plans timescale rules like Awaab's Law; check gov.wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland are not covered.

## Section 3: What Your Assessments Do and Do Not Establish

**In short:** An EPC does not comment on the safety or maintenance of services and does not tell a landlord whether there is an excess cold hazard. Your retrofit record of where mould is and how bad it is may be useful evidence, but it is not the Awaab's Law investigation unless the landlord commissioned you to carry one out. Remember that the existence of a ventilation system is not proof that a building is adequately ventilated.

### The EPC

An EPC's purpose "is to indicate how energy efficient a building is". It "will not comment on the safety aspects or maintenance of the services", nor confirm that a system "is fit for purpose" (DCLG, A guide to energy performance certificates for the marketing, sale and let of dwellings, December 2017, chapter 3). Neither the RdSAP 10 Conventions v12.2 nor the RdSAP10 Specification v4.0 mentions damp or mould.

So an EPC does not tell a landlord whether there is an excess cold hazard. The triage guidance defines it as how far a dwelling can maintain an appropriate internal temperature, given heating, insulation, ventilation and overall thermal performance. It sets no thresholds and asks for case by case judgement (Phase 2 triage guidance, general points). It gives a complete loss of heating in colder periods as an emergency example and says landlords "are not responsible for tenant's energy costs" (triage guidance, excess cold). Its only references to EPC metrics concern the minimum energy efficiency standard for social homes, not the hazard test.

### The retrofit assessment

PAS 2035:2023 asks more. The assessment shall include an appraisal of condition and building services (clause 7.3.2 b); "the location and severity" of any defects, leaks, condensation or mould growth (clause 7.3.2 d); confirmation that services work "(or otherwise)" (clause 7.3.2 j); and an appraisal of occupancy, including vulnerable persons (clause 7.3.2 k). The report goes to the Retrofit Designer with photographs of identified defects (clause 7.4.1), sorting defects into those to repair before retrofit can proceed and those recommended but not essential (clause 7.4.2). A PAS "shall" binds you where a scheme, contract or funding rule adopts it; the damp and mould guidance says landlords retrofitting under a government energy efficiency scheme "must be compliant with PAS 2035".

What a retrofit assessment does not do is decide whether an Awaab's Law hazard exists. PAS 2035:2023 does not mention Awaab's Law or the HHSRS. Under the Regulations that is decided by an investigation carried out by a competent investigator for the landlord (regulations 5 and 6). Your record of where the mould is and how bad it is may be useful evidence, but it is not the investigation unless the landlord commissioned you to carry one out.

### The ventilation assessment

Clause 7.3.2 f) requires a review of existing ventilation: fans, background ventilators, and measured flows below the minimum. It refers to "the checklist provided in Table C.1", but the published text contains no Table C.1. Annex C warns that "the existence of a ventilation system is not proof that a building is adequately ventilated" (C.1). Evidence of condensation or mould requires the Retrofit Designer to confirm whether ventilation is adequate and specify an upgrade if needed (clause 8.2.13). Where there is condensation or mould, no insulation or airtightness measures are proposed and ventilation does not meet Approved Document F Table D1, it shall be upgraded; whether or not ventilation is adequate, the Retrofit Coordinator shall advise the client on managing condensation, damp and mould (C.3.1).

Approved Document F Volume 1 (2021 edition, England) says building work should not reduce ventilation unless the result meets the minimum standards, accepts PAS 2035 as a way to show this (paragraphs 3.6 and 3.7), and its checklist says to look for mould or condensation and, if either is present, add ventilation or seek specialist advice (Table D1, note 1). A 2026 edition takes effect on 24 March 2027 for most work (its front page); ask building control which applies.

### Where retrofit and Awaab's Law meet

The damp and mould guidance says "some energy efficiency interventions may reduce overall ventilation rates", which "can increase the risk of damp and mould" if not addressed. The triage guidance adds that larger works "such as upgrades to insulation" may be needed under other standards, but "Awaab's Law is not intended to drive these improvement works" (excess cold). A retrofit programme is not, in itself, the landlord's Awaab's Law response. But where a damp and mould hazard results from "the absence of ventilation or poor insulation", the Phase 2 guidance says the landlord must take remedial action "such as installing mechanical ventilation or effective insulation" (section 2.2).

## Section 4: When Your Findings Meet the Landlord's Duties

**In short:** These Regulations place no duty on you, though your scheme, contract or the general law may set expectations, so ask your scheme and read your terms. A sensible habit is to tell the landlord promptly in writing when you see a possible hazard, with dated photographs and locations, describing what you saw and leaving categorising to them. The landlord will usually be considered aware from the date you notify it, subject to the contractor exception below.

### No duty on you in these Regulations

The covenant is the landlord's (section 10A(2)) and the Regulations apply to lessors (regulation 1). The Act, the Regulations and the Phase 2 guidance for landlords do not mention energy or retrofit assessors, and the scheme quality plans and rules checked for this course do not mention Awaab's Law. Your scheme, your contract or the general law may still set expectations; ask your scheme and read your terms.

### When your report makes the landlord aware

The clock runs from when the landlord "becomes aware" (regulation 6), not necessarily from your visit. Where a third party reports a potential hazard, the landlord "will usually be considered to have become aware" on the date it is notified. If the third party is the landlord's contractor, awareness may date from the contractor's own knowledge, depending on whether it is legally the landlord's agent (Phase 2 guidance, section 4).

### If you are asked to investigate

A competent investigator is someone who, "in the reasonable opinion of the lessor", has the skills and experience to determine whether a significant or emergency hazard exists (regulation 2). A standard investigation must confirm whether there is such a hazard and, if possible, the work needed; it may be remote or in person (Phase 2 guidance, section 5). That is a different job from an EPC or retrofit assessment, and it runs inside the landlord's 10 working days.

### Practice that fits the timescales

None of this is a legal requirement on you. These are sensible habits:
- Agree at instruction whether you are doing an EPC, a retrofit assessment or a hazard investigation.
- When you see damp, mould, a heating failure or another possible hazard, tell the landlord promptly in writing, with dated photographs and locations. Describe what you saw and leave categorising to the landlord.
- If commissioned to investigate, ask the date the landlord became aware.
- Refer what is outside your competence, such as electrical faults, in writing.
- Keep dated records; the guidance tells landlords to "keep clear records" (Phase 2 guidance).

## Key Takeaways

The points to carry away:
- Awaab's Law is section 10A of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 and SI 2025/1042. It binds registered providers of social housing in England only.
- Phase 1, in force since 27 October 2025, covers every emergency hazard except lack of space, and significant damp and mould hazards.
- The core periods: 24 hours for emergencies; 10 working days to investigate; 3 for the written summary; 5 to make safe; further work begun within 5 working days or at most 12 weeks.
- Phase 2 is a draft due on 30 November 2026 if approved. It adds excess cold, excess heat, falls, electrical, fire and explosions, structural collapse and domestic hygiene, with the same timescales.
- Carbon monoxide is planned for Phase 3, though an imminent and significant risk is already an emergency hazard. Private renting stays outside Awaab's Law while section 60 of the Renters' Rights Act 2025 is not in force.
- An EPC does not comment on safety. A retrofit assessment records condensation and mould but does not decide whether an Awaab's Law hazard exists.
- The Awaab's Law sources place no duty on assessors; prompt, dated, written findings to the landlord are good practice.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** What was the status of the Phase 2 amending regulations on 14 October 2026?

A) In force since 27 October 2025
B) Made on 13 July 2026 and in force from that date
C) A draft laid on 13 July 2026, due in force on 30 November 2026 if approved
D) Withdrawn after the 2024 consultation

**Correct answer: C.** legislation.gov.uk says the draft "has not yet been made", and draft regulation 1 gives 30 November 2026 (Section 2).

---

**Q2.** Under SI 2025/1042, by when must a standard investigation of a potential significant hazard be completed?

A) Within 10 working days beginning on the day after the landlord becomes aware
B) Within 24 hours of the tenant's report
C) Within 15 working days of the investigator's visit
D) Within 12 weeks of the report

**Correct answer: A.** Regulation 6 sets 10 working days; the other figures are the emergency period, the draft's definition and the limit for starting supplementary work (Sections 1 and 2).

---

**Q3.** Which of these would the draft 2026 Regulations NOT add as a significant hazard?

A) Excess cold
B) Excess heat
C) Electrical hazards
D) Carbon monoxide

**Correct answer: D.** The draft adds HHSRS paragraphs 2, 3, 14A, 18A, 21, 22, 23, 23A and 29, not 4A, and the June 2025 response put carbon monoxide in Phase 3 (Section 2).

---

**Q4.** According to the DCLG guide to EPCs, which statement is correct?

A) An EPC confirms that the heating system is safe and fit for purpose
B) An EPC indicates how energy efficient the building is and does not comment on the safety or maintenance of services
C) An EPC is an HHSRS assessment
D) An EPC satisfies the landlord's duty to investigate a potential hazard

**Correct answer: B.** The guide says an EPC "will not comment on the safety aspects or maintenance of the services" (chapter 3; Section 3).

---

**Q5.** A retrofit assessor emails a social landlord in England about extensive mould found during an assessment. When is the landlord usually considered to have become aware of the potential hazard?

A) When the tenant next complains
B) When the Retrofit Designer accepts the assessment report
C) On the date the landlord is notified by the assessor
D) Only after an HHSRS assessment

**Correct answer: C.** The guidance says a landlord notified by a third party "will usually be considered to have become aware" on that date, and no HHSRS assessment is required (Phase 2 guidance, section 4; Section 4).
