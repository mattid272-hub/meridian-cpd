

# EPC Reform and the Future Home Buying Process: What Assessors Must Know

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the specific reforms to the home buying and selling process announced by the UK Government in mid-2026 and explain how they alter the role of the EPC in property transactions.

## Introduction

The EPC has been embedded in property transactions since 2007, and for most of that period, its role has been static: a legal obligation triggered at the point of marketing, frequently treated as a tick-box exercise by sellers, agents, and conveyancers alike. That era is ending.

The reforms signalled by DLUHC and now crystallising through the home buying and selling reform programme represent the most significant change to the transactional role of the EPC since its inception. The direction of travel — confirmed through the June 2026 announcements and the preceding consultations — positions the EPC not as a standalone compliance document but as an integrated component of a broader upfront information pack designed to reduce failed transactions, improve buyer confidence, and accelerate the decarbonisation of the housing stock.

For working assessors, this is not abstract policy. It changes when you are instructed, what you are expected to deliver, who is commissioning your work, and what liability attaches to your output. If you are a DEA producing EPCs for estate agents on a volume basis, your commercial model is about to shift. If you are a Retrofit Assessor advising homeowners on improvement pathways, the referral pipeline from EPC assessments is about to become more structured — and more scrutinised.

This course cuts through the consultation language and examines what is actually confirmed, what remains under development, and what you need to do now. It is built on primary sources: the DLUHC policy statements, the consultation responses, the Building Research Establishment's technical work on the SAP/RdSAP methodology reforms running in parallel, and the regulatory framework under the Energy Performance of Buildings (England and Wales) Regulations 2012 (as amended).

There is no point pretending everything is finalised. Some implementation detail remains outstanding, and where that is the case, this course says so. But the structural direction is clear enough that any assessor still operating on a 2019 business model needs to recalibrate — starting now.

## Section 1: The Home Buying and Selling Reform Programme — What Has Actually Been Confirmed

The home buying and selling reform agenda predates the current government. The 2017–2019 call for evidence, the 2020 consultation on improving the home buying and selling process, and the subsequent pilots — including the BASPI (Buying and Selling Property Information) trials — all pointed toward a common conclusion: the English and Welsh conveyancing process is slow, opaque, and disproportionately prone to collapse. Approximately one in three transactions still falls through after an offer is accepted, at an average cost to consumers of over £2,000 per failed transaction.

The June 2026 announcements confirmed the government's commitment to mandating upfront material information at the point of marketing. This builds on the National Trading Standards Estate and Letting Agency Team (NTSELAT) material information requirements introduced under the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 (as applied through CPR guidance Parts A, B, and C). Part C — which covers property-specific information including energy performance data — is the critical interface for assessors.

What is confirmed:

**Mandatory upfront property information packs** will be required before a property is listed for sale. The EPC is explicitly included as a core component, not a standalone document, but part of an integrated data set that will also encompass title verification, lease information (where applicable), searches, and building safety data.

**Earlier triggering of the EPC requirement.** The current framework requires a valid EPC at the point of marketing. The reformed process tightens this by requiring the EPC to be in place and data-verified before the property is listed on any portal or marketed by any agent. This closes the existing compliance gap where properties are marketed with EPCs "in progress."

**Digital integration.** EPC data will be required in a machine-readable, interoperable format that feeds into a standardised property data set. The existing EPC Register infrastructure, maintained by Landmark Information Group under contract, is being adapted to support API-based data sharing with property portals, conveyancing platforms, and lender systems.

**Potential reduction in EPC validity periods** is under active consideration, though a final decision on whether the current 10-year validity will be shortened has not been formally confirmed at the time of publication. Assessors should monitor DLUHC announcements on this point.

## Section 2: How the Reforms Change Assessment Timing, Scope, and Commissioning

The practical consequences for assessors flow from three structural shifts: who commissions the assessment, when it must be completed, and what it must contain.

**Commissioning dynamics.** Under the current model, the estate agent typically drives EPC instruction — often selecting the cheapest available assessor to fulfil a compliance obligation as quickly as possible. The reformed framework introduces a different dynamic. Because the EPC must be completed, verified, and integrated into an upfront information pack before marketing commences, the instruction will increasingly come earlier in the pre-marketing phase. Sellers — and their conveyancers — will become direct commissioners more frequently, particularly where properties require remedial work or where the EPC rating is expected to influence pricing strategy.

This has direct implications for the race-to-the-bottom pricing that has characterised the DEA market for years. An EPC that is part of a regulated upfront pack carries greater weight and, critically, greater scrutiny. Assessors producing inaccurate or poorly evidenced certificates within this framework face exposure not only to accreditation scheme sanctions but to challenges from buyers relying on the pack information to inform purchase decisions.

**Assessment scope.** The integration of EPC data into the broader property information pack means assessors should expect increased demand for supplementary information beyond the standard RdSAP output. The direction of travel points toward EPC assessments that explicitly cross-reference improvement recommendations with estimated costs, available funding routes, and — where the assessor holds appropriate qualifications — retrofit pathway options. This is not yet a formal requirement in the assessment methodology, but the infrastructure being built around the upfront information pack clearly anticipates it.

**Timing pressures.** Earlier instruction sounds positive for workflow planning, but it also compresses the window between instruction and required delivery. Assessors operating in high-volume markets should anticipate seasonal spikes intensifying, particularly in spring and early summer selling seasons, as sellers seek to have packs prepared before listing.

The shift also raises a practical question about re-assessment frequency. If a property undergoes improvement works after an EPC is lodged but before a sale completes, the current framework does not require a fresh assessment. Under the reformed regime, there is growing expectation — though not yet a statutory mandate — that material changes to the property should trigger an updated EPC within the pack. Assessors should be prepared to advise clients on when a re-assessment is warranted, even where not yet legally required.

## Section 3: Data Standards, Digital Infrastructure, and the Evolving EPC Register

The technical backbone of the reforms is data interoperability. The EPC as a PDF or paper certificate is functionally obsolete within the reformed framework. What matters is the structured data behind it — and whether that data can be consumed, verified, and acted upon by other systems in the transaction chain.

**The EPC Register and API access.** The EPC Register has historically functioned as a repository — assessors lodge certificates, and the public can search them. Under the reformed model, the Register must function as an active data service. Property portals (Rightmove, Zoopla, OnTheMarket) will pull EPC data directly via API, populating listings automatically. Lender valuation systems will cross-reference EPC data as part of mortgage risk assessments, particularly as green mortgage products proliferate and lenders are required to report portfolio energy performance under evolving disclosure frameworks.

For assessors, this means the data quality of every field in RdSAP matters more than ever. Errors that previously resulted in nothing more than a slightly inaccurate recommendation report will now propagate across transaction platforms, trigger valuation queries, and potentially stall sales. The pressure on evidencing conventions — particularly around insulation assumptions, heating system age, and window specifications — will intensify. Accreditation scheme auditors are already signalling increased scrutiny on default assumptions, and assessors should anticipate that the proportion of audits focusing on data accuracy (rather than procedural compliance) will rise.

**The BASPI framework.** The Buying and Selling Property Information (BASPI) standard defines the structured data fields required for the upfront property information pack. EPC data maps onto specific BASPI fields. Assessors do not need to understand the full BASPI schema, but they should be aware that their output is no longer an endpoint — it is an input into a larger data set. Recommendations that are vague, generic, or disconnected from the specific property will be increasingly visible to downstream users.

**Interaction with RdSAP 2012 and the Home Energy Model.** The methodology reforms running in parallel — specifically the anticipated transition from RdSAP to the Home Energy Model (HEM) — intersect with the transactional reforms but operate on a separate timeline. Assessors should avoid conflating the two. The transactional reforms apply to the current EPC methodology. When HEM is formally adopted, it will change the calculation engine, but the transactional integration requirements will persist and likely deepen.

**Data retention and liability.** As EPC data feeds into regulated property information packs, questions of data retention, correction, and liability become more acute. Assessors should ensure their professional indemnity insurance covers the expanded downstream reliance on their data output. This is a conversation to have with your insurer now, not after a claim arises.

## Section 4: Preparing Your Practice — Commercial and Operational Readiness

This section is about what you do in the next six to twelve months.

**Review your pricing model.** If you are producing EPCs at £35–£50 for agency chains, consider what the reformed framework means for your risk exposure relative to your fee. An EPC that feeds into a regulated upfront information pack, is consumed by lender systems, and is scrutinised by buyers and their solicitors is not the same product as a PDF lodged on a register and forgotten. Price accordingly, and be prepared to articulate the value proposition to commissioners who are accustomed to bottom-of-market fees.

**Invest in evidence recording.** Photographic evidence, measurement records, and notes on departures from default assumptions should already be standard practice. Under the reformed regime, they become essential. If a buyer challenges an EPC rating and the sale has been informed by the upfront pack, your evidence file is your defence. Ensure your recording system is robust, backed up, and retrievable.

**Understand the retrofit interface.** The government's intent to link EPC improvement recommendations to actionable retrofit pathways is clear. If you are a DEA without retrofit qualifications, consider how you will handle the increased demand for improvement advice that goes beyond the standard recommendation report. This might mean formal upskilling — pursuing Retrofit Assessor or Retrofit Coordinator qualifications — or it might mean developing referral partnerships with qualified retrofit professionals. Either way, the days of generic recommendations with no follow-through are numbered.

**Monitor accreditation scheme updates.** Your scheme — whether Elmhurst, Quidos, Stroma, or ECMK — will issue updated guidance as the implementation regulations are finalised. Engage with these updates actively. Do not wait for the annual convention summary.

**Engage with your professional indemnity provider.** As noted above, the expanded reliance on EPC data within the transaction chain changes your risk profile. Confirm that your cover reflects the reformed environment.

## Key Takeaways

- The EPC is being repositioned from a standalone compliance document to an integrated component of a mandatory upfront property information pack, fundamentally changing its transactional significance.
- Assessment timing will shift earlier in the sale process, with stricter requirements for the EPC to be lodged and verified before any marketing activity commences.
- Data accuracy is no longer a matter of professional pride alone — errors will propagate across lender, portal, and conveyancing systems with tangible consequences for transactions and for assessor liability.
- The BASPI framework treats EPC data as a structured input, not a standalone output; assessors should understand that their data feeds directly into the property information ecosystem.
- Pricing models built on high-volume, low-margin EPC production are increasingly incompatible with the quality, evidence, and liability demands of the reformed regime.
- The interface between EPC recommendations and retrofit pathways is being formalised; assessors without retrofit competence should upskill or establish robust referral arrangements.
- Professional indemnity cover should be reviewed now to ensure it reflects the expanded downstream reliance on EPC data within regulated property transaction packs.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Question 1:** Under the reformed home buying and selling process, at what point must a valid EPC be in place?

A) Within 28 days of accepting an offer
B) Before the property is marketed or listed on any portal
C) At the point of exchange of contracts
D) Within 7 days of the property being listed

**Correct answer: B.** The reforms require the EPC to be completed, lodged, and verified before the property is marketed. This closes the existing compliance gap where properties could be listed with an EPC "in progress."

---

**Question 2:** What is the primary function of the BASPI framework in relation to EPCs?

A) It replaces the RdSAP methodology with a new calculation engine
B) It defines the structured data fields for the upfront property information pack, into which EPC data feeds
C) It establishes new accreditation requirements for DEAs
D) It sets minimum EPC ratings for properties to be marketed

**Correct answer: B.** BASPI (Buying and Selling Property Information) defines the standardised data structure for upfront property information. EPC data maps onto specific fields within this schema and is consumed by downstream systems.

---

**Question 3:** Which of the following is a confirmed element of the June 2026 reform announcements?

A) Reduction of EPC validity from 10 years to 5 years
B) Mandatory retrofit assessment for all properties rated D or below
C) Requirement for EPC data to be available in machine-readable, interoperable format
D) Compulsory re-assessment after any improvement works

**Correct answer: C.** Digital integration and machine-readable data are confirmed elements of the reform programme. While validity period changes and re-assessment triggers are under consideration, they have not been formally confirmed at the time of publication.

---

**Question 4:** Why should assessors review their professional indemnity insurance in light of the reforms?

A) Because PI insurance is no longer required under the reformed framework
B) Because the expanded downstream reliance on EPC data within regulated transaction packs changes the assessor's risk profile
C) Because accreditation schemes are withdrawing mandatory PI requirements
D) Because the government is introducing a state-backed indemnity scheme for assessors

**Correct answer: B.** EPC data feeding into regulated upfront information packs means it is relied upon by buyers, lenders, and solicitors in ways that go beyond the current framework. This expanded reliance increases potential exposure and should be reflected in PI cover.

---

**Question 5:** What is the relationship between the Home Energy Model (HEM) and the transactional reforms discussed in this course?

A) HEM implementation is a prerequisite for the transactional reforms to take effect
B) They are the same reform programme announced under different names
C) They are parallel but separate reform streams — the transactional reforms apply under the current EPC methodology
D) HEM replaces the need for EPCs in property transactions entirely

**Correct answer: C.** The methodology reform (transition from RdSAP to the Home Energy Model) and the transactional reform (integration of EPCs into upfront property information packs) are separate programmes operating on different timelines. Assessors should avoid conflating the two.
