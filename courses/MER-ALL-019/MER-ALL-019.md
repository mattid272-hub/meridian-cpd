## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- Explain in plain terms what a U-value is, how it becomes a heat loss figure, and why the moisture risk in a wall moves when insulation is added.
- Explain thermal bridging at junctions and the "fabric first" approach in PAS 2035:2023, and where cavity wall insulation sits within it.
- Recognise the wall conditions that published sources link to cavity wall insulation problems: exposure to wind-driven rain, narrow or obstructed cavities, damaged brickwork and pointing, and corroded wall ties.
- Recognise and record the common failure patterns after filling without stating a cause as fact.
- Signpost a householder to the right remediation route, and know which free schemes do not cover cavity walls.

## Introduction

Cavity wall insulation is the measure most assessors meet more often than any other. The RdSAP defaults show why it has been funded so widely: an as-built cavity wall from before 1976 is given a default U-value of 1.5 W/m²K in England, and a filled one 0.7 W/m²K. It is also the measure most often blamed when a householder finds a damp patch on an outside wall. Both can be true of the same house, and this course is about why.

Part 1 of the Insulation Series sets out the principles that apply to every insulation measure, then applies them to cavity walls. It does not train you to survey a wall for fill or decide whether an installation is defective. Your job is to recognise and record, to write "possible" rather than "is", and to know when to recommend professional advice.

## Section 1: How Insulation Works, and Why Moisture Travels With It

**In short:** A U-value is the heat lost per square metre for each degree of temperature difference, so lower is better: on the England RdSAP defaults, filling a pre-1976 cavity wall takes it from 1.5 to 0.7 W/m²K. Historic England says filling risks moisture crossing to the inside leaf; Approved Document L says gaps in insulation risk condensation and mould. Damp following the weather and condensation following the occupants and season are this course's guide, not tests.

### U-values in plain terms

BRE's "Conventions for U-value calculations" (BR 443, 2019 edition) explains the U-value by how it is used: the U-value of an element multiplied by its area gives the rate of heat loss per degree of temperature difference, in W/m²K (Section 1). Approved Document L says U-values "should be assessed using the methods and conventions set out in the Building Research Establishment's BR 443" (paragraph 4.1). So a U-value of 1.5 means each square metre loses 1.5 watts for every degree the inside is warmer than the outside. Lower is better.

The arithmetic behind it is simple. Each layer has a thermal resistance equal to its thickness divided by its conductivity (R = d/λ). The layer resistances are added to an inside and an outside surface resistance, and "U = 1/Rtot" (BR 443, Section 1). For walls, BR 443 gives surface resistances of 0.13 m²K/W inside and 0.04 m²K/W outside (Section 4), and an unventilated air cavity a resistance of 0.18 m²K/W (paragraph 4.7.1). Filling the cavity replaces that air with a material of much lower conductivity.

One detail matters for cavity walls. BR 443 says "The effect of wall ties may be negligible in an uninsulated cavity" (paragraph 4.8.2); once the cavity is filled, steel ties crossing the insulation are allowed for, at a typical 2.5 ties per square metre.

### Worked numbers

RdSAP 10 (BRE specification, version 3.0, June 2025, Table 6, England) gives these default wall U-values:

| Age band | Years | Cavity as built | Filled cavity |
|---|---|---|---|
| A to E | before 1900 to 1975 | 1.5 | 0.7 |
| F | 1976 to 1982 | 1.0 | 0.40 |
| G and H | 1983 to 1995 | 0.60 | 0.35 |

All W/m²K. From band I (1996 to 2002) onwards, the filled cavity defaults are marked "assumed as built".

Approved Document L Table 4.3 ("Limiting U-values for existing elements in existing dwellings") gives, for "Wall – cavity insulation", a threshold of 0.70 and an improved value of 0.55 W/m²K. Note 5 says: "This applies only to a wall that is suitable for cavity insulation. Where this is not the case, it should be treated as 'wall – internal or external insulation'" (threshold 0.70, improved 0.30).

Using heat loss rate = U × area × temperature difference, take an age band C house with 80 m² of net external wall, at 20°C inside and 0°C outside. The inputs are illustrative and the calculation is this course's own.

| Wall | U-value (W/m²K) | Calculation | Wall heat loss rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cavity as built (RdSAP default) | 1.5 | 1.5 × 80 × 20 | 2,400 W |
| Filled cavity (RdSAP default) | 0.7 | 0.7 × 80 × 20 | 1,120 W |
| ADL Table 4.3 improved value | 0.55 | 0.55 × 80 × 20 | 880 W |

On the defaults, filling cuts wall heat loss by 1,280 W, just over half. The same arithmetic shows why a wet wall matters. The BEIS research report "Waterproofing cavity walls to allow insulation in exposed areas" (Research Paper 2021/017, UCL and BRE, issue 1, May 2020) measured laboratory walls filled with EPS beads at "0.30-0.38 W/m2K in dry conditions and 0.38-0.51 W/m2K in wet conditions" (section 3.6.2).

Defaults are defaults. PAS 2035:2023 notes that for traditionally constructed buildings "it might not be appropriate to accept the default U-values offered by RdSAP software" (clause 7.3.2, note 8).

### The warm side and the cold side

In an unfilled cavity wall, the inner leaf is kept relatively warm by the room and the outer leaf takes the weather. Rain soaking into the outer leaf can run down its inner face. Historic England describes the intention, even once a cavity is filled, as being that "any moisture should make its way through the material and drain out of the bottom of the cavity" (Energy Efficiency and Historic Buildings: Insulating Early Cavity Walls, section 3.5).

Filling changes this. Historic England: "Filling the cavity with insulation introduces the risk that moisture will be able to find its way across to the inside leaf whatever the insulation material used" (section 3.3). The BEIS report says that in exposed areas "the insulation can facilitate moisture penetration across the cavity from the external to internal wall layers" (Introduction).

There is a temperature change too, which is this course's reading of the physics rather than a quotation. Once the cavity is filled, less heat from the room reaches the outer leaf, so it runs colder and dries more slowly after rain. Insulation works by moving the cold side of the wall outwards. Anything that bridges from the cold, wet outer leaf to the inner leaf (wet insulant, mortar on a tie, rubble at the base) becomes the route by which water and cold reach the room.

### Thermal bridging at junctions

PAS 2035:2023 defines a thermal bridge as an area where the insulation is "discontinuous, thinner or has higher thermal conductivity than the adjacent insulation", or "has reduced effectiveness due to the building geometry", "leading to locally increased heat loss and therefore locally reduced internal surface temperature" (clause 3.36). Approved Document L paragraph 4.14 states the consequence: "Gaps in insulation can have a significant impact on heat loss and thermal bypass and create a risk of condensation and mould." The government's damp and mould guidance describes condensation damp as what "happens when moisture generated inside the home cools and condenses onto colder parts of the buildings" (updated 1 April 2026, "Understanding damp and mould").

For cavity walls, look where the fill may stop:
- **The base of the wall.** For new work, Approved Document L says cavity wall insulation "should extend below the dampproof course (where applicable) and be at least the equivalent of one full block height (215mm) below the underside of the floor structure/slab" (paragraph 4.17(f)(ii)). That is a new-build provision, but it shows where the bridge sits.
- **Openings.** "Insulated cavity closers should be used for all construction types" (4.17(h)(ii)).
- **The wall head.** Historic England notes problems "when the cavity is not closed off at the head and the insulation material connects into the loft space" (section 3.3).
- **Unfilled pockets.** Historic England warns of "leaving unfilled air pockets creating cold bridging to the inside walls which may attract condensation" (section 3.3).

For existing homes, Approved Document L paragraph 4.19 says "care should be taken to reduce unwanted heat loss through thermal bridging".

So two different problems can follow cavity fill. Water crossing from outside tends to follow the weather: worse after driving rain and on the elevation facing it. Condensation at a cold spot tends to follow the occupants and the season: worse in winter and in moister rooms, concentrated where the surface is coldest. These are this course's practical guide to what to look for, not tests.

## Section 2: Fabric First and the Whole-House Context

**In short:** PAS 2035:2023's fabric first order puts repair first: water penetration, damp, cracks and poor pointing are dealt with before insulation, and they are the conditions most often linked to cavity fill problems. The assessment asks for the location and severity of defects, condensation and mould, not their cause, so record well enough for the designer and coordinator to decide. On an EPC visit, if you see a possible problem, tell the client in writing and recommend professional advice.

### The PAS 2035 order of work

PAS 2035:2023 calls "fabric first" "a technically sound and usually cost-effective approach" which "should always be considered when a retrofit plan is formulated" (clause 5.5.1). Its five stages are:
- **(a)** "Bring the building fabric into good repair by dealing with defects that inhibit energy efficiency and compromise improvement measures. Deal with water penetration and damp (so that the building fabric is properly dried out) and with structural defects (e.g. cracks) and poor pointing of masonry."
- **(b)** Low-cost measures such as "energy efficient lighting, basic heating controls, better control settings".
- **(c)** Insulation and airtightness, "and by minimizing thermal bridging". "When the insulation and airtightness of the building fabric are improved, adequate ventilation should be maintained."
- **(d)** Efficient heating for the remaining demand.
- **(e)** Low and zero carbon technologies.

Stage (a) matters most here. Water penetration, cracks and poor pointing are named as things to deal with before insulation, and they are the conditions most often linked to cavity fill problems (Section 3).

PAS 2035:2023 asks that a dwelling be "treated as a system comprising the building envelope (or building fabric)", its services and its occupants (clause 4.2.3), and warns that retrofit work "might have unintended consequences for IAQ and for the movement of moisture through the building fabric" (clause 4.2.6). The design must "maintain the continuity of the three-dimensional insulated envelope" (clause 8.2.16) and include "provision for the management of moisture within the construction, in line with BS 5250" (clause 8.2.10).

### What the retrofit assessment records

Clause 7.3.2 of PAS 2035:2023 includes:
- "an appraisal of the dwelling's heritage, architectural features, structure, construction and condition" (b));
- "identification of the location and severity of any existing construction defects or structural defects or leaks, and of any condensation and/or mould growth in the dwelling" (d));
- "the main materials, thermal transmittances (U-values) and moisture properties of the main building elements" (g));
- "the relationship of elements of the thermal envelope to one another at corners, junctions and within openings" (h)).

Clause 7.4.2 requires defects to be reported in two categories: "a) defects that need to be repaired before any retrofit work can proceed; and b) defects that are recommended but not an essential prerequisite to retrofit."

Clause 7.3.2 d) asks for location and severity, not cause. It does not ask you to decide whether a wall is suitable for fill or why an existing fill has failed. Record well enough for the designer and coordinator to decide.

On an EPC visit, damp is not part of the rating, but if you see a possible problem, tell the client in writing and recommend professional advice.

## Section 3: Is the Wall Suitable? Exposure, Cavity and Condition

**In short:** Suitability depends mainly on exposure to driving rain and wall condition (Historic England); not yours to decide. Record setting, height and elevation of findings, and spalled brick, poor pointing or hard mortar on soft masonry. Course practice: if a borescope inspection is needed, look first; drill only if it appears safe. No cracks does not mean sound ties; a regular horizontal crack pattern or bulging is a referral trigger: do not drill; recommend a structural engineer before any cavity work.

### Why suitability comes first

The Welsh Government's written statement of 13 June 2017 reported that the BRE study for Wales "identified instances where the insulation was installed in unsuitable properties or contrary to good practice", and named "the quality of pre-installation assessments" among its concerns. It said: "The desire to keep costs down and the way potential work or 'leads' are incentivised are suggested as having led to installations in unsuitable properties". It listed, among the factors to assess before installation, the "construction and condition of external walls, their exposure to wind driven rain".

Historic England: "The suitability depends mainly on the local exposure to driving rain and the condition of the existing construction" (section 3.3). Approved Document C says of insulation inserted into an existing cavity: "The suitability of the wall for filling is to be assessed before the work is carried out" (paragraph 5.15(c)).

None of this makes you the person who decides suitability. It tells you what to notice and record.

### Exposure to wind-driven rain

The BEIS report says cavity wall insulation "has been long established as unsuitable for brick-finished cavity walls (i.e. not rendered or otherwise clad) in regions of high exposure to wind driven rain" (Introduction).

Approved Document C (England; the Welsh edition uses the same numbers) contains Diagram 12, "UK zones for exposure to driving rain", and Table 4, "Maximum recommended exposure zones for insulated masonry walls". Paragraph 5.15(a) says suitability for cavity insulation "should be determined either by reference to the map in Diagram 12 and the associated Table 4 or following the calculation or assessment procedure in current British or CEN standards". Table 4 could not be read for this course. The BEIS research appendix (Appendix A: WP2 – Wall analysis, by BRE) summarises it for injected fill:
- "For injected fill (excluding urea formaldehyde foam) a 50mm filled cavity would only be acceptable up to zone 2 – moderate,"
- "while 75-125mm filled cavities would be acceptable to zone 3 – severe."
- "Only filled cavities of 150mm or greater are deemed acceptable in zone 4, very severe environments."

The appendix gives these limits straight after a sentence about facing masonry walls with tooled joints, and does not say whether they apply to other outer leaf finishes. Read Table 4 itself before relying on any of these figures. Historic England adds that "many early cavity walls were built in areas with severe exposure to driving rain" (section 3.3).

Exposure is not only a map zone. Approved Document C notes that "A method of describing the exposure to wind-driven rain is given in BS 8104:1992" (paragraph 5.8, in its guidance on solid walls); BS 8104 was not opened for this course. For your record, note the setting (coastal, hilltop, open, sheltered urban), the building height, and which elevation each finding is on. The same house can have a sheltered front and an exposed gable.

### The cavity itself

Historic England says that in cheaper early cavity walls "two single skins of brickwork would often be separated by only a 50mm cavity", and that "it is not normally cost effective to install insulation in cavities of less than 50mm width" (sections 1 and 3.3). Early walls can be "susceptible to damp bridging the cavity through the insulation, even at widths greater than 50mm" (section 3.3). Some are not continuous cavities at all: "Where bricks are used as ties the cavity cannot be continuous" (section 1). Its advice is: "Before deciding to fill an early cavity, check the cavity size and establish whether there is any material within the cavity itself" (Figure 9).

Historic England describes "a thorough survey of the cavity with a borescope" (section 3.3). You may also record overall wall thickness at a reveal.

**If your work calls for a borescope inspection.** Some jobs may call for a drilled inspection, for example to show that a cavity is already filled. This course's recommended practice, not a legal or scheme requirement: before you drill, take time over a careful visual inspection of the outer leaf from the ground and of the inside face. Look for the signs in this section and the next: a regular horizontal crack pattern, bulging, spalled or cracked brick at the drilling position, damp linked to the wall, or signs of failed or wet fill. Only drill and inspect if, having looked, it appears a safe course of action. A clear visual check does not show that the ties are sound. If any of those signs are present, do not drill: record what you see and recommend professional advice as set out in the referral triggers in Section 5 (a structural engineer or building surveyor for cracking or bulging). Follow your scheme's and your organisation's procedure for the inspection itself.

### Brickwork, pointing and render

Historic England:
- "The condition of the existing wall is critical as the addition of insulation to a wall in poor condition not only exacerbates the situation but may well hamper later repairs" (section 3.3).
- "Cavity walls with widespread spalling to the face-work should not be filled" (section 3.3).
- "All mortar joints should be full and the pointing in good repair" (section 3.3).
- Problems arise "particularly where soft masonry walls have been re-pointed using inappropriately hard mortars" (section 4.3).

The BRE field data support this. The BEIS appendix reports that in 2015 "BRE visited over 1300 dwellings whilst undertaking a cavity wall insulation review on behalf of Constructing Excellence Wales", "the most comprehensive independent investigation into failed cavity wall dwellings to date". Rendered dwellings were excluded, "leaving 390 properties", and the surveyors recorded the primary condition that had led to failure in each (section 2.3.5, Table 11, "Observed ABIS conditions in house surveys (2015)"; the table also gives a crack size for most rows, shown here only where it separates two rows):

| Primary condition | Failure mechanism (as recorded) | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Rubble in base of cavity | Water unable to escape cavity | 79 |
| Damaged brick face (crack size 2 to 3 mm) | Water bypasses brickwork | 71 |
| Saturation over 25% (common brick) | Water penetrates through brick | 67 |
| Damaged brick face (crack size 1 to 2 mm) | Water bypasses brickwork | 52 |
| Wall tie failure | Water penetrates gaps in mortar | 52 |
| Pointing failure | Water penetrates gaps in mortar | 21 |
| Saturated mortar behind new pointing, or cement pointing over lime | Water penetrates through mortar | 21 |
| Subsidence | Water penetrates gaps in mortar | 14 |
| No cavity closer around windows | Water bypasses brickwork | 13 |
| **Total** | | **390** |

The appendix says "Damaged brick faces alone represent almost a third of all cited primary ABIS conditions" (ABIS meaning "as built in service"). The passage read describes the dwellings as "failed cavity wall dwellings"; it does not say in that passage that every one had been filled.

When the BEIS team looked for exposed brick-faced homes in good enough condition to test waterproofing treatments, 100 were surveyed externally and 50 of those internally, and "all were found to have problems requiring fundamental repairs" (section 2.8).

### Wall ties before filling

Historic England notes that early metal ties "are liable to corrosion if the right damp conditions persist, particularly if the ties were embedded in an acidic mortar such as one containing ash" (section 1), and: "If the cracks are due to wall tie corrosion it must be remedied first as cavity insulation will only make the situation worse" (section 3.3). The BEIS appendix says "rust is the primary mechanism of failure of ties", that rusting "can lead to spalling of surrounding mortar", and that ties fouled with mortar droppings can "collect and concentrate moisture across the tie and deeper into the insulation layer if the cavity is filled" (p. 23).

LABC Warranty's technical update "Wall Tie Replacement in Existing Buildings" (written December 2020) describes what may show outside: "a pattern of horizontal cracks coinciding with the tie positions approximately every six courses"; with fishtail and vertical twist ties, "a visible splitting of the mortar bed"; and "Bulging to external cavity walls can also be an indication of failure". It warns that wire butterfly ties can "corrode right through without producing any evidence on the outside of the building", and that where stability is threatened "a suitably qualified Structural Engineer must be appointed to determine the necessary remedial works".

So no cracks does not mean sound ties. A regular horizontal crack pattern or bulging is a referral trigger: record it by elevation, photograph it, and recommend advice from a structural engineer before any cavity work.

## Section 4: How Cavity Fill Goes In, and What Goes Wrong

**In short:** Problems after filling may show from the room or the ground: damp after wind-driven rain on an exposed elevation, a low-level band, localised mould where the fill may have gaps, blocked or unsafeguarded vents, and escaped insulation. Most patterns have a twin with a different cause, so record both possibilities and treat patterns as reasons to look closely and refer, not proof. A blocked combustion air vent or a flue concern goes through your organisation's gas safety procedure.

### Installation types in general terms

Historic England names three generic types: "mineral wool", "beads or granules" and "foamed insulation" (section 3.3). Existing cavities are filled through holes drilled in the outer leaf, which "still necessitates drilling a considerable number of reasonably large holes in the brick or stone façade" (section 3.3). It notes that "services, ventilation ducts and flues need to be sleeved" (section 3.4).

The sources note differences:
- Moisture transfer is "less likely with EPS bead insulation than blown mineral wool insulation" because EPS "is non-absorptive" (BEIS appendix, p. 22).
- "foams can crack under shrinkage stresses giving pathways for moisture transfer", and "Mineral-fibre can hold moisture against vulnerable elements such as built in timber lintels" (Historic England, section 3.5).

You do not need to identify the material. If you can see it at a vent, weep hole or the wall head, describe it ("loose white beads at wall head in loft") rather than naming a product.

### What CIGA's own data pointed to

In written evidence to the House of Commons Energy and Climate Change Committee (October 2015), CIGA listed items "which CIGA's data shows can lead to problems with cavity wall insulation being reported":
- "Cavity vents correctly sealed;"
- "Underfloor vents safeguarded;"
- "Combustion vents safeguarded;"
- "Any evidence of injection points in close proximity to combustion flues;"
- "Fitment of room vents where required;"
- "Evidence of escape of insulation;"
- "Nature of surroundings and ground level in relation to DPC;"
- "Sheds, buildings or plantings abutting treated elevations;"
- "Omitted areas;"
- "Existing evidence of damp or condensation issues."

The list is ten years old, but nearly every item is visible from the room or the ground.

### The common failure patterns

These describe what you may see and the possible explanation each points to. They are reasons to look closely and refer, not proof.

**Rain bridging on exposed elevations.** Damp patches on the inside face of an external wall, worse after wind-driven rain, on the elevation facing the weather. The sources link this to exposure and the outer leaf's condition: damaged brick faces, failed pointing, hard pointing over soft masonry. Record the elevation, recent weather if known, and the outer face as seen from the ground.

**Debris at the cavity base bridging the damp-proof course.** A damp band at low level on the inside of an external wall. Historic England: "If the cavity is bridged in any way, either with mortar rubble or through stones", filling it "will also encourage the passage of damp" (section 3.5). The BEIS appendix: "The presence of debris that may bridge a cavity is considered an unreasonable risk of failure", and in the survey guidance it reviewed "no specific mention is made of inspecting the base of the cavity" (p. 23). Rubble at the base was the most frequent single condition in the Welsh data (79 of 390). A low-level band can also come from rising damp, a leak or raised ground levels (MER-ALL-018). Record it as "low-level damp band, cause not established".

**Ground level and abutments.** CIGA listed ground level "in relation to DPC" and anything "abutting treated elevations". Record paving or soil that looks high against the wall, and anything built or growing against it.

**Voids and omitted areas.** Localised mould near openings, at the top of the wall, or on a section the householder says was never drilled (an extension, a bay, a gable above a lower roof). Historic England's "unfilled air pockets" and CIGA's "Omitted areas" describe the cause. You will rarely see a void; you may see the mould pattern it produces, or drill holes that stop short of part of the wall.

**Blocked or unsafeguarded ventilation.** Air bricks to a suspended timber floor that are blocked or show insulation in them; room or combustion air vents blocked; injection holes close to a flue. A blocked combustion air vent or a flue concern is a gas safety matter: follow your organisation's gas safety procedure. A blocked underfloor vent is a reason to recommend professional advice on the floor void; do not lift floorboards.

**Unsuitable walls.** Narrow early cavities, widespread spalling, exposed fair-faced brick in severe exposure. You record the evidence (age band, construction, exposure, condition). You do not decide suitability, and you do not tell a householder their wall was "unsuitable".

**Escaped insulation.** Beads or fibre at weep holes, air bricks or in the loft at the wall head.

### Twins to rule out

Most patterns have a twin that looks similar and has a different cause. Record both possibilities.

| What you see on the inside face | Possible explanation linked to cavity fill | Twin that can look the same |
|---|---|---|
| Damp patch on one elevation, worse after rain | Rain crossing the filled cavity on an exposed elevation | Defective gutter, downpipe, sill or window seal on that elevation |
| Low-level band along an external wall | Debris at the cavity base bridging the damp-proof course | Rising damp; raised ground level; leak at floor level |
| Localised mould near a window or at the wall head | Void or omitted area in the fill; missing closer | Condensation at a cold reveal or eaves junction |
| Damp along a horizontal line | Moisture tracking along mortar-fouled ties | Corroded ties with cracking (structural); failed cavity tray over an opening |
| Damp patch below a window | Bridging at the sill or cavity closer | Condensation running off the glass; leaking frame |

### Timing

LABC's summary of the BRE report for Wales says "all of the cavity wall failures occurred within the timeframe of the guarantee provision", "in some circumstances in less than 10 years". Record when the householder says the fill went in and when the damp started, as they report it; it matters for the guarantee route in Section 6.

## Section 5: Recognise, Record, Refer

**In short:** You recognise and record; you do not state that a wall was unsuitable, that an installation is defective or that insulation must be removed. Use "possible", "consistent with" and "cause not established", and photograph each finding with the date, time and weather. Never tell a householder to open up walls or have the insulation extracted. The referral triggers and traffic light are this course's recommended practice, not legal requirements or a published scheme.

### What to record

You recognise and record. You do not state that a wall was unsuitable, that an installation is defective, or that insulation must be removed. Use "possible", "likely", "consistent with" and "cause not established".

A good record for a filled cavity wall covers:
- **Evidence of fill:** drill-hole pattern by elevation, areas without holes, visible material at vents or the wall head, and the householder's account of when and under what scheme it was installed ("householder reports").
- **Exposure context:** setting, height, and the elevation of each finding.
- **Outer leaf from the ground:** spalled brick faces, open or hard pointing, cracks (noting any regular horizontal pattern), bulging, render condition.
- **Ground and abutments:** ground levels, anything against the wall, covered air bricks.
- **Inside face:** location, extent and pattern of damp or mould, by room and elevation, with approximate dimensions.
- **Ventilation and combustion:** extract, trickle, room and combustion vents and whether open; any flue on a filled wall.
- **Limits:** rooms, elevations or loft not seen.

Photograph each elevation and each finding with a wider context shot, and note the date, time and weather.

### Never tell a householder to open up walls

Do not tell a householder or landlord to drill the wall, remove bricks, strip plaster, lift floors, or have the insulation extracted. The Welsh Government guide states: "Extraction of the CWI may not always be the correct remedy", and recommends advice from a building surveyor. Remedies are for the installer, the guarantee provider or a qualified professional to decide.

### Referral triggers

This course's recommended practice, not legal requirements:
- **Structural:** a regular horizontal crack pattern, bulging, significant cracking or signs of subsidence. Recommend a structural engineer or building surveyor before any cavity work.
- **Gas and combustion:** a blocked combustion air vent, a flue concern, or any smell of gas. Follow your organisation's gas safety procedure and the emergency arrangements in force.
- **Electrical:** water near electrical fittings. Recommend urgent advice from a qualified electrician; tell the occupant not to use affected fittings.
- **Active water ingress:** tell the occupant and, if rented, the landlord, promptly.
- **Damp or mould on a filled wall:** recommend the householder contacts the installer first, then the guarantee provider (Section 6).
- **Proposed fill where you have recorded exposure, condition or tie concerns:** record them as defects under PAS 2035:2023 clause 7.4.2 for the designer and coordinator to categorise.

### Traffic light

A way to present findings, recommended by this course rather than a published scheme:

| Light | Examples | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Green: monitor | Even drill pattern, sound pointing and brick faces, no internal damp or mould, vents open | Record as seen |
| Amber: investigate | Isolated mould at a reveal; open pointing or a few spalled bricks on an exposed elevation; air brick partly covered; householder reports damp began after filling | Record, photograph, recommend the installer or guarantee provider, or a building surveyor |
| Red: get professional advice | Horizontal crack pattern or bulging; damp near electrics; blocked combustion vent or flue concern; extensive damp on an exposed filled elevation; active water ingress | Record, tell the occupant, recommend the relevant professional now, in writing |

### A worked record

A 1950s semi-detached house, filled cavity, coastal, January visit:

"Householder reports cavity wall insulation installed approx. 2014; paperwork not seen. Drill holes in a regular pattern on front and west gable; none seen on single-storey rear extension. Gable: open, eroded mortar joints over approx. 4 m² at first-floor level; several spalled brick faces; no horizontal crack pattern seen from ground level. Inside, first-floor bedroom on gable: damp staining approx. 600 × 900 mm below window, mould spotting at window head; householder says worse after storms. Rear extension: mould along skirting of external wall, worst in corner. One front air brick partly covered by a raised planter. Gable findings consistent with possible rain penetration on an exposed elevation; extension mould consistent with a possible cold area; causes not established. Householder advised to contact the installer first and, if needed, the guarantee provider. Building surveyor's advice recommended on the gable masonry before further wall work. Gable masonry recorded as a defect under PAS 2035:2023 clause 7.4.2."

It does not say "the fill has failed", "the wall was unsuitable" or "the insulation needs extracting". It locates, describes, uses "possible", and refers.

## Section 6: Remediation Routes

**In short:** You signpost; you do not judge the installation, the claim or the remedy. The installer comes first; if it has gone out of business or not addressed the concern, the guarantee provider. CIGA is the route only where CIGA issued the guarantee; do not tell a householder they have one unless shown it. On the sources opened, TrustMark's free Find & Fix audit is for ECO4 or GBIS solid wall insulation and does not mention cavity walls.

You signpost. You do not judge whether an installation is defective, whether a claim will succeed, or what the remedy should be.

### Step 1: the installer

The Welsh Government guide (A Consumer Guide to issues arising from Cavity Wall Insulation (CWI) installations in Welsh properties, 7 February 2022) says: "Contact the installer who undertook the installation in the first instance", and that the installer should then investigate and put right any defects. The BEIS guide (Cavity wall insulation (CWI): consumer guide to issues arising on installation, October 2019) uses the same words. Ofgem's page on complaints about cavity wall insulation installed under ECO (published 6 March 2018) says: "Contact the installer responsible for your work."

### Step 2: the guarantee provider

If the installer "has gone out of business or has not addressed your concerns", the Welsh Government guide says to contact the guarantee provider.

**What CIGA is.** In its 2015 evidence to Parliament, CIGA said it "was established by the industry in 1995" and "acts as a guarantor for 25 years for cavity wall insulation fitted by registered installers". Where a guarantee holder "experiences problems with their cavity wall insulation that are due to workmanship or materials", it "will work with the installer, or step in ourselves where that isn't possible". CIGA's website now says it "provides independent 25 year guarantees for Cavity Wall Insulation fitted by registered installers in the UK and Channel islands". Its "How to Report a Concern" page also starts with the installer, says a householder can go to CIGA if the concern is unresolved after 41 working days, and says that to search its guarantee database "We simply need the property address and postcode". The guarantee document itself was not read for this course.

**Where CIGA applies.** The Welsh Government guide says insulation installed under Welsh and UK Government schemes after 2013 has required "an appropriate 25 year guarantee to be issued on completion of the work", that CIGA holds a "large percentage" of the guarantees for Welsh homes, and that since 2012 "a number of insurance backed guarantees have been issued", so a householder not on CIGA's records should check other providers. The BEIS guide says CIGA's support is "only available for consumers with a CIGA guarantee". Ofgem says that if the guarantee is lost and the measure was installed before April 2015, the householder "can contact all the guarantee agencies covering cavity wall insulation and ask them if they have a record of a guarantee covering your address", and that "Ofgem no longer administers appropriate guarantees or financial protection".

CIGA is the route only where CIGA issued the guarantee. Do not tell a householder they have a CIGA guarantee unless they have shown you one.

**Scale in Wales.** The Welsh Government statement of 13 June 2017: "The Cavity Insulation Guarantee Agency, CIGA, has issued just over 300,000 guarantees in Wales. Their data indicates there have been just over 2,000 claims." It recorded CIGA's commitment to the "Introduction of a requirement for an independent and properly qualified surveillance assessment for proposed installations".

### Step 3: disputed claims and no guarantee

The Welsh Government guide says that for CIGA guarantees "the arbitration dispute process can be entered into a point when a deadlock letter has been issued", and that for insurance-backed guarantees "redress can be sought through the Financial Services Ombudsmen". CIGA's concern page says a householder unhappy with the final decision of the installer or CIGA can refer the dispute to independent arbitration, run by the Centre for Effective Dispute Resolution, whose decision "will be final and legally binding on both parties". Without a valid guarantee, the householder may have to pay for remedial work; the guide recommends a building surveyor's advice and mentions the Welsh Government's home improvement loan (Wales only).

### Other routes

- **Ofgem, for ECO-funded work.** Both government guides give eco@ofgem.gov.uk for complaints about the standard of ECO-installed cavity wall insulation. Ofgem's page is clear about its limits: "We do not regulate the insulation industry." The energy supplier that funded the work may help with information, but "We cannot guarantee, however, that the energy supplier will undertake remedial works."
- **TrustMark, for registered measures.** Ofgem: "If the cavity wall insulation measure was handed over to the consumer after 1 January 2020 and the measure was TrustMark registered, then TrustMark may be able to help you if things go wrong." That is a complaint route, not a free audit.
- **Trading Standards and Citizens Advice.** Both guides give Trading Standards as 03454 040506. Ofgem gives the Citizens Advice consumer helpline as 0808 223 1133 (0808 223 1144 for the Welsh-language service).

### The free audit does not extend to cavity walls

Householders may have heard that faulty insulation is being checked and fixed for free. On the sources opened for this course, that programme is for solid wall insulation:
- TrustMark's "Free Find & Fix Audit Offer" page (updated 23 October 2026) describes a "Free external wall insulation audit for eligible homes under government funded schemes" for homes that "have had work done under ECO4 or GBIS". It does not mention cavity walls.
- Citizens Advice's "Get help with faulty solid wall insulation" says "This is for wall insulation installed inside and outside your home" where "the work on your solid wall insulation was carried out between April 2022 and January 2025" under ECO4 or GBIS. It does not mention cavity walls.
- The National Audit Office report "Energy efficiency installations under the Energy Company Obligation" (HC 1334, 14 October 2025) mentions cavity wall insulation only among "Other ECO4 and GBIS measures". It reports that DESNZ found "212 of the 910 additional measures installed alongside external and internal wall insulation" had major issues (23%), a figure for all additional measures combined, and says "It is not possible to extrapolate these results to all installations under ECO" (Summary, paragraph 17).

So a householder with a cavity wall problem should use the installer, guarantee and complaint routes above. TrustMark decides eligibility for its own programmes; if unsure, the householder should ask TrustMark.

### Claims management companies

The Welsh Government guide says "Most CWI has been completed in suitable homes to good standards" and warns about companies offering to claim compensation. If a householder asks, point them to the guide; do not comment on whether they have a claim.

## Key Takeaways

- A U-value is heat loss rate per square metre per degree of temperature difference (BR 443). On the RdSAP 10 England defaults, filling a pre-1976 cavity takes the wall from 1.5 to 0.7 W/m²K, roughly halving wall heat loss.
- Filling a cavity moves the cold, wet side outwards and connects the two leaves; it "introduces the risk that moisture will be able to find its way across to the inside leaf" (Historic England).
- Gaps in insulation "create a risk of condensation and mould" (Approved Document L, 4.14). Look at the cavity base, openings, the wall head and unfilled areas.
- PAS 2035:2023 puts repair first: water penetration, damp, cracks and poor pointing before insulation (clause 5.5.1(a)).
- Suitability depends mainly on exposure to driving rain and wall condition (Historic England). In the Welsh BRE data, rubble at the cavity base (79 of 390) and damaged brick faces (123 across two rows) led the primary conditions.
- A regular horizontal crack pattern or bulging is a structural referral trigger; no cracks does not mean sound ties.
- Write "possible" and "cause not established". Never tell a householder to open up a wall or have insulation extracted.
- The route is installer, guarantee provider (CIGA only where it issued the guarantee), then arbitration or ombudsman. The published free TrustMark audit covers solid wall insulation under ECO4 or GBIS, not cavity walls.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** Using the RdSAP 10 England defaults, an age band C cavity wall of 80 m² is filled. With a 20 K temperature difference, what is the change in the wall heat loss rate?

A) From 2,400 W to 1,120 W
B) From 1,500 W to 700 W
C) From 2,400 W to 880 W
D) No change, because RdSAP treats filled and unfilled cavities alike

**Correct answer: A.** Heat loss rate = U × area × temperature difference: 1.5 × 80 × 20 = 2,400 W as built, and 0.7 × 80 × 20 = 1,120 W filled (Section 1).

---

**Q2.** Under PAS 2035:2023 clause 5.5.1, what is the first stage of the "fabric first" approach?

A) Install insulation and airtightness measures
B) Fit low and zero carbon technologies
C) Bring the building fabric into good repair, dealing with water penetration, damp, structural defects and poor pointing
D) Upgrade heating controls and lighting

**Correct answer: C.** Stage (a) is repair: "Deal with water penetration and damp ... and with structural defects (e.g. cracks) and poor pointing of masonry" (Section 2).

---

**Q3.** In the BRE survey data for Wales reanalysed in BEIS Research Paper 2021/017, which of these conditions was recorded most often as the primary condition among the 390 non-rendered properties?

A) Wall tie failure
B) Rubble in the base of the cavity
C) Subsidence
D) No cavity closer around windows

**Correct answer: B.** Rubble in the base of the cavity was recorded in 79 properties. Damaged brick faces totalled more (123), but across two crack-size rows (Section 3).

---

**Q4.** On a filled cavity wall you see horizontal cracks in the outer leaf roughly every six courses, with slight bulging. What is the appropriate response?

A) Record it as a cosmetic pointing defect
B) Tell the householder the ties have failed and the insulation must be extracted
C) Record and photograph it by elevation, and recommend advice from a structural engineer or building surveyor before any further wall work
D) Drill a test hole to inspect the ties

**Correct answer: C.** LABC Warranty describes this crack pattern as a possible sign of tie corrosion, and bulging as a possible indication of failure. It is a structural referral trigger; the assessor records and refers, and does not name a cause or open up the wall (Sections 3 and 5).

---

**Q5.** A householder in Wales reports damp on a filled gable wall and asks whether the free TrustMark audit will check their cavity wall insulation. Which response matches the sources opened for this course?

A) Yes, the free audit covers all wall insulation installed under any scheme
B) Contact the installer first, then the guarantee provider; the free audit described by TrustMark and Citizens Advice is for solid wall insulation under ECO4 or GBIS, and TrustMark decides eligibility
C) Contact Ofgem, which regulates insulation installers
D) Have the insulation extracted and then make a claim

**Correct answer: B.** Both government guides start with the installer, then the guarantee provider. The free audit sources mention only solid wall insulation, and Ofgem says "We do not regulate the insulation industry" (Section 6).
