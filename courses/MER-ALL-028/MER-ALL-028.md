## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- Recognise solid, suspended timber and suspended concrete ground floors on site, and name the moisture defences each relies on: damp-proof membrane, damp-proof course, ventilated void and ventilation openings.
- Record the condition of those defences, and any evidence of moisture or timber decay, without naming a cause.
- Work the Approved Document C sub-floor ventilation rule for a real floor, and explain why the answer is for the designer to settle, not a pass or fail for the assessor.
- Explain what insulating or sealing a ground floor changes: sub-floor ventilation, room ventilation, perimeter cold spots, gas pipes, radon and traditional moisture-open floors.
- Report floor defects in the two PAS 2035:2023 categories and refer timber, damp, radon and safety findings to the right person.

## Introduction

Part 1 of this series (MER-ALL-018, *Damp, Condensation and Thermal Bridging*) covered where moisture comes from, how condensation works, the twins (condensation, penetrating damp, rising damp and leaks), meter limits, and the floor-perimeter mould pattern. This part does not repeat that. It takes one element, the ground floor, and asks what keeps it dry, what you can see of that on a visit, and what changes when someone insulates or seals it.

Floors are easy to under-record. The membrane is buried and the void is out of sight, so the evidence is at the edges: air bricks, ground levels, skirtings, thresholds and the feel underfoot. This course is about reading those edges and recording them so the designer and coordinator can decide what happens next.

The assessor recognises, records and refers. You do not lift floors, open up, probe timber or name a cause. Where something needs investigating, the recommendation is professional advice.

## Section 1: How a Ground Floor Keeps Dry

**In short:** Approved Document C describes a solid floor kept dry by a damp-proof membrane continuous with the wall damp-proof course. A suspended timber floor relies on three things: a covered ground, a ventilated void under the timber, and damp-proof courses between timber and anything carrying ground moisture. A suspended concrete floor needs a ventilated void and, where the ground below is excavated and undrained, a membrane. Most of these defences are hidden, so record what shows at the edges.

### Three floor types

You will meet three types: solid (concrete on hardcore, or in older homes flagstones, tiles, lime or earth on the ground); suspended timber (boards on joists, wall plates and sleeper walls over a ventilated void); and suspended concrete, including beam and block. Where construction is unknown, RdSAP 10 assumes suspended timber for age bands A and B and solid from band C (specification, Table 19). That is a calculation default, not a fact about any house; MER-DEA-008 covers data entry.

### What Approved Document C describes

They describe what the defences are meant to be, which is what you look for evidence of.

| Floor type | Defence | Approved Document C (England 2013 amendments; Wales 2010 amendments) |
|---|---|---|
| Solid | Concrete on hardcore with a damp-proof membrane | 4.6: "dense concrete laid on a hardcore bed and a dampproof membrane is provided" |
| Solid | Hardcore, concrete, membrane continuous with wall damp-proof courses | 4.7: hardcore "no greater than 600mm deep"; concrete "at least 100mm thick"; membrane "continuous with the dampproof courses in walls" |
| Solid | Polyethylene membrane | 4.8: "at least 300µm thick (1200 gauge) with sealed joints" (same wording, Wales 4.8) |
| Suspended timber | Three conditions | 4.13: ground covered "to resist moisture and prevent plant growth"; "a ventilated air space"; damp-proof courses between timber and moisture-carrying material |
| Suspended timber | Void depth | 4.14(b): at least 75mm to the underside of wall plates and at least 150mm to the underside of the floor "(or insulation if provided)" (same wording, Wales 4.14(b)) |
| Suspended timber | Ventilation openings | 4.14(b): "Two opposing external walls should have ventilation openings"; size rule in Section 3 |
| Suspended concrete | Void and membrane | 4.19: membrane if the ground was excavated below surrounding ground "and will not be effectively drained"; void "at least 150mm clear" |
| All floors | Surface condensation | 4.5: floors "should not promote surface condensation or mould growth"; 4.22: U-value not over 0.7 W/m²K "at any point" |
| Walls | Damp-proof course height | 5.5(b) (England): "at least 150mm above the level of the adjoining ground", unless part of the building protects the wall |

Two more details: "To prevent water collecting on the ground covering", its top should be above the highest adjoining ground, or up-slope drainage considered on sloping sites (4.14); and where flooding is likely, a means of "inspecting and clearing out the sub-floor voids" may be considered (4.20).

### What you can and cannot see

You cannot see a membrane under a finished floor, and you should not try. You can see:
- the external ground level against each wall, and any paths, patios, ramps or planting beds;
- the damp-proof course line, if visible, and its height above the ground;
- air bricks or vents: how many, on which walls, their condition and whether anything covers them;
- extensions, conservatories or porches built across walls that once had vents;
- inside, the floor finish, the feel underfoot, skirtings, thresholds and any visible staining or mould at the base of walls.

If a void is visible through an access point already open, record what you see from where you stand. Do not lift boards, coverings or panels. Where you cannot see something, write "not inspected" and why.

## Section 2: Solid Floors: What You Can See and What You Cannot

**In short:** A modern solid floor has a hidden membrane; a traditional one may be flagstones, lime or earth laid on soil, a moisture-open construction. The BEIS solid floor guide says damp or mould on floors or lower walls "may be indicative" of rising damp, and doubts should go to a specialist damp survey. Record the floor finish, external ground levels, thresholds and fixtures near floor level, and record unknowns as unknown.

### Modern and traditional build-ups

The BEIS guide *Solid floor insulation: guide to best practice* (June 2022), cited in PAS 2035:2023 clause 15.1.3, describes a typical modern floor as "150mm of compacted hardcore, 50mm of sand, a polythene sheet DPM, 100mm of concrete", with "around 100mm of insulation and a 60mm screed" (Annex A, p. 38). Older floors are different: "lime-based ground floor slabs, flagged-stone or tiles laid straight onto soil" (Annex A, p. 37).

The difference matters for moisture. The guide notes that "some solid floors may be of compacted earth or lime screed which is a moisture open construction" (Section 7, p. 24). It also says "Traditional (and typically moisture open) floors can sometimes be contributing to the diffusion of moisture in walls" (Section 3, p. 10), and that other floors "may be moisture open and contributing to damp problems in the building" (Section 5, Table 1, p. 16).

### What the guide asks the survey to look at

The guide's Table 1 is written for whoever assesses a floor for insulation. These rows are worth recording:
- **Damp signs.** "Damp, mould growth or condensation on the floors or lower walls may be indicative of rising damp", and "Any doubts arising from the visual inspection should be followed by a damp survey from a specialist" (p. 15).
- **Ground levels.** "High external ground levels may prevent solid floors from draining properly" (p. 14).
- **Moisture balance.** The risk named is "Potential for solid floor insulation to upset the moisture balance of the existing building" (p. 16).
- **Thresholds.** "Record all door thresholds on ground floor and height of these above floor level" (p. 17).
- **Fixtures.** "Record any permanent fixtures that are within 200mm of the current finished floor level", such as radiators, sockets and pipework (pp. 17 to 18).
- **Foundations.** "Often in older properties there are very shallow foundations and digging down could undermine the wall" (p. 18).
- **Radon.** "If high levels of radon are evident, take expert advice" (p. 15).

"May be indicative" signals a possibility, not a finding, and doubts go to a specialist. That matches Part 1: the government's damp and mould guidance says rising damp "is often misdiagnosed" and that "chemical testing is the most appropriate way of confirming it" ("Understanding damp and mould").

### Recording a solid floor

Your record can say a great deal without anything being lifted:
- **Finish.** "Quarry tiles laid directly, no screed edge visible at the threshold"; "carpet throughout, construction not seen". The finish is a clue, not proof.
- **Edges and outside.** Staining, salts or mould at the base of walls, with height and length; ground and path levels against each wall relative to the floor or a visible damp-proof course line.
- **Thresholds and services.** Threshold heights, and pipes or sockets near floor level.
- **Unknowns.** If the floor could not be assessed, "all available evidence should be retained" to show why (Section 1, p. 7).

A householder may ask whether a new membrane is needed. The guide's design principles say a membrane "should be introduced on the existing solid floor" before insulation, "lapped up the walls to overlap the damp proof course" (Section 7, p. 24). For moisture-open traditional floors it asks instead for "careful consideration" of insulation, thickness and finish, and a moisture strategy that complements the existing one. Both are design decisions. Your answer is that the designer will consider it.

## Section 3: Suspended Timber Floors and the Sub-floor Void

**In short:** A suspended timber floor depends on air crossing the void. The BEIS guide says durability is "in most circumstances totally dependent" on adequate cross-flow ventilation. Air bricks get blocked by raised ground, paths, decking, ramps and extensions. Approved Document C sets opening areas by the greater of two rules, but does not say which wall run the perimeter rule uses. Record vents and obstructions; leave the arithmetic to the designer.

### Why the void matters

The BEIS *Guide to Best Practice: Retrofit Floor Insulation, Suspended Timber Floors* (July 2020), PAS 2035:2023 reference [16], puts it plainly: "The durability of suspended timber floors is in most circumstances totally dependent on there being adequate and suitable cross-flow ventilation to the void below." Ventilation should be "effective and unobstructed by any new materials, debris, or other detritus material" (para 53, p. 24).

Historic England's *Insulating Suspended Timber Floors* (HEAG086, v1.1, April 2016) adds that "Cross-ventilation of the space is necessary to prevent any air stagnation" and that sub-floor ventilation is also "a significant source of heat loss from rooms above, particularly if the boarding is butt-jointed with open gaps" (§1.4, p. 5). Damp-proof courses under wall plates may be absent in earlier construction, in which case "the only protection on offer" is the ventilation (§1.4).

### What blocks the air

The BEIS guide asks for an inspection "of the floor levels externally in relation to the internal finished floor level", because later pavements, decking, landscaping or ramps may obstruct air bricks (para 54). Historic England says "Air bricks and other vents are often easily obstructed by raised external ground levels" (§2.3, p. 7), and that new air bricks or vents may be needed where an extension has cut off the ventilation paths (§2.3; BEIS para 57). The government's damp and mould guidance says "Existing air bricks should be kept clear and free of debris and obstruction" ("Ventilation checklist").

What you might see: air bricks level with or below a path, patio or bed; vents painted over, filled or covered by decking or a bin store; vents on one wall only, or an extension across the line between them; broken grilles (Approved Document C 4.14(b) wants grilles that keep vermin out but "not resist the air flow unduly").

Record what you see and where. Do not recommend clearing, digging or new vents: the guide's responses (reducing raised soil, periscope vents, new air bricks) are for the installer and designer to specify (paras 54 and 57).

### Board gaps and the rooms above

Where a floor has been in stable condition, "these stable conditions may have been helped by ventilation through board gaps to the room above", so adding insulation "may significantly obstruct this ventilation", and new ventilation paths below the floor may be needed (para 59). The same air movement is part of how the house is ventilated, which Section 5 returns to.

### Working the opening area

Approved Document C 4.14(b) says openings "should be not less than either 1,500mm²/m run of external wall or 500mm²/m² of floor area, whichever gives the greater opening area", with any pipes carrying ventilating air at least 100 mm in diameter. Paragraph 4.19 repeats the rule for suspended concrete floors. The BEIS guide takes Part C 2013 as "the default requirement" for sub-floor voids (para 55).

The paragraph does not say whether "run of external wall" means the two opposing walls that carry the openings or every external wall around the void. The two readings give different answers.

> Calculated, floor-area rule (500 mm² per m²): mid-terrace 5 × 8 m, 40 m² → 20,000 mm²; semi 6 × 8 m, 48 m² → 24,000 mm²; detached 8 × 8 m, 64 m² → 32,000 mm²

> Calculated, wall-run rule (1,500 mm² per m), opposing walls with openings only: mid-terrace 2 × 5 m = 10 m → 15,000 mm²; semi 2 × 6 m = 12 m → 18,000 mm²; detached 2 × 8 m = 16 m → 24,000 mm²

> Calculated, wall-run rule, all external walls around the void: mid-terrace 10 m → 15,000 mm²; semi 20 m (front, rear and side) → 30,000 mm²; detached 32 m → 48,000 mm²

On the first reading the floor-area rule governs all three floors. On the second it still governs the mid-terrace, but the wall-run rule governs the semi (30,000 mm²) and the detached house (48,000 mm²). The source does not settle it, so you should not either.

RdSAP's floor U-value method assumes ventilation openings of 0.003 m² per m of exposed perimeter (specification, section 5.12): 60,000 mm² for the semi (calculated). That is a model input, not a requirement or a test of any floor.

No opened source gives the free area of a typical air brick, so this course does not convert any figure into a number of bricks. Record the number, size, position and condition of vents, and any legible marked free area.

## Section 4: Telling Floor Moisture Apart

**In short:** Damp or mould at the base of a ground-floor wall can come from condensation at a cold floor edge, possible rising damp, ground or paths bridging the damp-proof course, a leaking pipe, or a moisture-open traditional floor. Several can occur together. Part 1's twins apply; this section adds the floor-specific clues you can record and the observation that would point away from each. None of them settles the cause.

### Five candidates at the skirting

Part 1 (MER-ALL-018, Section 4) set out the four kinds of damp the government guidance names. At the base of a ground-floor wall the floor adds its own routes, giving five candidates:
1. **Condensation at the floor-wall junction.** Part 1, Section 3 described the floor perimeter as a thermal bridge; Approved Document C 4.5 asks that floors "should not promote surface condensation or mould growth".
2. **Possible rising damp.** Moisture "from the ground that rises up through parts of the buildings in contact with the ground" (guidance); floor and lower-wall damp "may be indicative" of it (BEIS 2022).
3. **Ground or paths bridging the damp-proof course.** The guidance's external checks include a "bridged (compromised) damp proof course"; Approved Document C 5.5(b) (England) puts it at least 150 mm above ground.
4. **A leak.** Traumatic damp includes "leaking water from waste and heating pipes" (guidance); void pipes can freeze, with "subsequent leakage or damage" (BEIS 2020, para 92).
5. **A moisture-open traditional floor.** Such floors "can sometimes be contributing to the diffusion of moisture in walls" (BEIS 2022, Section 3, p. 10).

### What you can record for each

The candidates are sourced as above; the other columns are this course's indicators to record, not tests.

| Candidate | Where it tends to show | What you can record | What would point away from it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Condensation at floor edge | External walls at skirting, corners, behind furniture; mould spotting | Wall orientation; furniture position; room temperature and humidity if measured; extract and trickle vents | Same pattern on internal walls with no cold surface; marks that do not change with heating or season (from the householder's account) |
| Possible rising damp | Low on walls in contact with the ground; may be a band, sometimes salts or blown plaster | Height and length of the band; walls affected; any visible damp-proof course | Damp confined to one point below a pipe or wet room; damp high on the wall with none at the base |
| Bridged damp-proof course | Low on the wall directly inside raised ground, a path, a patio or a planter | External level against the wall; level of any visible damp-proof course; photograph from outside and inside | Ground well below any damp-proof course line along that wall |
| Leak | Near pipe runs, radiators, a kitchen or bathroom; a patch growing from a point | Pipe routes you can see; radiator valves; wet room positions; householder's account of when it is worse | No pipes or wet rooms near the patch; patch that follows the whole external wall |
| Moisture-open traditional floor | Lower walls next to a flagstone, tile, lime or earth floor, perhaps after an impermeable finish was laid | Floor finish and any recent covering (vinyl, laminate, sealed tiles); wall construction | A modern concrete floor with visible screed and membrane edge at a threshold |

### Holding more than one candidate

Most real cases mix candidates. A raised patio can bridge the damp-proof course and cover an air brick at once. Record each observation separately, with its location, and say what you could not see. "Mould at skirting, rear wall, worst behind sofa; patio level about 50 mm below internal floor at rear door; one air brick on rear wall partly below patio; front air bricks clear" lets the designer work. "Rising damp in rear room" does not, and the guidance warns it is often wrong.

Do not use a meter reading on masonry or plaster as evidence for any candidate. Part 1, Section 5 explained why: meters on masonry respond to salts and are set up for timber.

## Section 5: What Insulating or Sealing a Floor Changes

**In short:** Approved Document L sets 0.70 threshold and 0.25 improved for existing floors, and Table C1 notes uninsulated floors are often "relatively low" against walls and roofs. Sealing or insulating a suspended floor is a "major" measure in Approved Document F Table 3.1, because it changes how the house is ventilated. It also changes the void, the floor edge, gas pipes and radon risk. Each is a design matter; your record feeds it.

### The energy standards, as a grid

| Item | ADL 2021 (England, from 15 June 2022) | ADL 2026 (England, from 24 March 2027) |
|---|---|---|
| Existing floor: threshold / improved U-value, W/(m²·K) | Table 4.3: 0.70 / 0.25 | Table 3.3: 0.70 / 0.25 |
| Where adjoining floor levels cause problems | Note 8: "a lesser standard may be appropriate" | Note 8: "a higher U-value may be appropriate" |
| If 0.25 is not feasible or exceeds a 15-year payback | 4.13: lowest feasible U-value; generally not above 0.7 once upgraded, unless the work complies with Part C on condensation | 3.14: same |
| When the whole renovated element should meet the improved value | 11.3: more than 50% of the element, or more than 25% of the envelope | 11.3: same |
| Historic and traditional dwellings | 0.10: improve "only if doing so will not cause long-term deterioration" | 0.11: same |
| Wall-to-floor junction (new dwellings; "where appropriate" for existing, 4.19 / 3.20) | 4.17(f)(ii): in masonry, external or cavity wall insulation at least 215mm below the underside of the floor structure/slab | 3.18(f)(ii): same |

Table C1 of both editions adds context for ground floors (2021 wording): "In many cases, existing uninsulated floor U-values are already relatively low when compared with wall and roof U-values". Where the existing value is above 0.70, added insulation "is likely to be cost-effective", and because the cost-benefit curve is flat, 0.25 is the target "subject to other technical constraints (adjoining floor levels, etc.)".

A floor with a short exposed perimeter for its area models lower than one with a long perimeter. |---|---|---|---|
| Mid-terrace 5 × 8 m (40 m², P = 10 m) | 8.0 | 0.44 / 0.31 / 0.25 / 0.21 / 0.18 / 0.14 | 0.50 / 0.37 / 0.29 / 0.24 / 0.21 / 0.16 |

### Ventilation: a "major" measure

Approved Document F Volume 1 (2021 and 2026, England) lists "Sealing and/or insulating a suspended ground floor" as a **major** measure in Table 3.1 (item j), alongside external or internal wall insulation to more than half the wall area. Paragraph 3.6 says building work "should not reduce the ventilation provision of the dwelling" unless the result meets requirement F1(1). MER-RA-008 and the Ventilation Series (MER-ALL-025 to 027) cover how the categories work.

The BEIS timber floor guide agrees: where sub-floor ventilation "will have supplied an element of the ventilation requirement of the property through infiltration", a ventilation assessment "must be undertaken in accordance with" PAS 2035 (para 46), so that the building "will not become less compliant than before" (para 52).

RdSAP shows the change on a much smaller scale. For age bands A to E, a suspended timber floor is "unsealed" with floor infiltration 0.2, or "sealed" at 0.1 if its U-value is below 0.5, or if it has retro-fitted insulation and no U-value is supplied (specification, p. 29). The SAP worksheet enters these in air changes per hour at item (12) and skips that item where a pressure test result is used (SAP 10.3).

> Calculated (not an RdSAP output), for scale only: an assumed 90 m² dwelling with 2.4 m ceilings has a volume of 216 m³; 0.1 air changes per hour × 216 m³ = 21.6 m³/h = 6.0 l/s at the worksheet input, before shelter, wind and the effective-rate step

Approved Document F Table 1.3 gives 31 l/s as the bedroom-based minimum whole-dwelling rate for three bedrooms. The figures measure different things, so this shows scale, not compliance: the energy model's input changes a little, while Approved Document F treats the work as major.

### The void, the edge and the services

The BEIS timber floor guide sets out what changes underneath:
- **Void height.** The void should leave "sufficient air movement post-installation (150mm minimum)" (para 50). It is "not recommended to fully fill the void with blown insulation material ... under any circumstances" (para 70).
- **Damp-proof course.** It is "essential" that insulation "does not bridge the damp proof course and allow moisture damage to the main wall structure" (para 98).
- **Condensation on timber.** With insulation above the joists, "interstitial condensation can occur on the timber" (para 100).
- **Gas pipes.** "Gas pipes should not be insulated and must remain in a ventilated space" (para 93).
- **Other services.** Water pipes "should be insulated appropriately" (para 92); high-amperage cables "must not be covered with insulation" (para 94); flues "must be safeguarded" (para 95).

For solid floors, the BEIS solid floor guide says thermal bridges should be minimised to an fRsi above 0.75, and an insulated perimeter upstand "should be considered" (Section 7, pp. 22 to 23). Part 1 covered fRsi.

### Radon

The BEIS timber floor guide's Addendum says "Presence of radon makes the requirement for ventilation in sub-floor areas even more vital to prevent its build up", and that addresses should be checked against the radon map before installation, as sealing the floor "could exacerbate any build up of radon gas" (p. 39). The solid floor guide says "take expert advice" where high levels are evident (p. 15). You can note whether a radon check is on file; the check and any response belong to the designer and a radon specialist.

### Traditional and decorative floors

PAS 2035:2023 notes that "Solid floor insulation will affect any decorative tiled floors" (Annex E, E.1.5 NOTE 4). For historically significant boards that should stay in place, Historic England says that if there is no access from below, "it may be preferable to leave these floors un-insulated" (HEAG086, §2.1, p. 6). Record tiles, boards and finishes as features.

> **Wider view: one carefully measured floor**
>
> Pelsmakers and Elwell, "Suspended timber ground floors: Heat loss reduction potential of insulation interventions" (*Energy and Buildings*, 2017; doi 10.1016/j.enbuild.2017.07.085), measured one 12.15 m² living-room floor in a pre-1919 solid-wall terraced house in London, using 27 heat flux sensors. The uninsulated floor measured 1.04 ± 0.12 W/m²K, against modelled values of 0.57 (ISO 13370), 0.51 (RdSAP) and 0.52 (CIBSE 2015): "about twice that derived from modelling" (Section 4.4). Woodfibre between the joists brought it to 0.36 (65% lower); a bead full-fill with air bricks sealed to 0.09 (92% lower). In both insulated floors, the 1,000 mm perimeter zone lost significantly more heat than the rest (Section 4.2).
>
> The authors are careful about limits. It is one floor in one house, and "study in a larger, international floor sample is needed" (Section 4.4). Models are steady-state and "generally perfect insulation fit is assumed, but this might be difficult to achieve in reality" (Section 4.4). On the bead-filled floor, "the long-term impact of sealing airbricks remains uncharacterised" (Section 3.1), moisture effects are "poorly characterised" (Section 5), and they question whether Part C's ventilated void would be met.
>
> **What it means for your work:** a modelled floor U-value is an estimate, and in this study the perimeter zone lost the most heat. Record exactly what you can see at the perimeter (vents, gaps at skirtings, any insulation and how it meets the wall), because that is where both the model and the installation are least certain.

## Section 6: Timber Decay and Other Stop Points

**In short:** You do not identify rot or insect attack, and you do not probe timber. The BEIS guide and Historic England give triggers you can record: sloping or springy floors, visible decay, staining, condensation, bore holes, joist ends in damp walls, and suspected asbestos. The BEIS 20% timber moisture limit is the installer's and specialist's figure, not a pass or fail for your meter. Red in this section means get professional advice.

### What the sources ask the installer to check

Before underfloor insulation, the BEIS timber floor guide says the void and structure should be "thoroughly assessed", with emphasis on the floor structure being "free from rot (dry rot and wet rot), especially at the abutment to any external walls", "no existing evidence of dampness, staining or condensation", and "no evidence of infestation (bore holes or active infestation)" (para 49, p. 24). Defects found "should be either rectified before work commences" or handled as the guide sets out (para 51).

Its Table 3 adds:
- "Timber moisture content should not exceed 20%" (p. 18).
- "Insulating timber with high levels of moisture/rot or decay can lead to adding additional stress to the timbers" (p. 18).
- Joist ends built into walls carry a "Greater risk of rising damp causing rot of joist ends" (p. 17).
- Combustion appliances: "Carbon monoxide risk or failure of the appliance to operate effectively" (pp. 19 to 20).
- Asbestos: "Would need specialist removal or encapsulation" (p. 19).

Items in red in Table 3 "indicate that the works must not proceed" (para 47, p. 15). That instruction is to the installer.

Historic England adds: "Sloping or springy floors may be due to either the failure of the supporting structure" or defective timber, and where defects are found, "make sure the cause of the defect is found" (HEAG086, §2.2, p. 7).

### The 20% figure and your meter

The 20% limit is written for the person surveying timber before installation, not for you to test against with a meter. Part 1, Section 5 found no source for assessor timber thresholds. If timber looks damp, record what you see and where; the figure explains why the finding goes to a specialist.

### What you can record without touching

- the floor underfoot: firm, springy, sloping or uneven, and where; gaps opening at skirtings;
- visible staining, growth or holes on any timber you can see without moving anything;
- any smell the householder reports, in their words;
- void materials that might contain asbestos, recorded as "material of unknown type";
- the gas meter and any gas pipe you can see entering the floor.

Do not press, probe, cut or lift anything. Decisions to open up belong to someone qualified (Part 1, Section 7).

### The urgency traffic light

The bands below are this course's recommended practice, not a regulation. Red means "get professional advice"; it is advisory and does not stop anyone's work.
- **Green: record as seen.** Air bricks present on opposing walls, clear; external ground clearly below any damp-proof course line; floor firm; no staining or mould at skirtings.
- **Amber: record and refer to the coordinator or designer.** Vents blocked, buried or on one wall only; an extension across the cross-flow; ground or paths at or above the damp-proof course line; damp or mould at skirtings; an impermeable finish over a traditional floor; void not inspected.
- **Red: get professional advice.** Floor springy or sloping (structural engineer or building surveyor); visible decay, growth or bore holes in timber (timber and damp specialist); water lying in a void you can see (tell the occupant, and the landlord if rented, promptly); suspected asbestos (specialist); water near electrics (electrician); any gas concern (follow your organisation's gas safety procedure).

**Visual:** Traffic light. Three bands with icons. Green: "Vents clear on opposing walls; ground below DPC line; floor firm. Record." Amber: "Vents blocked or one-sided; extension across cross-flow; raised ground; skirting damp; void not inspected. Record and refer to coordinator or designer." Red: "Springy or sloping floor; visible decay or bore holes; water lying in void; suspected asbestos; water near electrics; gas concern. Get professional advice." ## Section 7: Recording and Referring

**In short:** PAS 2035:2023 asks for the condition of the dwelling, the location and severity of defects, leaks, condensation and mould, and the moisture properties of exposed floors. Defects go in two categories: repair before retrofit, or recommended but not essential. Photograph the floor's edges, outside and in. Refer to the coordinator and designer, and to specialists by finding. Where ECO4 or GBIS floor insulation is involved, name TrustMark's route first.

### What PAS 2035 asks you to record

For Retrofit Assessors working under PAS 2035:2023, clause 7.3.2 requires:
- an appraisal of "heritage, architectural features, structure, construction and condition" sufficient to establish suitability for improvement (b);
- "the location and severity of any existing construction defects or structural defects or leaks, and of any condensation and/or mould growth" (d);
- the main materials, U-values and "moisture properties of the main building elements (exposed floors, walls and roofs)" (g).

NOTE 1 to 7.3.2 d) says this "might require the use of test equipment or invasive assessments, including opening up of the building fabric". In Meridian's reading, that is something the coordinator or designer may commission, not something you do or tell a householder to do.

Clause 7.4.2 then sorts defects into "defects that need to be repaired before any retrofit work can proceed" and "defects that are recommended but not an essential prerequisite to retrofit". Clause 5.5.1 a) shows why damp findings matter: fabric first means dealing "with water penetration and damp (so that the building fabric is properly dried out)". Which category a finding belongs in is for the coordinator and designer; your record should make it easy to decide.

### Photographs

Clause 7.4.1 asks for "a photographic record of all the recorded features of the building and of any identified defects". For a ground floor, this course's recommended set:
- each external wall at ground level: air bricks, ground or path levels, any damp-proof course line, and close-ups of each vent;
- extensions, ramps or decking that meet the wall;
- inside: the floor finish at a threshold, skirtings, and any staining or mould with a scale reference;
- visible gas pipe entry and water pipes near floor level.

### Who to refer to

The government's damp and mould guidance lists the professionals landlords might use, including "CSRT - Certificated Surveyor in Remedial Treatment", "CRDS - Certificate in Remedial Damp Surveying", "CSDTB/CSDB (Chartered Surveyor in Timber and Dampness in Buildings)", building surveyors and environmental health ("Working with professionals"). Naming the types it lists is fine; naming a firm or product is not.

| Finding | Suggest |
|---|---|
| Any floor defect in a PAS 2035 project | The Retrofit Coordinator, through your report |
| Blocked vents, raised ground, extension across cross-flow | Coordinator and designer |
| Damp or mould at the base of walls you cannot account for | A specialist damp survey (BEIS 2022, Table 1, p. 15) |
| Visible timber decay or bore holes | A timber and damp specialist |
| Springy or sloping floor, structural concerns | A structural engineer or building surveyor |
| Radon map shows the home in an affected area, or no check on file | Designer and radon specialist (BEIS 2020, Addendum) |
| Suspected asbestos | An asbestos specialist |
| Gas concern | Your organisation's gas safety procedure |

### Floor insulation under ECO4 or GBIS

ECO4 Delivery Guidance v4.0 (26 March 2026) treats underfloor insulation on a suspended floor and solid floor insulation as different measures, and expects both to be installed in line with the government's best practice guides (paras 5.41 to 5.44). Measures covered by PAS should be installed "by or under the responsibility of a TrustMark registered installer" (para 4.19).

If a householder tells you their floor was insulated under ECO4 or GBIS and something is wrong, name TrustMark first. TrustMark's "Free Find & Fix Audit Offer" page (updated 23 October 2026) says "If you have had work done under ECO4 or GBIS, see the next steps", but describes the free audit for external wall insulation and does not mention floors. The householder should ask TrustMark whether their floor is covered, rather than rely on your reading.

TrustMark's complaints process (updated 7 April 2026) runs in order: the registered business, its Scheme Provider, then the Dispute Resolution Ombudsman, escalated within 12 months of the first complaint to the business. The page does not say whether those stages are free.

### A worked record

A 1920s semi, suspended timber floor in the front and rear rooms, November visit:

"Front reception: suspended timber floor (boards visible at threshold), firm underfoot. Two air bricks on front wall, clear. Side wall: one air brick, partly below gravel path. Kitchen extension across rear wall; no rear air bricks seen. Rear reception: floor slightly springy within approx. 1 m of rear wall; skirting staining to approx. 150 mm, 2 m long. Void not inspected (no open access). Gas pipe enters floor at front left. Householder reports a musty smell in winter. Recommend: cross-ventilation and floor condition to coordinator and designer; springy floor and staining to a timber and damp specialist or building surveyor before any floor insulation is considered."

It says what was seen, where, and what was not seen. It names no cause and tells nobody to lift anything.

## Key Takeaways

- A solid floor keeps dry with a membrane continuous with the wall damp-proof course; a suspended timber floor relies on covered ground, a ventilated void and damp-proof courses under the timber (Approved Document C 4.6 to 4.14). Most of this is hidden: record the edges.
- The BEIS guide says suspended timber floor durability is "in most circumstances totally dependent" on cross-flow ventilation. Raised ground, paths, decking, ramps and extensions block air bricks.
- Approved Document C sets openings at the greater of 1,500 mm² per m run of external wall or 500 mm² per m² of floor area, without defining the wall run. Record vents; the designer sizes them.
- Damp or mould at the base of a ground-floor wall has at least five candidates. The BEIS solid floor guide says it "may be indicative" of rising damp and sends doubts to a specialist damp survey.
- Approved Document L sets 0.70 threshold and 0.25 improved for existing floors (Table 4.3, 2021; Table 3.3, 2026), and Table C1 notes uninsulated floors are often "relatively low" already. One measured floor read about twice its modelled value.
- Sealing or insulating a suspended floor is a major measure in Approved Document F Table 3.1. Gas pipes stay uninsulated in a ventilated space; radon makes sub-floor ventilation "even more vital".
- The 20% timber moisture limit is the installer's figure. You record triggers (springy floors, visible decay, bore holes, suspected asbestos) and refer; you never probe or lift.
- Report defects in the two PAS 2035 7.4.2 categories. Where ECO4 or GBIS floor insulation is involved, name TrustMark first.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** A semi-detached house has a 6 × 8 m suspended timber ground floor (48 m²) with air bricks in the front and rear walls, each 6 m long. Taking "run of external wall" as those two walls, what minimum total opening area does Approved Document C 4.14(b) give?

A) 18,000 mm²
B) 24,000 mm²
C) 30,000 mm²
D) 60,000 mm²

**Correct answer: B.** The wall-run rule gives 1,500 × 12 = 18,000 mm² and the floor-area rule 500 × 48 = 24,000 mm²; "whichever gives the greater" is 24,000 mm². 30,000 mm² is the wall-run rule over all 20 m of external wall, the other reading of the paragraph. 60,000 mm² is RdSAP's modelling input, not a requirement (Section 3).

---

**Q2.** During an EPC visit you see mould and staining along the skirting of a rear external wall, and a patio laid up to the wall at about internal floor level. Which record best fits this course?

A) "Rising damp in rear room; patio bridging DPC."
B) "Staining and mould at skirting, rear external wall, approx. 2 m long; patio level approx. at internal floor level against that wall; void not inspected. Recommend professional advice."
C) "Condensation caused by poor heating; advise householder to lift carpet to check the floor."
D) "Meter reading 40% at skirting confirms damp."

**Correct answer: B.** It records location, extent and the external level without naming a cause, says what was not inspected and refers. A names a cause the guidance says is "often misdiagnosed"; C names a cause and tells the householder to open up; D treats a meter reading on masonry as evidence (Sections 4 and 7; Part 1, Section 5).

---

**Q3.** Where does Approved Document F Volume 1 (England) place "Sealing and/or insulating a suspended ground floor" in Table 3.1?

A) Minor measure
B) Major measure
C) Not listed; floors are outside Approved Document F
D) Only a measure where mechanical ventilation is present

**Correct answer: B.** Item j of Table 3.1 lists it as major in both the 2021 and 2026 editions. The BEIS timber floor guide likewise asks for a ventilation assessment where sub-floor air has supplied part of the dwelling's ventilation (para 46) (Section 5).

---

**Q4.** The BEIS suspended timber floor guide says timber moisture content should not exceed 20%. How should an assessor use that figure?

A) Take a pin-meter reading on a visible joist and report pass or fail against 20%
B) Lift a board to reach the joists and test them
C) Treat it as the installer's and specialist's limit: record what you see and refer any sign of damp or decaying timber
D) Ignore it, because timber moisture is never relevant to an assessment

**Correct answer: C.** The 20% figure sits in the guide's survey table for those assessing the floor for insulation (Table 3, p. 18). This course gives no assessor timber threshold, and assessors do not lift or probe (Section 6).

---

**Q5.** A householder says their suspended floor was insulated under ECO4 last year and the room now smells musty. What should you do first?

A) Tell them the insulation was badly installed
B) Name TrustMark first: suggest they contact TrustMark to ask whether its free audit covers their floor, and about its complaints process; record the report factually
C) Recommend they remove the insulation
D) Advise them to go straight to the Dispute Resolution Ombudsman

**Correct answer: B.** TrustMark's audit page names ECO4 and GBIS but describes the free audit for external wall insulation, so the householder should ask TrustMark. TrustMark's complaints process runs business, Scheme Provider, then the Ombudsman, in order. A and C go beyond the assessor's role (Section 7).
