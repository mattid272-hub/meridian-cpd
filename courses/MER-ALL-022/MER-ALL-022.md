## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- Explain in plain terms how a heat pump moves heat from a source to a home, and why the temperature lift between the two matters.
- Distinguish air-to-water, ground and water source, air-to-air, hybrid and exhaust air systems, and say what the Boiler Upgrade Scheme and MCS recognise for each, as at the dates given.
- Define COP, SCOP and SPF, explain the H1 to H4 system boundaries, and say why a figure cannot be compared without its boundary and period.
- Summarise the two government field trials, the Renewable Heat Premium Payment monitoring (2013 to 2015) and the Electrification of Heat demonstration (2020 to 2022), including their medians, the coldest-day result and their stated limits.
- Test common claims about heat pumps against what those trials measured, and record what you see on site without judging how well a system performs.

## Introduction

Heat pumps now appear on assessment visits more often than any other low carbon heating system. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme (BUS) had paid 90,115 grants by 31 July 2026, and 88,092 of them (97.8%) were for air-to-water heat pumps (Ofgem, BUS Quarterly Report Issue 17, Figures 4 and 5). Householders ask assessors whether a heat pump will work in their home, whether it will cope with a cold snap, and whether the one already fitted is "working properly". This course gives you the evidence to talk about those questions accurately, and the limits of that evidence.

Part 1 of the Heating Series covers how heat pumps work, how their efficiency is measured, and what two large government-funded field trials found. Part 2 covers design: heat loss, emitters, hot water, siting and installer standards. Part 3 covers running costs, carbon, grants and how heat pumps are entered in RdSAP.

This course does not train you to design, commission or diagnose a heat pump. Your job on site is to recognise and record what is there, to write "possible" rather than "is", and to know when to suggest the householder gets professional advice.

## Section 1: How a Heat Pump Moves Heat, and the Main Types

**In short:** Think of a heat pump as moving heat, not making it. It takes low-grade heat from outside air, the ground or water, raises its temperature with a compressor and delivers it to radiators, underfloor pipes, warm air or a cylinder. The smaller the gap between the source temperature and the delivery temperature, the less work the compressor has to do. Almost all BUS-funded systems are air-to-water, so you will meet the other types less often on site.

### Source, compressor, sink

The Energy Saving Trust describes an air source heat pump in three steps. It takes "heat from the air and transfer[s] it to a fluid refrigerant"; the refrigerant "passes through a compressor, which raises the fluid's temperature"; and the heat is passed to the home's heating system, which can also heat "water stored in a hot water cylinder" (EST, *Air source heat pumps*, updated 16 July 2026). A ground source system collects heat through "a long or coiled pipe buried in trenches" or "a long loop (called a 'probe') inserted into a borehole", carrying a water and antifreeze mix (EST, *Ground source heat pumps*, updated 20 May 2026).

Three terms are used in the rest of this course:
- **Source:** where the heat comes from (outside air, the ground, water, or air extracted from the house).
- **Sink:** where the heat goes (heating water, room air or the cylinder).
- **Lift:** the temperature difference between source and sink.

The lift drives efficiency. The Electrification of Heat interim report found that on the coldest days, "as the difference in temperatures increases, the COP decreases" (section 5.6), and that a lower mean flow temperature was linked to a higher seasonal performance factor (section 5.3). A colder source or a hotter sink both widen the lift.

### Air-to-air and hybrids

MCS describes air-to-air heat pumps as transferring "heat from the outside air into your home using electricity", through fans and indoor units. As at 24 July 2026, MCS said the technology is "currently being incorporated into MCS" and that "no installers are yet certified to install air-to-air heat pumps". Certified systems "must be optimised to provide heating to your whole property" and "must not be optimised for cooling", and "many air-to-air heat pumps do not provide hot water" (MCS, *Air to air heat pumps*). This course does not cover cooling.

A hybrid pairs a heat pump with another heat source, usually a boiler. MIS 3005-D:2025 requires the heat pump to provide "a minimum of 55% of the calculated heat load", rated at 55°C flow at the design external temperature, with the combined sources providing not less than 100% (clause 3.4.2(d)). "The control philosophy shall be capable of prioritising heat pump utilisation" (clause 3.4.2(e)), and the sources shall be "fully and operationally integrated into a single master control system" (clause 3.4.2(f)). The 55% rule is about capacity, not about how much of the year's heat the heat pump supplies; Section 4 shows what the field data found.

MIS 3005-D:2025 applies to systems with a "thermal output not exceeding 45 Kilowatt (kWth)" (clause 1.5), and BUS uses the same limit for an individual system (para 3.79). Larger systems are outside this course.

## Section 2: COP, SCOP and SPF, and Why the Boundary Matters

**In short:** Read every efficiency figure as heat out divided by electricity in, over a stated period and within a stated boundary. A COP is a short-period or test-condition figure; a SCOP is a calculated seasonal figure; an SPF is measured in a home over a season or year. Before you compare two numbers, check which boundary (H1 to H4) each uses and whether it was measured or calculated. If you cannot tell, you should not compare them.

### One ratio, three names

All three terms express units of heat delivered for each unit of electricity used. A ratio of 3 means 3 kWh of heat for each 1 kWh of electricity. What differs is the period and how the figure was obtained.

| Term | What it is | Where you will see it |
|---|---|---|
| COP (coefficient of performance) | The ratio at one set of test conditions, or measured over a short window | Product datasheets; the trial "coldest day" figures |
| SCOP (seasonal coefficient of performance) | A seasonal ratio calculated from test data over a range of temperatures | The MCS installation certificate; the BUS 2.8 rule |
| SPF (seasonal performance factor) | The ratio measured in a real home over a season or year | Field trials |

The Electrification of Heat (EoH) interim report draws the line by period: "All SPF calculations were carried out on the 12-month data windows", while its COP results "use the same system boundaries as the SPFH4 calculation however they are performed over a period which is not equal to 12-months" (section 4.4). It warns that "performance over shorter time frames is susceptible to large variations" (section 5.6).

### SCOP: a calculated figure

MCS's calculator for SCOP is MCS 026, which is "mandatory in MCS 007 (Heat Pump Product Standard)" and is used to "produce a table of SCOPs over a range of temperatures" that is "used in the creation of the MCS certificate". Its 2025 update aligned it with "BS EN 14825: 2022" (MCS consultation page, 31 March 2025, modified 20 May 2026).

Two rules use SCOP:
- **Design.** MIS 3005-D:2025 requires the SCoP "as taken from the MCS website" to be provided "based on the above design flow temperature" (clause 3.8.1(g)). The SCOP depends on the design flow temperature.
- **Funding.** BUS requires air-to-water and ground source heat pumps to "achieve an in situ seasonal coefficient of performance (SCOP) of at least 2.8", calculated with the MCS calculator and checked against the MCS installation certificate (Installer Guidance V5.1, para 3.83). Compressors "must be electric-driven" (para 3.84).

Because paragraph 3.83 ties the figure to the MCS calculator and the installation certificate, "in situ" here reads as calculated for the installed design, not measured after installation. A certificate SCOP of 2.8 and a trial SPF of 2.8 are different measures.

### The H1 to H4 boundaries

A measured SPF depends on which electricity and heat are counted. The RHPP final report sets out four nested boundaries (section 1.2):

| Boundary | What is counted |
|---|---|
| H1 | Core heat pump components only: compressor, condenser, expansion valve and evaporator |
| H2 | H1 plus the "supply air fan or ground loop pump power into the HP" |
| H3 | H2 plus "backup heaters, including electric immersion for domestic hot water if present" |
| H4 | H3 plus "system circulators or pumps" |

The RHPP report also defines an H5 boundary for cylinder losses, not used in the headline figures. Each step outwards adds electricity that is not the heat pump's own cycle, so of H1 to H4, H4 gives the lowest figure. The EoH report uses the same boundaries "as defined in the SEPEMO project" and reports at H2 and H4 (section 4.4, Figure 4.3). H4 is closest to what a householder pays for, because it includes the immersion, backup heater and pumps.

### Worked numbers: how much the boundary moves the figure

| Trial (air source) | Median SPFH2 | Median SPFH4 | H4 lower by |
|---|---|---|---|
| RHPP (Table 3-2) | 2.65 | 2.44 | 8% |
| EoH (section 1.1.1) | 2.94 | 2.80 | 5% |

Calculated here: (2.65 − 2.44) ÷ 2.65 = 0.079, and (2.94 − 2.80) ÷ 2.94 = 0.048. A brochure COP, a certificate SCOP and a trial SPFH4 are three different measures.

Published advice sometimes uses one headline ratio. The Energy Saving Trust's air source page says "heat pumps are more than three times more efficient than a gas or oil boiler". That is a statement about energy delivered per unit of energy used, not about running cost or carbon, which depend on prices and emission factors (Part 3). When a householder quotes a ratio, it is reasonable to ask which measure it is, over what period, and within which boundary.

## Section 3: The Two Government Field Trials

**In short:** Use the two trials as the main measured evidence for heat pumps in British homes, and quote them with their limits. The RHPP monitoring (October 2013 to March 2015) found a median air source SPFH4 of 2.44; the Electrification of Heat trial (data to August 2022) found 2.80. You should not present the rise as a like-for-like improvement, because the samples, installation dates and selection differ.

### The RHPP monitoring, 2013 to 2015

The Renewable Heat Premium Payment (RHPP) scheme "provided subsidies for private householders, Registered Social Landlords and communities", supporting about 14,000 heat pumps. The monitoring "covered a total of 700 heat pump sites", with "2 minute heat and electricity data collected from 31st October 2013 to 31st March 2015" (RHPP final report, section 1.1). The report was prepared by the RAPID-HPC team under contract to BEIS and issued in March 2017.

| RHPP (Table 3-2) | Sample | Median SPFH2 (IQR) | Median SPFH4 (IQR) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air source | 292 (H2), 293 (H4) | 2.65 (2.33 to 2.95) | 2.44 (2.15 to 2.67) |
| Ground source | 92 | 2.81 (2.63 to 3.14) | 2.71 (2.48 to 3.02) |

The IQR (interquartile range) is the spread of the middle half: a quarter of systems were below the lower figure and a quarter above the upper.

Limits the report attaches:
- "RHPP heat pumps were installed between 2009 and 2014" (Context), and the results "should not be assumed to apply to heat pumps installed after the RHPP" (section 7).
- Sites with SPFH4 outside 1.5 to 4.5 were filtered out as "likely to have been influenced by monitoring anomalies" (section 3.3).
- Heat meters were "calibrated for water, rather than glycol", so "SPFs will be over-estimated by 4-7%", and the correction "has not been applied" (section 3.3). The report says this is likely to apply to most heat pumps (section 8) and to occur in both air and ground source systems (Appendix A3).
- There were "a low number of days in the RHPP dataset with daily mean external temperatures of 0C" (section 7.6), so it says little directly about cold weather.

### The Electrification of Heat demonstration, 2020 to 2022

The government-funded Electrification of Heat project aimed to "demonstrate the feasibility of a large-scale roll-out of heat pumps in Great Britain", installing and monitoring around 750 systems, mostly on the gas grid (gov.uk, *Electrification of Heat demonstration project*, last updated 29 June 2020). The interim report records 742 systems installed between September 2020 and November 2021 "in a range of home types and ages" (section 3.1):

| Type | Installed | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Low-temperature air source | 306 | 41.2% |
| High-temperature air source | 243 | 32.7% |
| Ground source | 38 | 5.1% |
| Hybrid | 155 | 20.9% |

The executive summary describes the data as running from November 2020 to August 2022 (section 2.2 gives the end as 24 August 2022), with SPFs over 12-month windows (sections 1.1.1, 4.4 and 5.1; Tables 5.1 and 5.2):

| Measure | Sample | Median (IQR) |
|---|---|---|
| Air source SPFH4, all | 291 | 2.80 (2.53 to 3.09) |
| Air source SPFH2, all | 291 | 2.94 (2.66 to 3.20) |
| Low-temperature air source SPFH4 | 187 | 2.74 |
| High-temperature air source SPFH4 | 104 | 2.89 |
| Hybrid, heat pump unit only, SPFH4 | 58 | 2.37 (2.01 to 2.81) |
| Ground source | Not analysed | Too few suitable samples |

The hybrid figure covers the heat pump unit only and excludes the boiler. The report states its own limits:
- The homes "were deemed suitable for a heat pump installation by trained designers and installers", so results "may not be representative of the UK housing stock" (section 5.4).
- Delivery areas were in England and Scotland, and "there may be some geographical bias in the analysis results" (section 3.1). Wales is not named.
- Results with SPF outside 1.5 to 4.5 were excluded, as were sites with less than 50% of expected data (section 4.3).
- Variation between installations was high and "difficult to explain" (section 5.1).
- The results are interim; the final reports could not be opened for this course.

### Comparing the two

The air source median at H4 rose from 2.44 in RHPP to 2.80 in EoH, and the EoH report sets the two side by side, "not accounting for weather variations" (section 1.1.1). It is fair to say the later trial measured higher median performance. These sources do not support saying the difference has a single cause, or that a heat pump installed today will reach 2.80: the samples were selected differently, installed in different years under different standards, in different areas, and RHPP carries an uncorrected meter over-estimate.

A calculated illustration: for a home needing 10,000 kWh of heat a year (an assumed figure), SPF 2.44 implies 10,000 ÷ 2.44 = 4,098 kWh of electricity, and SPF 2.80 implies 10,000 ÷ 2.80 = 3,571 kWh, about 13% less.

## Section 4: What Drives the Numbers: Cold Days, Flow Temperature, Hot Water and Hybrids

**In short:** Expect a heat pump's efficiency to fall as the weather gets colder and as the water it heats gets hotter. On the coldest day in the EoH trial, air source heat pumps had a median COP of 2.44 at a mean outdoor temperature of −0.4°C. Lower flow temperatures were linked to higher efficiency, heavy immersion use was linked to low efficiency in RHPP, and in EoH hybrids the heat pump supplied a median of well under half the space heating.

### Cold days

The EoH report selected, for each air source heat pump, "the coldest day where each heat pump was operational", between November and March (sections 4.3 and 5.6). Across all air source systems (Table 5.7), the coldest-day COP at the H4 boundary had a median of 2.44 (IQR 2.20 to 2.70) and a mean of 2.44 (95% confidence interval 2.40 to 2.48), n = 484.

The executive summary gives "the coldest day" a "mean temperature" of −0.4°C (section 1.1.4). Because each system's coldest day was chosen on "the lowest mean external temperature" (section 4.3), this course reads it as a mean outdoor temperature. The report does not say how the figure was averaged across systems. It is not the lowest temperature any system saw, and no minimum is given.

Quote the result with its temperature: "a median COP of 2.44 on the coldest day, at a mean outdoor temperature of −0.4°C". The report adds that there was "significant variation in the COP values, especially where a mean temperature difference of 15 to 20°C" applied (section 5.6). Hybrids and ground source were not included.

### Flow temperature

Across EoH air source and hybrid systems the mean flow temperature was 38.2°C, and lower mean flow temperatures were linked to higher SPF. Of 104 high-temperature units, 94 reached 65°C at some point, and 81 of those 94 exceeded 65°C less than 1% of the time (section 5.3). RHPP found "average winter space heating flow temperatures were generally low (<45°C), with only a few sites showing average winter flow temperatures >50°C" (section 5), with 17% of systems designed for 50°C to 60°C (section 7.6).

MIS 3005-D:2025 says high-temperature heat pumps "should be avoided unless the application requires a flow temperature higher than 55oC" (clause 3.4.3). Where a design exceeds 55°C, "an alternative design using a flow temperature of 55oC or lower should also be provided", with the differences "explained to the customer allowing them to choose" (clause 3.4.4).

### Hot water and the immersion heater

For hot water, MIS 3005-D:2025 requires that "the heat pump shall be capable of a flow temperature of at least 55oC at design conditions" (clause 3.5.5).

RHPP found that, where measured, "immersion heater electricity consumption was 12% of the total" on average, and "more than half of the sites with SPFH4<2 had immersion heating use > 20% of total electricity" (section 5). Some immersion use is intended: where cylinder storage was below 60°C, "the installation should have incorporated regular sterilisation", and between a quarter and a third of 220 metered immersions showed operation "consistent with regular sterilisation" (section 7.5). That is inferred from the pattern of use, as cylinder temperatures were not monitored. An immersion heater is not a fault; long running periods are something to record, because the data links them with lower efficiency.

### Hybrids: how much heat came from the heat pump

In EoH hybrid homes the heat pump supplied a median 38.6% of space heating (n = 53; quartiles 31.4% and 49.6%), and a higher share was linked to a higher SPF (section 5.5, Table 5.6). The report cautions that the hybrids were "controlled to operate cost-optimally", that a large proportion "had their controls optimised" in spring 2022, that hot water provision was not metered, and that the result "may be slightly skewed". A hybrid designed so the heat pump can meet 55% of the design load may, in operation, supply less than half the year's space heating; the two figures answer different questions.

### Other factors the trials reported

- **House type.** Detached homes had a statistically significantly higher SPF than semi-detached; terraced and semi-detached did not differ significantly (EoH section 5.4).
- **Home age.** "Home age and household income do not have a statistically significant impact on the observed SPF in this study" (EoH executive summary; section 5.4).
- **Refrigerant.** Median SPFH4 was 2.94 for R32 (n = 82), 2.89 for R290 (n = 98) and 2.66 for R410a (n = 111); the report warns refrigerant alone does not explain the gap, as R410a units tended to be older (section 5.2, Table 5.3).
- **Sizing.** RHPP found "a majority of heat pumps appear to be adequately sized when compared to peak measured load" (section 7.2), but that "SPF was sensitive to load factor" (section 6.7).

## Section 5: Common Claims Tested Against the Evidence

**In short:** When a householder repeats a common claim about heat pumps, you should answer with what was measured, the conditions it was measured under, and what the evidence does not cover. The trials give measured answers on cold weather, home age, flow temperature, hybrids and the gap between brochure and field figures. They give no measured answer on noise in this course, and they do not cover every house type or Wales.

Each claim is set out with what the evidence opened for this course says and its limits. None of it predicts how a particular home will perform.

| Claim | What the evidence says | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| "Heat pumps don't work in cold weather" | Median coldest-day COP(H4) 2.44 at a mean outdoor temperature of −0.4°C; COP falls as the temperature difference grows (EoH Table 5.7, section 5.6) | A mean, not the lowest temperature; English and Scottish areas only; RHPP had few days averaging 0°C |
| "Old houses can't have one" | Home age had no statistically significant effect on SPF; systems were in "a range of home types and ages" (EoH sections 3.1, 5.4) | Every home was pre-screened as suitable; says nothing about whether any old home is suitable; Historic England guidance not opened |
| "You need underfloor heating" | EST describes heating "via radiators or underfloor heating" and lists "Whether your radiators are appropriately sized" as a running cost factor; mean EoH flow temperature 38.2°C; RHPP winter flows "generally low (<45°C)" | The interim EoH report does not give the radiator and underfloor split; radiator sizing is a design question (Part 2) |
| "A high-temperature heat pump runs hot all the time" | 81 of 94 high-temperature units that reached 65°C exceeded it less than 1% of the time; their median SPFH4 was 2.89 against 2.74 for low-temperature units (EoH section 5.3; Table 5.2) | Different homes, so descriptive, not a test of one type against the other |
| "A hybrid runs mostly on the heat pump" | Heat pump supplied a median 38.6% of space heating (EoH Table 5.6) | 53 homes; cost-optimised boiler use; controls changed mid-trial |
| "The datasheet figure is what you'll get" | Measured median SPFH4 2.80, a quarter of systems below 2.53 and a quarter above 3.09 (EoH 1.1.1); the certificate SCOP is calculated for the design flow temperature (MIS 3005-D cl. 3.8.1(g)) | Test standards BS EN 14511 and 14825 not opened |
| "Heat pumps are noisy" | No primary measured evidence opened for this course | Planning noise rules and MCS 020(a) are in Part 2 |
| "They've only just got good" | Median air source SPFH4 2.44 in RHPP and 2.80 in EoH | RHPP results "should not be assumed to apply to heat pumps installed after the RHPP"; samples differ (Section 3) |

Two of these deserve a further word.

On cold weather, MIS 3005-D:2025 says an air source system "should be able to maintain the internal design temperatures across multiple defrost cycles" (clause 3.4.1(f)) and requires a heat pump to be selected that will provide "at least 100% of the calculated heat load" "without input from any supplementary electric heater" (clause 3.4.1(e)). Those are design requirements. Whether a particular system meets them is for the installer or a heat pump designer, not the assessor.

On old houses, the finding is about how homes judged suitable performed. MIS 3005-D:2025 requires the building to be "assessed by a competent professional experienced in heat pump systems" (clause 3.1.4). The honest answer to "can my old house have one?" is that the trial does not settle it, and a heat loss survey and design will.

## Section 6: What the Assessor Records on Site

**In short:** Record what is there and which documents the householder can show, not how well the system performs. Note the type, visible plant, emitters, any cylinder, immersion or backup heater, any boiler working alongside, and the controls. Ask for the MCS installation certificate and the design information. If something may affect performance or safety, record it as "possible", adjust nothing, and suggest the householder contacts the installer, or a competent professional for a safety concern.

### Why the line sits here

The trials found performance varied widely for reasons "difficult to explain" even with detailed monitoring (EoH section 5.1). A site visit cannot measure SPF. What you can do is make an accurate record that a designer, installer or later assessor can rely on, and point the householder to the right person.

### What to record

| Item | What to note | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| System type | Air-to-water, ground source, water source, exhaust air, air-to-air, hybrid; number of units | BUS and MCS treat types differently (Section 1) |
| Make and model | As on the unit's label; photograph it | Lets the product be matched to its published data |
| Emitters | Radiators, underfloor, fan units or a mix; any heated rooms without one | Design is to "the worst performing room" (MIS 3005-D cl. 3.8.1(d)) |
| Hot water | Cylinder; immersion heater; any separate water heater | Air-to-air often gives no hot water (MCS); immersion use linked to low SPFH4 (RHPP s5) |
| Backup heating | Electric backup heater, boiler, room heaters | Counted at H3 and H4 (Section 2); hybrids need integrated controls (cl. 3.4.2(f)) |
| Controls | Programmer, thermostats, immersion timer | Hybrid controls should prioritise the heat pump (cl. 3.4.2(e)) |
| Outdoor or ground plant | Outdoor unit location; visible manifold or borehole cover | Siting is in Part 2 |

### What evidence to ask for

MIS 3005-D:2025 lists the design information to be provided to the installer (clause 3.8.1), including "All specific room heat losses (in W/m2)" (a), "The type and dimensions of emitter(s)" (c), "The design emitter temperature based on the worst performing room" (d), and the SCoP "based on the above design flow temperature" (g). A householder with an MCS certified installation may hold some of this, with the MCS installation certificate. For BUS-funded systems, Ofgem checks the SCOP against that certificate (Installer Guidance V5.1, para 3.83). Record which documents you saw, and photograph them where the householder agrees.

How design flow temperature evidence is entered in RdSAP is set by the RdSAP conventions, which could not be opened for this course. Check the current conventions issued through your accreditation scheme.

### Language in the record

Write what you saw and what you were told, with its source:
- "Air-to-water heat pump, outdoor unit on rear wall. Label photographed. Cylinder with immersion heater. Householder reports immersion timer runs daily; not verified."
- "Gas boiler present alongside heat pump; possible hybrid arrangement. MCS certificate not available on the day."

Avoid writing that a system "is undersized", "is running inefficiently" or "is faulty". Those are design and diagnostic judgements a visit gives you no basis to make.

### Traffic light

The bands are advisory. They help you decide what to record and whether to suggest advice. They do not grade the system, and red does not mean a fault has been found. The examples in each band are this course's own reading for recording and referral; no source opened for this course sets them, and your accreditation scheme's guidance takes precedence.

| Band | What you might see | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Green | Type clear; label readable; emitters in all heated rooms; MCS certificate and design information available | Record and photograph. |
| Amber | No certificate or design information; immersion or backup heater apparently set to run often; a boiler alongside with no clear controls; heated rooms without emitters | Record as observed, using "possible" where cause is unclear. Suggest the householder asks the installer for the design information or a check of settings. |
| Red | Something you can see that may be a safety concern, such as visibly damaged wiring or water near electrical parts; the householder reports the home cannot be kept warm in cold weather; error messages they cannot clear | Record what you saw or were told. Adjust nothing and do not diagnose. Suggest the householder gets professional advice promptly: the installer, and a competent professional for any possible safety concern. |

Green: readable label, cylinder, radiators in each room, MCS certificate in a folder. Amber: an immersion timer set to a long daily period, and a boiler beside the heat pump with a question mark over the controls. ## Section 7: Where the Evidence Is Thin, and Where to Refer

**In short:** Be clear with householders about what the field evidence does not cover: recent ground source performance, Welsh homes, homes not pre-screened as suitable, the final EoH figures, air-to-air and noise. When a householder has a problem with an installed system, you should point them first to the installer, then to MCS if the installation was MCS certified, and then to the dispute resolution route MCS describes.

### Gaps in the evidence

| Gap | What the sources say |
|---|---|
| Ground source | EoH installed 38 but had too few suitable samples; the latest opened figure is RHPP's 2.71 (2013 to 2015, n = 92) |
| Wales | EoH delivery areas were in England and Scotland; RHPP gives no regional split. Wales had 4,722 of the 90,115 BUS grants paid to 31 July 2026, 5.2% (Quarterly Report Issue 17, Figure 6), but no measured data for Welsh homes was opened |
| Homes not pre-screened | EoH results "may not be representative of the UK housing stock" |
| Hybrids | One sample of 53 to 58 homes, with cost-optimised boiler use |
| Air-to-air | No BUS grants paid and no MCS certified installers as at July 2026; no field data opened |

These gaps are not a reason to say heat pumps do not work in those cases. They are a reason to say the trial evidence does not settle the question, and that a site-specific design by a qualified installer is the route to an answer.

### Where to refer

MCS sets out a route for MCS certified installations in *What to do if things go wrong* (MCS consumer page, last modified 7 September 2026):
1. **The installer first.** "If you notice a problem, you should contact your installer first"; "14 days is a reasonable maximum time to expect a response."
2. **MCS.** If unresolved, the householder can complain to MCS online, by email, post, phone or live chat. MCS will "acknowledge your complaint within 5 working days", update at least every 10 working days, and says "Most complaints should be fully resolved within 8 weeks".
3. **Alternative dispute resolution.** The householder can then escalate to dispute resolution run by MCS's partner, the Dispute Resolution Ombudsman, within 12 months of MCS concluding the complaint; MCS says it is free for eligible disputes. For contracts entered into before 1 January 2025, the route is through the installer's consumer code (RECC or HIES).

On 18 June 2026 MCS reported that DESNZ had recognised its redeveloped installer scheme as a Code of Practice for BUS, and that "The transition phase will last until 31 March 2027". Check the MCS consumer page for the current route before passing it on.

For whether a home is suitable, or whether an existing system's design and settings are right, the householder should get advice from an MCS certified heat pump installer or designer.

### What not to do

- Do not change settings, timers or controls, even if asked.
- Do not tell a householder their system is faulty, undersized or inefficient.
- Do not quote a trial median as a prediction for their home.
- Do not recommend or name a manufacturer or installer.

Arrow "not resolved" to box 2 "MCS complaint: acknowledged within 5 working days; most resolved within 8 weeks". Arrow "still not resolved" to box 3 "Dispute resolution via MCS's partner, within 12 months of MCS concluding". ## Key Takeaways

- A heat pump moves heat from a source to a sink; the wider the lift, the lower the efficiency. Lower flow temperatures were linked to higher SPF in EoH.
- Air-to-water made up 97.8% of BUS grants paid to 31 July 2026. Fossil fuel hybrids are not BUS-eligible; air-to-air had no grants paid and, as at July 2026, no MCS certified installers.
- COP, SCOP and SPF are the same ratio over different periods and methods. SCOP is calculated for a design flow temperature; SPF is measured. Always state the boundary: H4 includes immersion, backup heaters and pumps.
- RHPP (2013 to 2015): median air source SPFH4 2.44, ground source 2.71. EoH (data to August 2022): air source 2.80, IQR 2.53 to 3.09.
- On the coldest day, EoH air source systems had a median COP(H4) of 2.44 at a mean outdoor temperature of −0.4°C.
- EoH homes were pre-screened, in England and Scotland with no Welsh delivery area, and the figures are interim. RHPP figures carry an uncorrected meter over-estimate of 4% to 7%.
- RHPP immersions used 12% of electricity on average, and over 20% at more than half the sites below SPFH4 2. EoH hybrid heat pumps supplied a median 38.6% of space heating.
- On site, record type, label, emitters, hot water, backup heat and controls; ask for the MCS certificate and design information; write "possible"; refer to the installer first, then MCS.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** Which SPF boundary includes the electricity used by an immersion heater and the heating circulation pump?

A) H1
B) H2
C) H3
D) H4

**Correct answer: D.** H3 adds backup heaters including the immersion; H4 adds "system circulators or pumps" as well. H4 is the widest boundary and gives the lowest figure (Section 2).

---

**Q2.** A householder says heat pumps "stop working when it's cold". Which statement matches the Electrification of Heat interim report?

A) On the coldest operating day, air source systems had a median COP(H4) of 2.44 at a mean outdoor temperature of −0.4°C
B) Air source systems kept a COP above 3 at −6°C in every home
C) The coldest-day COP was the same as the annual SPF
D) The report did not look at cold weather

**Correct answer: A.** Table 5.7 gives a median COP(H4) of 2.44 (IQR 2.20 to 2.70, n = 484) on the coldest day, below the annual median SPFH4 of 2.80, and the executive summary gives that day's mean temperature as −0.4°C (Sections 4 and 5).

---

**Q3.** Which limit does the Electrification of Heat interim report state about its own sample?

A) It included only homes built after 2000
B) The homes had been judged suitable by trained designers and installers, so results may not be representative of the UK housing stock
C) All homes were in Wales
D) Only ground source heat pumps were monitored

**Correct answer: B.** The homes "were deemed suitable for a heat pump installation by trained designers and installers". Its delivery areas were in England and Scotland, and it could not analyse ground source systems (Section 3).

---

**Q4.** An MCS installation certificate shows a SCOP of 3.1. How should you treat that figure?

A) As the measured efficiency of the system since installation
B) As directly comparable with the EoH median SPFH4 of 2.80
C) As a calculated seasonal figure tied to the design flow temperature, not a measurement
D) As the COP at −0.4°C

**Correct answer: C.** MIS 3005-D:2025 requires the SCoP to be "based on the above design flow temperature" (clause 3.8.1(g)), and MCS 026 calculates it in line with BS EN 14825. SPF is measured in the home over a year (Section 2).

---

**Q5.** On site you find a heat pump alongside a gas boiler, no design paperwork, and an immersion timer set to run for long periods. What is the appropriate record and action?

A) Record that the system is inefficient and reset the immersion timer
B) Record what you saw, including "possible hybrid arrangement" and the immersion setting as observed or reported; suggest the householder asks the installer for the design information and a check of the settings
C) Tell the householder to remove the boiler
D) Record nothing, as performance cannot be measured on a visit

**Correct answer: B.** This is an amber case. RHPP links heavy immersion use with low SPFH4, and MIS 3005-D requires hybrid controls to prioritise the heat pump, but neither gives grounds to diagnose. Record, write "possible", adjust nothing, and refer to the installer first (Sections 4 and 6).
