## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- Describe the heat load calculation that MCS's heat pump design standard, MIS 3005-D:2025, asks for, and the design temperatures it sets.
- Explain the sizing rules: at least 100% of the calculated heat load for a heat pump system, at least 55% for the heat pump in a hybrid, and what the standard says about flow temperatures above 55°C.
- Explain why radiator output falls at lower flow temperatures, and work through a sourced example of how much.
- Describe the hot water sizing basis in MIS 3005-D:2025 and what the sources say about controlling bacterial growth and the cost of immersion heating.
- Set out the permitted development rules for air source heat pumps in England (from 29 May 2025) and Wales (from 1 June 2026), and the MCS 020(a) sound calculation they rely on.
- Identify the installer standards and consumer protection routes that apply, and know what to ask for and record as an assessor or coordinator.

## Introduction

Part 1 of the Heating Series looked at how heat pumps work and what the field data shows. This part is about design: how big the heat pump is, how hot the radiators run, how the cylinder is sized, and where the outdoor unit can go.

Design is the designer's and installer's work, not yours. But you will meet heat pump designs on most retrofit projects, householders will ask whether a quote "sounds right", and RdSAP needs documentary evidence before you can record a low design flow temperature. So you need to know what a design contains and what documents it produces, well enough to recognise and record what you see and to know when to recommend professional advice.

### Where this course applies

This course is written for England and Wales:
- **MIS 3005-D:2025** and **MCS 020(a)** are MCS standards. They apply to MCS contractors wherever they work. MIS 3005-D's own design temperature table lists eight UK locations, of which Cardiff is the only one in Wales.
- **Permitted development** differs by nation. England's rules are in Part 14, Class G of the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) Order 2015, as amended by SI 2025/560. Wales's are in Part 40, Class G of its own Order, as amended by SI 2026 No. 79. Both are set out in Section 5.
- **Approved Document L** cited here is the England document. The 2021 edition (incorporating 2023 amendments) applies until a 2026 edition comes into force on 24 March 2027, subject to transitional provisions (Section 2). The Welsh Approved Document L was not opened.
- **The Warm Homes: Local Grant** guidance covers England only.

### How the sources were handled

Every figure, threshold and quotation comes from a document opened for this course, and clause numbers are given as read. The gaps that matter:
- **BS EN 12831-1:2017** (heat load) and **BS EN 442** (radiator output) are paid British Standards and were not opened. What the course says about them comes from documents that refer to them.

## Section 1: The Heat Load Calculation

**In short:** A heat pump design starts with a heat load calculation, and MIS 3005-D:2025 sets its terms: internal temperatures no lower than its Table 1, external temperatures from its Table 2, and compliance with BS EN 12831-1 in other respects. The installer is given in writing each room's specific heat loss and a design emitter temperature based on the worst performing room. You do not check the calculation; ask whether one exists and record what you see.

### The standard

MCS's design standard for heat pumps is MIS 3005-D:2025, "Heat Pump: Design Standard", Issue 1.0, dated 1 January 2025. Its foreword says: "Compliance with this Standard is mandatory for MCS Contractors certified to MCS: 2025." Where this course quotes a clause, it is from that issue.

### What the calculation uses

Clause 3.4.1(a) says "A heat load calculation should be performed on the building using internal temperatures not less than those specified" in Table 1. The external design temperature comes from Table 2, column A or column B, chosen by the contractor. "If column B is selected, no uplift factor for intermittent heating is required." The calculation "shall in other respects comply with BS EN 12831-1:2017" (clause 3.4.2(a), which repeats the same requirement for hybrid systems).

Table 1 sets minimum internal design temperatures by room. Three examples:

| Room | Minimum internal design temperature (Table 1) |
|---|---|
| Living room | 21°C |
| Bedroom | 18°C |
| Bathroom | 22°C |

Table 2 is titled "Outside design temperatures for different locations in the UK taken from CIBSE Guide A Table 2.5". It lists eight locations: Belfast, Birmingham, Cardiff, Edinburgh, Glasgow, London, Manchester and Plymouth. Column A is the hourly temperature equalled or exceeded for 99% of the hours in a year, and column B for 99.6%, the colder design case.

Floors differ. For a solid ground floor, the temperature difference is "the internal design room temperature (Table 1) minus the local annual average external air temperature" (clause 3.4.1(c)); for a suspended floor, the Table 2 design external temperature is used (clause 3.4.1(d)).

### Room by room

MIS 3005-D does not use the phrase "room by room". What it does require is that, before installation starts, the installer is given in writing "All specific room heat losses (in W/m2)" (clause 3.8.1(a)) and "The design emitter temperature based on the worst performing room" (clause 3.8.1(d)). The Carbon Trust's report for DESNZ on the Heat Pump Ready programme states that a room-by-room assessment "is still required by BS EN 12831 (the standard required by MCS)" to size heat emitters correctly (Improving the installer journey from survey and design to aftercare, June 2025, p. 8).

So expect a list of rooms, each with its heat loss and emitter, not one figure for the house.

### Not the EPC

MIS 3005-D does not mention RdSAP, SAP or the EPC. The design heat load is a separate calculation to a separate standard, using the standard's own design temperatures. Do not quote an EPC figure to a householder as the size of heat pump they need.

Clause 3.1.4 adds that "The contractor shall ensure the building (including curtilage) is assessed by a competent professional", confirming the works will not compromise Building Regulations compliance. Your retrofit assessment may inform that, but it does not replace it.

> **Wider view: measured heat loss and the survey calculation**
>
> The MEASURED project ("The role of measured building performance in heat pump specification, system design and management") was funded through DESNZ's Heat Pump Ready programme. It was led by Build Test Solutions with Veritherm UK and Elmhurst Energy Services Ltd (gov.uk, Heat Pump Ready programme stream 2 projects).
>
> The project report says "A field trial compared measured heat loss with a standard survey and calculation methodology across 56 properties." It reports that standard survey-based heat loss calculations (the report names BS EN 12831 as the established method) were "accurate in only 30% of cases", or "accurate relative to the measured data for around 30% of homes"; that they "overestimated heat losses in 59% of cases" and "underestimated the heat loss in 11% of homes tested" (Build Test Solutions, Improving the survey and design process: MEASURED). The measured figure was a heat transfer coefficient from the SmartHTC method, which the Carbon Trust describes as using "internal temperature data and smart meter readings over a three-week period", "validated against co-heating tests" (June 2025, section 1.4, p. 5).
>
> Three limits apply. Neither document defines "accurate" or gives the tolerance used. Neither states when the trial took place or how the homes were chosen, beyond "a mixture of building archetypes". And the Carbon Trust notes that "there is not currently an approved way to incorporate measured heat loss data into BS EN 12831 survey assessments", with two CIBSE working groups set up to look at it (p. 8).
>
> **What it means for your work:** a design heat load is a calculation with assumptions in it, and in this one 56-home sample most survey calculations came out higher than the measured figure. That says nothing about any particular design. Record the design heat load as the designer's figure, attributed to them, without calling it right or wrong; the design standard still requires the survey-based calculation.

## Section 2: Sizing, Flow Temperature and Hybrids

**In short:** MIS 3005-D:2025 asks for a heat pump that provides at least 100% of the calculated heat load without a supplementary electric heater, and an air source system that holds temperature through defrost cycles. High temperature heat pumps should be avoided unless a flow above 55°C is needed; above 55°C, a 55°C-or-lower alternative design should also be provided and explained. In a hybrid, the heat pump covers at least 55% of the load.

### At least 100% of the load

Clause 3.4.1(e): "A heat pump shall be selected that will provide at least 100% of the calculated heat load", taking account of the flow temperature at the heat pump and "without input from any supplementary electric heater". Performance data from the manufacturer and "the emitter system designer should be provided".

Two further rules go with it:
- "Any supplementary electric heater shall be designed to not operate above the external temperatures in Table 2" (clause 3.4.1(b)).
- "An air source heat pump system should be able to maintain the internal design temperatures across multiple defrost cycles" (clause 3.4.1(f)).

### The 55°C threshold

MIS 3005-D does not set an absolute ceiling on flow temperature. It sets 55°C as the point beyond which a design has to justify itself:
- "The selection of High Temperature Heat Pumps (HTHPs) should be avoided" unless "the application requires a flow temperature higher than 55oC" (clause 3.4.3).
- "Where a system design proposes a flow temperature greater than 55oC", an alternative design at 55°C or lower "should also be provided", and the differences in efficiency and energy consumption "shall be explained to the customer" so they can choose (clause 3.4.4).

The note to clause 3.4.3 recognises why a higher temperature may sometimes be needed: "Listed buildings or other design limitations may prevent the use of larger heat emitters."

The design flow temperature is one of the items given to the installer: "The design flow temperature of the water leaving the heat pump and before any blending valves" (clause 3.8.1(f)). So is the efficiency figure that goes with it: "The installation's SCoP/SPER (as taken from the MCS website) based on the above design flow temperature", when supplying space heating "at the external design temperature" (clause 3.8.1(g)). For the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, Ofgem's installer guidance says air-to-water and ground source heat pumps "must achieve an in situ seasonal coefficient of performance (SCOP) of at least 2.8", calculated with the "MCS SCOP calculator (MCS026)" (BUS Installer Guidance V5.1, para 3.83).

### Hybrids

Clause 3.4.2 covers hybrid systems, where a heat pump works with another heat source:
- "The combined output of all heat sources shall be not less than 100% of the calculated heat load" (3.4.2(d)).
- "The heat pump power output (kW) shall be selected such that it will provide a minimum of 55% of the calculated heat load", with that output rated "at 55oC flow temperature for the device (as taken from the MCS database)", at the design external temperature for the location, even if the emitter design is based on a different flow temperature (3.4.2(d)).
- "The control philosophy shall be capable of prioritising heat pump utilisation" (3.4.2(e)).
- "All combined heat sources shall be fully and operationally integrated into a single master control system" (3.4.2(f)).

Whether a hybrid attracts a grant is a separate question. Ofgem's BUS guidance says "Fossil fuel hybrids are not eligible", including heat pumps installed alongside a separate fossil fuel boiler ("retrofit hybrids") and heat pumps with an integrated fossil fuel boiler (para 3.95). "Where a hybrid-capable heat pump is installed as a standalone unit (without the presence of a fossil fuel appliance), it may be eligible for BUS" (para 3.96).

### The network and the Building Regulations

Clause 3.1.7 says "The MCS Contractor shall ensure that the installation is notified to the Distribution Network Operator", and that permission to connect is sought where necessary, following ENA procedures. Clause 3.1.6 says that where the contractor is responsible for notification under the Building Regulations, it "shall ensure" notification "has been completed prior to handing over the installation".

### Approved Document L: which edition

In England, a new Approved Document L, "Energy and greenhouse gas emissions (2026)", was published on 24 March 2026 and revised on 8 September 2026. The gov.uk page says: "Earlier versions of Approved Document L continue to apply to buildings subject to previous regulatory standards."

The government's circular of 24 March 2026 sets the dates. The Building Regulations etc. (Amendment) (England) Regulations 2026 (SI 2026/335) come into force generally on 24 March 2027 and apply only to building work in England (paras 1 and 8). They "will not apply to work on a building where a building notice" (or initial notice, or full plans) was given before that date, provided the work starts before 24 March 2028 (para 16). Annex C withdraws the 2021 edition of Volume 1 "and all amendments to it" from 24 March 2027, subject to those provisions.

## Section 3: Emitters at Lower Flow Temperatures

**In short:** A radiator's output depends on how much warmer its water is than the room, and it falls faster than that difference does. In the IDHEE Heat Emitter Supplement's worked example, a radiator run with a mean water temperature 21 K above the room gives about a third of its catalogue output. That is why designs at lower flow temperatures often need larger emitters. Record emitters as found and the design flow temperature only where documents support it.

### Why output falls

The Home Energy Model's emitter paper gives the relationship as P = c × (T_E − T_rm)^n, where T_E is "the mean emitter temperature", T_rm is the air temperature in the room or zone, and c and n are constants "from the characteristic equation of emitters derived from BS EN 442 tests". The paper introduces the equation "In the case of radiators", taking it from the 2020 ASHRAE Handbook, but gives no value of n for radiators (DESNZ, HEM-TP-16, Modelling heat emitters within the Home Energy Model, v2.0, January 2026, section 2.1 and Annex A). For underfloor heating, "The exponent n is set equal to 1" (section 2.1); for fan coils, output is looked up from manufacturer data (introduction to the methodology).

Two things follow. Output depends on the mean water temperature, not the flow temperature alone. And where n is above 1, as the radiator factors below imply, output falls faster than the temperature difference.

### Worked numbers

Catalogue outputs are given at "standard conditions of 75°C flow 65°C return, 20°C room temperature", a mean water temperature of 70°C and a 50 K difference (BEIS, Domestic Heat Distribution Systems: Evidence Gathering, Research Paper 2021/015, February 2021, executive summary, footnote 4). The IDHEE's Heat Emitter Supplement to the Domestic Heating Design Guide also quotes outputs "at 50 t degC (50K)" (Issue 11/13). For other differences it does not print a factor table: it says "Conversion factors for other mw-air differences are given in Table 10.1 of the Domestic Heating Design Guide", which was not opened. The factors below are the ones the Supplement itself applies to radiators in its worked examples:

| Mean water to room difference | Factor applied to the 50 K output | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 50 K | 1.000 | Rating basis |
| 39 K | 0.724 | Supplement, column radiator example |
| 30 K | 0.515 | Supplement, panel radiator example ("Factor f1 from Table 10.1") |
| 21 K | 0.324 | Supplement, column radiator example |

The Supplement states an exponent only for skirting heating (n = 1.30) and a fan coil (n = 1.10); it gives none for radiators, and BS EN 442 was not opened. The radiator factors above happen to match (ΔT/50)^1.3 to three decimal places (for example, (21/50)^1.3 = 0.324; this course's arithmetic), but the course relies on the factors as printed, not on an exponent. In the panel radiator example the Supplement also applies a further factor from the Guide's Table 10.2 (0.96, giving 0.494 overall), so real output can be lower still. Treat the figures as illustrations; real emitters differ.

The Supplement's own example is a four-column radiator in a room at 21°C with a heat loss of 2,120 W:
- "Output for 50K m.w.- air t 117 x 24 sections = 2808 Watts".
- "The boiler is to be replaced by a heat pump providing a flow temperature of 45°C and return of 39°C." The mean water temperature is 42°C, so the difference is 21 K: "2808 x 0.324 = 909 Watts".
- "To achieve 2120 Watts from the radiator the mean water temperature will need to be 61°C."
- "Therefore a 65°C flow temperature from the heat pump would be required if the radiator size is not to be increased."

The Supplement's check line works at 60°C rather than 61°C, a small inconsistency in the source. The arithmetic below is this course's own. To deliver 2,120 W at a 21 K difference, the radiator would need a 50 K rating of 2,120 ÷ 0.324 = about 6,540 W, roughly 2.3 times the existing one. At a 30 K difference (a mean water temperature of 51°C in a 21°C room) it would need 2,120 ÷ 0.515 = about 4,120 W, roughly 1.5 times.

| Mean water temperature (room 21°C) | Difference | Output of the 2,808 W radiator | Share of 2,120 W heat loss met |
|---|---|---|---|
| 71°C | 50 K (the rating point) | 2,808 W | all |
| 60°C | 39 K | 2,808 × 0.724 = 2,033 W | about 96% |
| 51°C | 30 K | 2,808 × 0.515 = 1,446 W | about 68% |
| 42°C | 21 K | 2,808 × 0.324 = 909 W (as the Supplement prints it; 909.8 unrounded) | about 43% |

The 39 K and 21 K rows repeat the Supplement's own figures for this radiator. The 30 K row applies the factor from its panel radiator example to this radiator, which the Supplement does not do. Shares are this course's arithmetic.

### What the stock looks like

The BEIS heat distribution study collected data from "515 domestic properties in the UK" and modelled the stock with wet central heating. It estimated that "10% of dwellings in the UK are already suitable, on a peak winter day, for heat pumps with a 55°C flow temperature", and "only 1% are suitable for a heat pump with a flow temperature of 45°C". "On an average winter day, 53% of dwellings could use a 55°C flow temperature, and 6% could use a 45°C flow temperature, with no changes to their heat emitters or flow rates."

The study adds that allowing for "reductions in radiator performance over time or additional heat demand above what is calculated by SAP", "these figures are significantly reduced" (executive summary, February 2021).

So in many existing homes, a design at 55°C or lower is likely to involve changing some emitters. Because the design emitter temperature is "based on the worst performing room" (clause 3.8.1(d)), one under-sized room can set the flow temperature for the whole house. Which emitters change is the designer's decision, recorded as "The type and dimensions of emitter(s)" (clause 3.8.1(c)).

### What you record

RdSAP convention 4.01b (Conventions v12.2 for RdSAP 10, applicable from 6 August 2026) says "The design flow temperature for condensing boilers and heat-pumps should be recorded as unknown" "unless there is documentary evidence that the system has been designed and commissioned as a low temperature one." Radiator sizes alone are not that evidence; a design and commissioning record is. Convention 4.01a adds: "If Heat Pump serves both radiators and underfloor, enter radiators when they are serving 50% or more of the total floor area."

On site, record emitter type by room and any radiators that look recently replaced, as observations. Do not tell a householder their radiators are "too small"; that judgement belongs to the designer.

## Section 4: Hot Water

**In short:** MIS 3005-D:2025 sizes daily hot water demand at 45 litres times N, where N is the number of bedrooms plus one or the number of known occupants, whichever is greater. The heat pump shall be capable of at least 55°C flow at design conditions, and an existing cylinder's insulation shall be upgraded, with its size meeting the heat pump maker's requirements. Systems shall include a means to prevent bacterial growth. Field data link heavy immersion use with lower efficiency.

### Sizing the demand

Clause 3.5.2 gives the design daily hot water volume as "Vd,average = 45 x N". N is the greater of "The number of bedrooms + 1" or "The number of known occupants". For new builds, where occupancy is unknown, the bedroom figure is used. So the basis is 45 litres per notional person, with a floor set by the number of bedrooms.

Two examples, this course's arithmetic:

| Home | Bedrooms + 1 | Known occupants | N | Design daily volume |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Three bedrooms, couple | 4 | 2 | 4 | 45 × 4 = 180 litres |
| Three bedrooms, family of five | 4 | 5 | 5 | 45 × 5 = 225 litres |

Clause 3.5.3 then says "an appropriately sized hot water cylinder should be specified", based on that demand and the heat pump's capacity. The demand figure and the cylinder specification, with its recovery rate, are both given to the installer before work starts (clause 3.8.1(b) and (e)). Clause 3.5.1 says non-domestic buildings use a method based on building use and outlets.

### The cylinder and its coil

- "The specification of the heat exchanger for the domestic hot water cylinder shall follow" the manufacturer's recommendations. The note to clause 3.5.4 says heat pump coils generally need more surface area than coils designed for boilers.
- "the heat pump shall be capable of a flow temperature of at least 55oC at design conditions" (clause 3.5.5).

Where an existing cylinder is kept, clause 3.5.6 says:
- "The thermal insulation of the hot water cylinder, and all pipes connected to it, shall be upgraded" to at least the standard for new installations (a).
- The cylinder is to be "fit for purpose" mechanically, with scale, corrosion and the extra stress of the heat pump connection considered (b).
- "The size of the cylinder shall comply with the heat pump manufacturer's requirements" (d).

So an existing cylinder that suited a boiler may not suit a heat pump. Whether it can stay is the designer's call.

### Bacterial growth

Clause 3.5.7: "Domestic hot water systems shall incorporate a means to prevent bacterial growth (including legionella bacteria)." Where periodic pasteurisation is used, "a bacterial risk assessment can help determine how frequent this pasteurisation should occur." The standard does not set a frequency.

This course could not open HSE's guidance on hot water temperatures, so it does not quote storage or outlet temperatures. If a householder or landlord asks about legionella control, recommend they ask the installer how the system does it and, for a rented home, that the landlord takes professional advice on their duties.

### The immersion trap

The government-funded RHPP field trial (heat pumps monitored 2013 to 2015; final report March 2017) found: "On average, where measured, immersion heater electricity consumption was 12% of the total, but more than half of the sites with SPFH4<2 had immersion heating use > 20% of total electricity" (section 5, p. 21). It says excessive direct electric immersion use "has a direct adverse effect on SPFH4".

The same report found that between a quarter and a third of 220 metered immersion heaters showed operation "consistent with regular sterilisation", mostly weekly or daily (section 7.5, p. 28), and recommended controls that limit resistance heating "whilst still ensuring regular sterilisation" (section 8, p. 31). These installations pre-date MIS 3005-D:2025, so the figures describe that trial, not current practice. They show why the pasteurisation method and frequency is a design question with a running cost.

### What you record

Record the cylinder as found: size if labelled, insulation, any immersion heater and its timer. If the householder says the immersion runs daily, record it as "householder reports", without a conclusion.

## Section 5: Noise and Siting

**In short:** Permitted development for an air source heat pump requires the MCS 020(a) sound calculation to show no more than 37 dB LAeq,5mins at 1 m from a neighbour's habitable room window or door, combining two units that share an assessment position. England's rules changed on 29 May 2025 and Wales's on 1 June 2026; both allow two units on a detached house and 1.5 m³ units on houses, and both dropped the boundary distances. Listed buildings fall outside both.

### MCS 020(a): the sound calculation

MCS 020(a) is titled "Air Source Heat Pump Sound Calculation (For Permitted Development Installations)". Issue 1.1 is dated 14 April 2025. Its main features:
- **The limit** is 37 dB LAeq,5mins (clause 1.2), applied as 37.0 dB(A) in its Tables 1 and 2 and Step 8.
- **The assessment position** is "1m external to the centre point of any door or window to a habitable room of a neighbouring property", measured perpendicular to it (Section 2, definitions), with the limit applied "ignoring the effect of the façade" (clause 1.2). Step 2 says assessment positions should cover neighbouring properties on all sides and include ground floor, first floor and so on (Section 3).
- **Two heat pumps** sharing an assessment position are each calculated, then combined by "logarithmic summation", and the total is compared with the same 37.0 dB(A) limit (Table 2, Steps 7 and 8). Two units far apart may have different assessment positions and are then considered individually (Step 2).
- **Barriers** count only if solid: a Type 1 barrier is a solid brick or masonry wall or a solid fence at least 18 mm thick with no cracks or gaps, extending 1 m or more beyond either edge of the heat pump, and a Type 2 is a solid fence less than 18 mm thick. Open fences, fences with gaps and cracks, vegetation and walls or fences in poor repair do not count (Step 6).
- **Background noise** is not part of the calculation.

Clause 1.3 says compliance with the standard alone "does not bestow permitted development rights"; the other conditions in the planning legislation still apply. Clause 1.4 says contractors "shall be under a duty to ensure compliance" for permitted development installations.

MCS's consultation response explains the change. The earlier MCS 020 used a "Permitted Development noise limit of 42 dB(A)" with an assumed 40 dB background. Removing the background steps (7 to 9) left "a corresponding overall limit of 37dB(A)". The new method uses "exact distances", gives vegetation "no reduction", and applies "despite the distance to a neighbouring property" (MCS 020 Consultation Response findings).

**37 or 40?** MCS's news article on the Welsh changes (22 May 2026) says the MCS 020 a) limits "will apply to two units in this case" and "the two units in operation together must not exceed the 40db limit". MCS 020(a) Issue 1.1 contains no 40 dB figure and applies 37.0 dB(A) to the combined level of two units. This course follows the standard; the article does not explain its figure.

MIS 3005-D:2025 clause 3.1.3 still refers to "MCS 020 Planning Standards" for installations in England under permitted development. It was issued on 1 January 2025, before the England change to MCS 020(a) described below.

### Permitted development in England and Wales

In England, the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) (Amendment) Order 2025, SI 2025 No. 560, was made on 6 May 2025 and came into force on 29 May 2025. It amended Part 14, Class G of the 2015 Order. It redefined the MCS Planning Standards as "MCS 020 a)" (article 6), removed "or equivalent standards" from the MCS condition, with a transitional provision that ran "until the end of 28th May 2026" (articles 5(2) and 7), and added a condition that the heat pump "is not solely used for the purpose of cooling" (G.3(aa)).

Other conditions require siting that, so far as practicable, minimises the effect on external appearance and amenity, and removal when no longer needed (G.3(b) to (d)). MCS says compliance with MCS 020(a) under permitted development "will apply irrespective of whether it's an MCS certified installation or not" (29 May 2025).

In Wales, the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (Amendment) (Wales) Order 2026, SI 2026 No. 79, was made on 10 March 2026, and its heat pump articles came into force on 1 June 2026. It amends Part 40, Class G in a similar way, including a condition that the heat pump "must not be solely used for the purpose of cooling" (G.3(a)) and a redefinition of the MCS Planning Standards as MCS 020(a) (article 6). The Order contains no decibel figure. The pitched roof, flat roof and wind turbine limbs in Wales were read only on the Welsh Government's guidance page (gov.wales, Planning permission: heat pumps).

| Rule | England (SI 2025/560, from 29 May 2025) | Wales (SI 2026 No. 79, from 1 June 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Number of units | 1; 2 on a detached house (G.2(a)) | 1; 2 on a detached house (G.2(a)) |
| Outdoor unit, house | 1.5 m³ | 1.5 m³ (G.2(c)) |
| Outdoor unit, block of flats | 0.6 m³ | 1 m³ (G.2(c)) |
| Boundary distance | Removed (previously 1 m) | Removed (previously 3 m) |
| Sound | MCS 020(a) | MCS 020(a) |
| Cooling | Not solely for cooling | Not solely for cooling |
| Listed building | Excluded, including on a building or land within the curtilage (G.2(i)) | The same: excluded on a building or on land within the curtilage if the house or block "is a listed building" (G.2(g)) |
| Conservation area | With World Heritage Sites: not on a wall or roof fronting a highway, or nearer a bounding highway than the building | Not on a wall or roof fronting a highway, or nearer a bounding highway (G.2(i)) |
| Highway wall elsewhere | Not above ground floor storey level | Not above ground floor storey level (G.2(j)) |
| Roofs | Not on a pitched roof; not within 1 m of a flat roof's edge | The same (guidance page) |
| Wind turbine | Excluded on the building or in the curtilage (G.2(b), (c)) | Excluded if a standalone wind turbine is already installed (guidance page) |
| Scheduled monument | Excluded (G.2(h)) | Not in SI 2026 No. 79 or on the guidance page; the unamended Welsh limbs were not read |

**The boundary rules have gone.** MCS's consultation response recorded that in England "the rule is one metre", and in Wales heat pumps had to be "at least three metres from the site boundary". SI 2025/560 omitted G.2(e) and G.3(a); the omitted wording was not shown in the legislation as read, and MCS describes the change as enabling "heat pumps to be installed within 1m of the property boundary". The Welsh Explanatory Memorandum lists "Removing the requirement for ASHPs to be installed a minimum of 3 metres from a boundary" (11 March 2026, Part 1, para 17). Older guidance that quotes either distance is out of date.

MCS's Welsh article says the Welsh Government intends to add a condition that any acoustic barrier needed for compliance "must be permanently retained and maintained". No such condition appears in SI 2026 No. 79 as read. A unit outside these rights may still be possible with planning permission, which is a question for the installer and the local planning authority.

### What you record

You do not decide whether a unit is permitted development. Record the property type (detached or not, house or flat), whether you know or are told it is listed or in a conservation area, and, where a unit is already fitted, its position (wall or ground, elevation, whether it fronts a highway) with photographs. If a householder asks whether they need planning permission, recommend they ask the installer and, if unsure, the local planning authority.

## Section 6: Installer Standards and Consumer Protection

**In short:** MIS 3005-D:2025 is mandatory for contractors certified to MCS:2025. For the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, Ofgem's guidance requires an MCS certified installer and membership of a listed consumer code; MCS:2025 itself is now one of those codes, with a transition deadline of 31 March 2027. The Warm Homes: Local Grant in England adds TrustMark registration, PAS 2035:2023 and lodgement by a retrofit coordinator.

### MCS

MIS 3005-D:2025 is "mandatory for MCS Contractors certified to MCS: 2025" (foreword). On 18 June 2026 MCS reported that DESNZ "has formally recognised the robust consumer protection offered by MCS's redeveloped installer Scheme" as a Code of Practice for the Boiler Upgrade Scheme. The article refers to "centralising complaints management, strengthening financial protection" and contacting every recipient of an MCS certified installation. "The transition phase will last until 31 March 2027", the deadline for all MCS certified installers to move to the redeveloped scheme.

### The Boiler Upgrade Scheme

Ofgem's Boiler Upgrade Scheme: Installer Guidance V5.1 (2 July 2026) says:
- "Installers must be MCS certified to install the heating system they are applying for" (para 3.01).
- Systems must meet "the relevant MCS standards approved and published by the Secretary of State" (para 3.17), and "Eligibility under BUS is assessed against the MCS standards that are in force at the time the system is commissioned" (para 3.18).
- MCS certified installation companies "must also be a member of a consumer code" (para 2.08). The codes listed are the Home Insulation and Energy Systems Contractors Scheme (HIES), the Renewable Energy Consumer Code (RECC) and MCS:2025 (para 2.09).
- Installers under the redeveloped MCS scheme "are therefore no longer required to hold a separate consumer code membership" (para 2.17); those still on the previous scheme need HIES or RECC membership (para 2.18).

### The Warm Homes: Local Grant (England)

The DESNZ policy guidance for local authorities (2026) says:
- "Installers must be Trustmark registered and certified under the latest version of PAS 2030 or by MCS", with MCS certification required for low carbon heating (section 7.1).
- "All upgrades must be compliant with the latest PAS 2035 guidance (currently PAS 2035:2023)" (section 7.1).
- "Installations that do not use products that are listed in the MCS Product Directory, will not be eligible" (section 7.6).
- "All measures must be lodged onto the TrustMark data warehouse by a retrofit coordinator" (section 7.6).

### Where you fit

On a funded PAS 2035 project, the coordinator holds the documents together and, under the Warm Homes: Local Grant, lodges the measures. On an EPC visit you may be the only independent professional to see the paperwork. Either way, record what is there and, if something worries the householder, point them to the installer first, then the installer's consumer code or scheme. You do not judge whether an installer complied with a standard.

## Section 7: What to Ask For, What to Record

**In short:** Before an install starts, MIS 3005-D:2025 clause 3.8.1 says the installer is given certain design information in writing, and lists what it is. You should ask whether it exists and record what you see, attributed to its author. Record the design flow temperature in RdSAP only where documents show a low temperature design and commissioning. Use red for professional advice now, amber for gaps to raise, and green for documents in place.

### The design pack

Clause 3.8.1 lists what the installer is given in writing before installation starts:

| Item | Clause 3.8.1 |
|---|---|
| All specific room heat losses (W/m²) | (a) |
| Calculated daily hot water demand | (b) |
| Type and dimensions of emitters | (c) |
| Design emitter temperature, based on the worst performing room | (d) |
| Specification and recovery rate of any new cylinder | (e) |
| Design flow temperature leaving the heat pump, before any blending valves | (f) |
| Installation SCoP/SPER from the MCS website at that flow temperature | (g) |
| Any metering that may be needed for financial incentives | (h) |
| For ground source: site plan of the ground heat exchanger and a completed Table 4 | (i) |

The standard does not say this pack goes to the customer; the only customer-facing requirement read is the explanation in clause 3.4.4. If a householder has the pack, it is the best evidence you will see. If not, they can ask their installer for it.

### Questions to ask

- Before an install: is there a heat load calculation with room heat losses; what design flow temperature was chosen, and if above 55°C, was an alternative offered; which emitters change; is the cylinder kept; has the noise calculation been done?
- After an install: is there a commissioning record stating the design flow temperature, enough for RdSAP convention 4.01b; how is pasteurisation set, if the householder knows?

### Traffic light

A way to present findings, recommended by this course rather than a published scheme. Red is advisory: it means recommend professional advice now, not that you have found a fault.

| Light | Examples | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Green: in place | Design pack seen with room heat losses, design flow temperature and emitter schedule; commissioning record states the design flow temperature | Record the documents by title, date and author; enter the design flow temperature in RdSAP only as convention 4.01b allows |
| Amber: raise | No design pack seen; design above 55°C with no alternative mentioned; householder unsure whether an existing cylinder was assessed; outdoor unit near a neighbour's window and no noise calculation seen | Record as "not seen" or "householder reports"; recommend the householder asks the installer for the documents |
| Red: get professional advice | Water leaking from a cylinder or its pipework; damaged electrical connections or wiring to the outdoor unit; signs of refrigerant pipework damage; householder reports no heat in cold weather and no response from the installer | Record, tell the occupant, recommend the installer or a qualified professional now, in writing |

An illustration written for this course, not a prescribed format: an EPC visit to a 1960s semi-detached house with an air-to-water heat pump fitted last year.

"Air source heat pump, outdoor unit on rear elevation at ground level, approx. 2 m from side boundary fence (solid timber). ## Key Takeaways

- MIS 3005-D:2025's heat load calculation uses Table 1 internal temperatures, Table 2 external temperatures and BS EN 12831-1; the installer gets room heat losses and a design emitter temperature set by the worst performing room. The EPC is not the design heat load.
- In the MEASURED trial of 56 homes, survey calculations were reported accurate in around 30%, high in 59% and low in 11%, with "accurate" not defined.
- A heat pump meets at least 100% of the load without a supplementary heater; in a hybrid, the heat pump at least 55%, rated at 55°C flow. Above 55°C, a 55°C-or-lower alternative should also be provided and explained.
- At a 21 K mean water to room difference, a radiator gives about a third of its 50 K output (IDHEE factor 0.324).
- Hot water design demand is 45 × N litres a day, and systems shall include a means to prevent bacterial growth.
- MCS 020(a): 37 dB LAeq,5mins at 1 m from a neighbour's habitable room window or door, two units combined where they share an assessment position. The 1 m (England) and 3 m (Wales) boundary rules have gone; listed buildings are outside permitted development in both.
- Approved Document L 2021/2023 applies in England until the 2026 edition comes into force on 24 March 2027, subject to transitional provisions.
- Record design flow temperature in RdSAP only on documentary evidence (convention 4.01b).

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** Under MIS 3005-D:2025, what must the heat pump in a hybrid system provide?

A) At least 100% of the calculated heat load at 45°C flow
B) At least 55% of the calculated heat load, with its output rated at 55°C flow
C) At least 55% of the annual hot water demand
D) Whatever share the customer chooses, provided controls are fitted

**Correct answer: B.** Clause 3.4.2(d) says the heat pump "shall be selected such that it will provide a minimum of 55% of the calculated heat load", rated "at 55°C flow temperature", with all sources together providing at least 100% (Section 2).

---

**Q2.** A design proposes a flow temperature of 65°C. What does MIS 3005-D:2025 say should happen?

A) The design is not allowed under any circumstances
B) An alternative design at 55°C or lower should also be provided, and the efficiency and energy differences explained to the customer
C) The installer must fit underfloor heating instead
D) Nothing; the standard does not mention flow temperatures

**Correct answer: B.** Clause 3.4.4 asks for an alternative design at 55°C or lower and says the differences "shall be explained to the customer". Clause 3.4.3 says high temperature heat pumps should be avoided unless the application needs more than 55°C (Section 2).

---

**Q3.** Using the IDHEE Heat Emitter Supplement factor of 0.324, what does a radiator rated at 2,808 W at 50 K give with water at 45°C flow and 39°C return in a 21°C room?

A) About 2,808 W
B) About 2,033 W
C) About 1,446 W
D) About 909 W

**Correct answer: D.** The mean water temperature is 42°C, a 21 K difference from the room, so 2,808 × 0.324 = 909 W (Section 3).

---

**Q4.** A three-bedroom home has two known occupants. What is the design daily hot water demand under MIS 3005-D:2025 clause 3.5.2?

A) 90 litres
B) 135 litres
C) 180 litres
D) 225 litres

**Correct answer: C.** N is the greater of bedrooms plus one (4) and known occupants (2), so 45 × 4 = 180 litres (Section 4).

---

**Q5.** A householder in Wales asks whether their new heat pump can go 2 m from the side boundary under permitted development. Which statement matches the sources?

A) No; Wales still requires 3 m from the boundary
B) The 3 m boundary requirement was removed from 1 June 2026; the unit must still meet the other conditions, including MCS 020(a), and you should recommend they confirm with the installer
C) Yes, any unit anywhere on the plot is permitted
D) Only if the house is listed

**Correct answer: B.** The Welsh Explanatory Memorandum lists "Removing the requirement for ASHPs to be installed a minimum of 3 metres from a boundary". The other conditions in SI 2026 No. 79, including MCS 020(a) and the listed building exclusion, still apply, and the assessor does not decide permitted development (Section 5).
