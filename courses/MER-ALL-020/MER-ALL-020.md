## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- Describe how a rendered external wall insulation (EWI) system is built up, and why its edges matter more than its face.
- Name the six zones where EWI defects concentrate and say what a sound detail and a doubtful detail look like in each.
- Explain why each zone fails, using published industry and warranty-provider guidance, and record what you see in terms someone else can act on.
- Summarise what the National Audit Office found about EWI installed under ECO4 and the Great British Insulation Scheme (GBIS), and what the free TrustMark audit route offers a householder.
- Recognise when to stop describing and recommend professional advice, without diagnosing, without condemning an installation and without telling anyone to open up a wall.

## Introduction

External wall insulation is the measure most likely to change how a house looks, and, if it went in under ECO4 or GBIS, the measure most likely to need putting right. The National Audit Office (NAO) reported in October 2025 that the government and Ofgem estimate 98% of homes with external wall insulation under those schemes have major issues requiring remediation (Energy efficiency installations under the Energy Company Obligation, HC 1334, summary paragraph 11).

Most of what goes wrong is visible from the ground if you know where to look. The face of a rendered wall rarely fails on its own. Failures cluster at the edges and interruptions: where the system meets the roof, the windows, the ground and the neighbour's house, and wherever a pipe, cable or bracket passes through it. This course takes you round those edges.

It does not train you to inspect or certify an installation. A TrustMark audit, a system designer or a surveyor does that. Your job is to recognise and record, to describe what is possible or likely, and to point the householder or client to the right route. MER-ALL-018 covers damp, condensation and thermal bridging in depth; this course refers to it rather than repeating it.

### Where this course applies

This course is written for England and Wales:
- The Approved Documents cited are England editions: Approved Document L Volume 1 (2021 edition incorporating 2023 amendments, "for use in England") and the June 2022 amendment booklet to Approved Document B. Wales publishes its own Approved Documents; the Welsh editions of B and L were not opened for this course.
- ECO4 and GBIS operate across Great Britain. The Welsh Government's written statement of 23 January 2025 says affected households will receive a letter from Ofgem setting out steps to resolve any issues.
- The industry and warranty guidance cited (INCA, LABC Warranty, BRE) is not regulation. It is cited as the guidance of the body that published it.

## Section 1: How an EWI System Is Built Up

**In short:** A rendered EWI system can add 70 to 160 mm to the wall, so everything on the old face, from the roof edge to the flue, has to be re-detailed. At every edge, ask what would stop the water if the visible sealant failed: INCA does not accept sealant as the sole means of a weather tight seal, so if only mastic is visible, record it. Each edge must also keep the insulation reasonably continuous.

### The layers

LABC Warranty's technical update on external wall insulation (March 2022) and the Welsh Government's householder planning guide (October 2013) describe the same build-up, from the wall outwards:
- **Substrate.** LABC Warranty says solid masonry must be in "sound condition and free from dampness".
- **Adhesive.** LABC Warranty: the "dot and dab method of fixing using adhesives is not acceptable on masonry substrates"; it expects a full-face coat. The BRE guide prepared for the Welsh Government (External Wall Insulation Project Guide, 16 April 2018, p.30) says it is "preferable to also use adhesive on the insulation boards in addition to mechanical fixings".
- **Insulation boards.** LABC Warranty lists EPS, XPS, phenolic and mineral wool.
- **Mechanical fixings.** LABC Warranty says fixing density should be designed on a site-specific basis by a suitably qualified structural engineer, and a pull-out test report confirming fixing type and length will be required.
- **Reinforcing mesh in a basecoat.** LABC Warranty: "Reinforcement mesh should be accommodated throughout the base coat application", with additional reinforcement at vulnerable areas and the corners of openings, and an additional layer of mesh where there are concerns about straight-line joints.
- **Finish.** Render, or another finish such as brick slips. LABC Warranty accepts only the render system named on the system's third-party certificate.
- **Beads, trims and closures** at every edge.

The Welsh Government guide says the system "can add between 70mm and 160mm in depth", and that "All external pipe work and other fittings have to be removed and replaced during this process" (p.8).

That sentence is the key to this course. Moving the face of the wall out by 70 to 160 mm means everything that sat on the old face has to be re-detailed: the roof edge, the sills, the base, the gutters and downpipes, the meter box, the flue.

### Why the edges matter more than the face

Historic England (Energy Efficiency and Historic Buildings: Insulating Solid Walls, v1.1, April 2016, Section 3.4) says external insulation "should normally be considered as a two-component system where all layers need to work together in harmony", and that impermeable systems "often depend on mastic sealants which can have a limited lifespan".

INCA's weathering guide does not accept sealants as "the sole means of providing a weather tight seal"; they need a backing such as compressed hydrophobic tape or backing rod, and its window and sill details require double seals. Its roofline principles require "at least two lines of weathering protection".

So the question at every edge is: if the visible sealant bead failed tomorrow, what would stop the water? If all you can see is a line of mastic, record it.

### Two jobs at every edge

Each edge detail must keep rain out and keep the insulation reasonably continuous. Approved Document L Volume 1 paragraph 4.19 says: "When carrying out work in existing dwellings, care should be taken to reduce unwanted heat loss through thermal bridging." The BRE guide notes that "heat loss from poorly detailed junctions can be nearly 50% of that from all the fabric elements" and that "fRsi should be greater than 0.75 for residential dwellings" (p.23). MER-ALL-018 explains the temperature factor.

INCA rates its standard details green or amber for thermal bridging. Amber details carry increased condensation and mould risk, and INCA states that amber "will increase the assessed inherent technical risk level in table B2 of PAS 2035 by 1". This course uses its own traffic light for recording later; do not confuse the two.

### The regulatory frame, briefly

The Building Regulations 2010 define renovation of a thermal element as "the provision of a new layer in the thermal element or the replacement of an existing layer, but excludes decorative finishes" (regulation 2, as made). Regulation 23 (as at 1 October 2015, the version opened) requires that where the renovation of a thermal element constitutes a major renovation or amounts to "more than 50% of the element's surface area", the whole element meets the energy efficiency requirement "in so far as that is technically, functionally and economically feasible". Approved Document L Volume 1 Table 4.3 gives, for "Wall – internal or external insulation", a threshold of 0.70 W/m²K and an improved value of 0.30 W/m²K.

You will not check compliance with these. They explain why a building control record or system certificate should exist for most installations.

## Section 2: Reading an EWI House from the Ground

**In short:** Looking systematically from the ground and the rooms, you can record most edge defects, but not the adhesive, fixings, mesh or inside of the wall, so say so. Walk the six zones in the same order every time, then inside, recording internal mould and outside edges at the same location without concluding that one causes the other. For EWI on a building with a storey at or above 11 m, or any fire question, record and recommend professional advice.

### What you can and cannot see

On a domestic assessment you are on the ground, outside and in. What you can see from the pavement, the garden and the rooms is enough to record most edge defects, if you look systematically. What you cannot see is the adhesive, the fixings, the mesh and the inside of the wall. Say so: "System build-up not visible; not inspected" is an honest record.

### The six defect zones

The grouping is this course's; each zone matches standard details in INCA's weathering guide, failures listed by LABC Warranty, or examples in the NAO report:

| Zone | What to look at | Main sources |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Eaves and verges | Does the roof oversail the insulation; trims and sealant at the roof edge | INCA roofline bulletin and weathering guide; NAO Figure 6 |
| 2. Openings | Reveals, sills, heads, sealant at frames | INCA weathering guide; LABC Warranty |
| 3. Base and DPC | Base closure, DPC, ground levels, splash | LABC Warranty; BRE guide for the Welsh Government |
| 4. Render surface | Cracks, joint lines, staining, bulges, damage | LABC Warranty; BRE guide |
| 5. Penetrations and fixtures | Pipes, cables, flues, vents, meter boxes, brackets | INCA weathering guide; LABC Warranty; NAO Figure 6 |
| 6. Boundaries and corners | Stop ends at neighbours, steps, external corners | INCA weathering guide; BRE guide; Welsh Government guide |

Walk the house in the same order every time: roofline, each opening, base, surface, every penetration, the ends of the system. Then go inside and look at the same zones from within: reveals, the wall-to-ceiling junction below the eaves, skirting level, and walls next to the party wall.

### Inside tells you about outside

NAO summary paragraph 11 says the major EWI issues that affect performance often create "the risk of water ingress and mould". A strip of mould along the top of a bedroom wall, at the height of the eaves outside, is worth linking to the roofline in your notes. You are not concluding that one causes the other. You are recording both, at the same location, so whoever investigates can see the connection.

### Fire: where it enters a domestic assessment

From the Approved Document B amendment booklet (June 2022, for use in England from 1 December 2022):
- Regulation 7(2) requires "materials which become part of an external wall, or specified attachment, of a relevant building" to be class A2-s1, d0 or A1. A relevant building has a storey "at least 18 metres above ground level" (regulation 7(4)(a)).
- In buildings with a residential purpose and a storey 11 m or more in height, "any insulation product, filler material" in an external wall should be class A2-s1, d0 or better (paragraph 10.7).
- For residential buildings of 11 m or less, Table 10.1 gives "Class B-s3, d2 or better" for the external surface of a wall less than 1000 mm from the relevant boundary, and "No provisions" at 1000 mm or more.

LABC Warranty says boiler flues through EWI need sleeving in line with Approved Document B. The BRE guide says fire spread over EWI is "mainly only a concern in taller buildings (>18m)" (p.9).

For an assessor of houses and low-rise flats the point is narrow. If you see EWI on a building with a storey at or above 11 m, or are asked whether a system meets fire requirements, record what you see and recommend professional advice. Do not comment on the fire class of a product you cannot identify.

## Section 3: Zone 1, Eaves and Verges

**In short:** Check whether the roof edge visibly oversails the render, or only a sealant bead protects the top of the insulation. INCA calls an extended roofline, designed and installed appropriately, the most robust solution; the NAO gives eaves not effectively extended over the insulation as an example of a major issue affecting performance. Check whether gutters were reinstated and soffit vents covered, and look for mould at the top-floor ceiling line, without saying water is getting in.

### What a sound detail looks like

Adding insulation brings the face of the wall out towards, or beyond, the roof edge. Unless something protects it, rain off the roof and wind-driven rain at the verge will hit the top of the insulation.

INCA's roofline closure bulletin (2025) says extending the roofline over the insulation "remains the most robust solution when designed and installed in an appropriately suitable way". INCA's weathering guide shows this as eaves detail WRD-E001, rated green, and verge detail WRD-V002: "Roof extended as necessary to provide overhang to EWI system", weathering risk "Low".

The BRE guide for the Welsh Government recommends a "roof overhang to protect the insulation rather than relying on silicone sealant that will need to be re-applied over time" (p.23). It shows as a poor example EWI "capped at roof gable level, not protected by an inherent roof overhang" (Figure 4, p.36).

### When the roof is not extended

On a mid-terrace or half of a semi-detached pair the roof continues next door, so extension may not be feasible. INCA's bulletin records that traditional verge trims "were banned under PAS 2030:2017", and sets out a roofline closure route with design principles including:
- At least two lines of weathering protection; sealant never the primary barrier; every joint double-sealed.
- An overhang appropriate to exposure: "40 mm moderate or sheltered exposure – 50 mm severe or very severe exposure".
- Prefabricated corner, apex and connector units; site-formed mitres not permitted.
- Insulation continuity with the loft of at least 0.6 m²K/W.
- Repair of roof defects such as broken slates, ripped felt and cracked or missing fillets before installation.

The bulletin lists the failure modes of older verge trims, including "Over reliance on mastic / no secondary line of defence", cheapened components, large thermal bridges and difficulty maintaining trims at the head of the property.

### What the NAO found

NAO Figure 6 gives as an example of a major issue affecting performance insulation "where roof eaves have not been effectively extended to fully cover the insulation".

### Ventilation and the top of the wall

Extending or closing the eaves can block loft ventilation. INCA's WRD-E001 says "Ensure ventilation pathway is maintained", and the guide adds: "It is critical that cross-flow ventilation is maintained." The bulletin requires alternative ventilation to be designed where existing eaves ventilation will be covered.

INCA also requires loft insulation to extend across the top of the wall and the EWI to reach at least the height of the loft insulation. If it stops short, there is an uninsulated band at the top of the room below.

### What to look for and record

From the ground:
- Does the roof edge (tiles, fascia, gutter) visibly oversail the render at the eaves and each verge, or does the render run up to or past the roof edge?
- At gables, is there a trim capping the insulation? Are its joints overlapped, or open?
- Is the only visible seal a bead of sealant? Is it cracked or pulling away?
- Staining or streaking running down from the top edge.
- Were gutters and downpipes reinstated? NAO Figure 6 lists "Drainage and gutters have not been reinstated" as a minor issue.
- Have soffit or eaves vents been covered?

From inside: mould or staining at the junction of external wall and ceiling on the top floor, below the eaves or a verge.

"Roof does not appear to oversail the EWI at the rear eaves; top edge protected by sealant bead only, cracked in places; mould at ceiling line in rear bedroom below" is a complete record. It does not say water is getting in.

## Section 4: Zone 2, Window and Door Openings

**In short:** Openings interrupt the insulation, render, mesh and weathering line at once, and LABC Warranty says EWI at reveals often fails through poor detailing or support. Check whether the render returns into the reveal to the frame, whether each sill projects clearly beyond the render with end caps, and whether the head has a drip line. Record split or missing sealant and drainage slots covered by render, and inside, mould on reveals, at window corners or below the window.

### Why openings are the hardest detail

Every opening interrupts the insulation, the render, the mesh and the weathering line at once. LABC Warranty says: "The installation of EWIs at window/door reveals often fail due to poor detailing or support." Historic England (Section 2.3) says that when wall insulation is added, the weak points "are typically at window and door reveals". The BRE guide says reveal insulation "should be a fundamental part of any installation" (p.9).

### Reveals

INCA's weathering guide says insulation at reveals, heads and sills "should have a thermal resistance of not less than 0.6 m2K/W", with the main board commonly over-sailing the reveal by 20 mm. Its standard reveal details are green, sealed to the frame by compressible tape and bead or mastic: "Double seals must be provided."

Its uninsulated reveal, WRD-WJ003, is amber on solid brick of 225 mm or more, "Not suitable for random stone constructions", and "not allowed where frame thickness allows for reveal insulation". Where the frame leaves no room, the BRE guide notes that "windows could be replaced and re-profiled to accommodate this" (p.23). That is a design decision, not yours.

Look for:
- Outside: does the render return into the reveal to the frame, or stop at the wall face leaving the old reveal exposed?
- Is the frame now so buried that opening lights are restricted? LABC Warranty says they must not be.
- Inside: mould or condensation on reveals, at window corners, or on the frame. MER-ALL-018 explains why mould follows a cold reveal.

### Sills

When the wall face moves out, the old sill no longer projects. LABC Warranty says cills must "adequately project beyond the finished surface of the EWI", with the throating clear of the finish.

INCA's extended sill detail, WRD-WS001, requires a "min 40 mm projection from face of render", and says designers should consider sills with greater projection where exposure is Zone 4/very severe (BR 262). Its oversill details (WRD-WS003, WS004) also require at least 40 mm, and put that greater projection at 50 mm. INCA adds that "All sills should have end caps", that sill sections are joined "with metal connectors with seals on both sides of the joint", and that a sill which is not thermally broken makes the detail amber.

Look for:
- Does the sill project clearly beyond the render, or is it flush with or behind it? You will not measure it from the ground; you can see whether it projects at all.
- End caps, or an open sill end against the render.
- Staining, algae or erosion on the render below the sill ends or below a short sill.
- Inside: damp or mould below the window.

### Heads

INCA's head details use a "Drip edge corner bead at arris" (WRD-WH001), and for a flush head: "Ensure specified insulation is taken over the window frame by 15 - 20 mm" (WRD-WH002). Its uninsulated head is amber on solid brick of 225 mm or more. LABC Warranty requires drainage deflection beads around openings.

Look for a visible drip line at the head, streaking on the frame below, and mould at the head of the reveal inside.

### Sealant at the frame

INCA's guide says windows must be sealed to the opening before EWI is installed and frame drainage holes must not be blocked. The BRE guide warns that "Silicone sealant used around windows and verges can degrade quite quickly" (p.36). Record sealant that is split, missing or pulled away, and drainage slots covered by render.

Doors follow the same principles, and their thresholds are also base details (Zone 3).

## Section 5: Zone 3, The Base, the DPC and the Splash Zone

**In short:** LABC Warranty says the system must not bridge any horizontal DPC, so look for a base track, bead or break at about DPC level, or render running to the ground. Record paving or soil raised against the system, a gapped, damaged or blocked base closure, low-level staining, impact damage, and damp at skirting level inside. Apparent rising damp is so often misdiagnosed that the right response here is to record the base, not name a cause.

### The DPC must not be bridged

The base of the system must close and support the bottom edge, keep the DPC working, and let water drain out.

LABC Warranty's EWI update says the system "must not bridge any horizontal damp proof course". Its rendering update (October 2021) says: "The horizontal DPC must form a break in the render system." It also says renders and boards within 150 mm of adjacent ground level need third-party approval for that location.

The BRE guide for the Welsh Government notes that system certificates may not cover insulating below DPC, even though the DPC can be preserved (p.29), and that where it is done the DPC should be maintained "via a break-channel in the insulation" (p.34).

If render or insulation runs continuously from below the DPC to above it, ground moisture has a path around the DPC. MER-ALL-018 explains why apparent rising damp is so often misdiagnosed; here, the right response is to record the base, not name a cause.

### Starter tracks and closures

The BRE guide points out that a metal starter rail running "around the entire perimeter" is a thermal bridge whose effect "can actually be equivalent to leaving large sections of the wall uninsulated" (p.31).

INCA's insulated plinth detail, WRD-B001, says: "Maintain clearance below render to allow free drainage of water from the base of the system." It uses a plastic or metal closure, a gravel margin, and plinth insulation of "not less than 75% of the thermal resistance" of the main insulation, and is only for use where ground conditions allow.

### Ground levels and splash

The BRE guide records soil banked above the DPC as a moisture source on a real scheme, the use of gravel soakaways "to prevent potential water build up adjacent to the EWI" (p.40), and "splash protection" for high exposure (p.26).

This course uses "splash zone" for the low part of the wall wetted by rain bouncing off the ground. No opened source gives it a height; LABC Warranty's 150 mm is a threshold for product approval, not a definition.

### What to look for and record

- **The DPC line.** You may see it on an uninsulated neighbour. On the EWI house, is there a visible base track, bead or break at about that level, or does the render run to the ground?
- **Ground levels.** Has paving, a drive or soil been raised against or above the bottom of the system?
- **The base closure.** Continuous, or gapped, damaged or blocked with soil?
- **Staining.** Tide marks, algae, salts or a dark band at low level.
- **Damage.** Holes or cracks where bins and bikes hit the wall let water straight into the insulation.
- **Inside.** Damp or blistering at skirting level.

"Front elevation: render continues to paving level; no visible base track or break at DPC level; paving appears laid against render; dark staining to about 200 mm above paving. Inside front room: damp staining at skirting level along front wall." That is a set of observations, not a diagnosis.

## Section 6: Zone 4, Render Cracking, Board Joints and Mesh

**In short:** The render and basecoat are the rain screen: a crack lets water into the insulation, where it may not drain or dry. This course does not teach crack diagnosis; describe the pattern precisely: diagonal from opening corners, a regular grid, along corners, or impact damage. A crack continuing a line in the old wall, or a bowed area, is for referral. Do not tap, probe or pick at the render, or tell a householder to fill cracks themselves.

### Why the surface matters

The render and basecoat are the rain screen. A crack lets water into the insulation, where it may not drain or dry. The BRE guide says: "Any cracks in the external finish due to movement/ settling or other damage should be quickly rectified" (p.37).

### What the guidance says about causes

LABC Warranty's rendering update says "Render failures are generally due to poor workmanship, inadequate preparation of substrates". Its EWI update adds:
- Render should not be applied "below 5°C" or onto "saturated substrates", and must be protected from rain.
- "Movement joints in the backing substrate should be mirrored through the insulated render system."
- Additional reinforcement is required at vulnerable areas and the corners of openings, with an additional layer of mesh where there are concerns about straight-line joints, and beads at openings, corners, angles and interfaces.

### Describing crack patterns

This course does not teach crack diagnosis. Some cracks reflect movement in the wall behind, and some of that is structural. What you can do is describe the pattern precisely, because that is what a qualified person will use. The categories below are this course's recording vocabulary, tied to the locations the guidance singles out:
- **Diagonal cracks from the corners of openings.** The corners of openings are where LABC Warranty requires extra reinforcement. Record which corners and how long.
- **Straight lines in a regular grid.** These may follow board joints; LABC Warranty calls for an additional layer of mesh where there are concerns about straight-line joints. Record the approximate spacing.
- **A crack continuing a line in the old wall**, such as an old movement joint or the junction with an extension. LABC Warranty says substrate movement joints should be mirrored through the system. Refer.
- **Cracks along corners or beads.**
- **Impact damage**, often at low level.

### Other surface signs

- **Staining.** The BRE guide notes render can be "prone to discolouration through the build-up of organic particles" (p.37). Staining concentrated below a sill end, a fixture or a crack is worth recording as a location.
- **Bulging or bowed areas.** The BRE guide notes that insulation can pull away from the wall under wind loading (p.30). A bowed area is a referral trigger.
- **Rainwater goods.** "Faulty rainwater systems and blocked gutters are a common cause of building damage" (BRE guide, p.37).

### What not to do

Do not tap, press or probe the render to test whether it is hollow. Do not pick at a crack. Do not tell a householder to fill cracks themselves; the repair depends on the cause and the system designer's instructions. Do not suggest cutting into the system to look behind it.

## Section 7: Zones 5 and 6, Penetrations, Fixtures, Boundaries and Corners

**In short:** Every pipe, cable, flue, vent, bracket and box is a hole in the weathering line and often a thermal bridge. Check whether meter box doors open, vents serving gas appliances are covered, flues sit close to openings, or cables are exposed. For any gas or combustion concern, follow your organisation's gas safety procedure; for exposed cabling, recommend urgent advice from a qualified electrician. At boundaries, record stop ends and sealant, but do not advise on party wall matters.

### Zone 5: penetrations and fixtures

Every pipe, cable, flue, vent, bracket and box through or on the system is a hole in the weathering line and often a thermal bridge.

What the guidance says:
- **LABC Warranty:** flues and pipes must be "appropriately sleeved and fully sealed"; external fixtures should be installed after the system is complete and supported from the substrate, not "solely fixed to the EWI".
- **INCA's weathering guide:** external fixtures use thermally broken fixings sealed "against render with EPDM gaskets or proprietary waterproof sealant" (WRD-EFF001, EFF002). Gas pipe and cable enclosures are sealed with backing rod and mastic, and gas pipe enclosures need removable ventilated covers (WRD-G001). Surface-mounted gas meter boxes need "a min. gap of 25 mm for access and maintenance" (WRD-SB001). Moving service boxes is for the owner, such as the utility company. Flues are referred to a separate NIA and HHIC specification, not opened for this course.
- **The BRE guide:** fixing points for aerials and satellite dishes that span the insulation "effectively cause a thermal bridge" (p.31); insulation "cut around security alarms rather than removal and remounting" is unacceptable (p.19); vents must be extended through the EWI, and passive vents serving a combustion appliance kept (pp.22 to 23).

**What the NAO found.** NAO Figure 6 gives "Insufficient ventilation for the gas boiler", "Boiler flues too close to windows, doors or other openings" and "Live electricity cabling is left exposed" as examples of major issues posing immediate health and safety risks.

Look for and record:
- Gas meter box: can the door open; has the system been finished over it?
- Gas pipe on the wall: boxed in with a removable, ventilated cover?
- Flue terminals: still projecting through the new face; now close to an opening window or door?
- Air bricks and wall vents, especially for any room with an open-flued or flueless gas appliance: extended through the system, or covered?
- Cables: exposed, cut, or buried in render without protection.
- Dishes, lights, alarm boxes, brackets: fixed through the system, fixed to the render only, or cut around?
- Downpipes: refixed and sealed at each bracket?

**Referral triggers.** A covered vent serving a gas appliance, a flue near an opening, or any combustion concern: follow your organisation's gas safety procedure and the emergency arrangements in force; do not investigate. Exposed cabling: recommend urgent advice from a qualified electrician and tell the occupant not to touch it.

### Zone 6: boundaries with neighbours

- The Welsh Government planning guide says: "A party wall agreement may be required where external wall insulation ends at a party wall" (p.14), and recommends a whole-block approach where possible (pp.16 to 17).
- The BRE guide warns that "steps and staggers are often introduced when EWI installations are selectively 'pepper potted' across rows of dwellings", that "New thermal bridging can result", and that insulating one home can affect uninsulated neighbours, creating new condensation risk next door (pp.9 to 11).
- INCA's weathering guide has no standard detail for the junction with an uninsulated neighbour; its survey section requires the relationship with adjoining untreated buildings to be recorded. Its full-system stop bead detail (WRD-T001) uses sealing tape behind the bead, silicone sealant and a 5 mm expansion gap.

Look for and record: whether the end of the system is closed with a stop bead or trim; exposed insulation edges; sealant condition; staining or cracking at the step between insulated and uninsulated faces; how the roofline has been closed over the boundary; and, if you are assessing the neighbour's house, mould on the wall nearest the boundary. Do not advise on party wall matters.

### Zone 6: external corners

LABC Warranty requires beads "at corners, angles and interfaces" and corrosion protection for metals in coastal or severe exposure. INCA's front-to-gable corner section projects "min 40mm past the render". Look for corner beads showing through the render, rust staining, cracks along the corner, impact damage at low level, and gaps where a gable trim meets an eaves trim.

**Visual:** Traffic light for Zones 5 and 6. Green, monitor: penetration sealed and fixture fixed through the system; stop end at the neighbour closed with a bead and intact sealant; corner bead covered and undamaged. Amber, investigate: sealant split at a downpipe bracket; satellite dish fixed to render only; staining at the step to the uninsulated neighbour; crack along a corner. Red, get professional advice: vent serving a gas appliance covered; flue terminal next to an opening window; exposed cable; meter box door blocked by the system. Footnote: Red items involving gas or electricity follow the organisation's safety procedure, not just a report line.

## Section 8: The ECO4 and GBIS Evidence, the TrustMark Route, and Recording

**In short:** The NAO reported government and Ofgem estimates that 98% of homes with EWI under ECO4 and GBIS, installed before 16 January 2025, have major issues requiring remediation. Use these extrapolated estimates to explain why you look carefully and mention the audit route, never to tell a householder their installation is defective. Point householders with ECO4 or GBIS solid wall insulation to TrustMark rather than judging eligibility or quoting cover figures. Never tell anyone to open up a wall.

### What the National Audit Office found

The NAO report (HC 1334, 14 October 2025) set out government and Ofgem estimates from audits of 758 projects, extrapolated (summary paragraph 11). For external wall insulation installed before 16 January 2025:

| Audit category (NAO Figure 6) | Share of EWI homes | Estimated homes |
|---|---|---|
| Major issues that pose immediate health and safety risks | 6% | 900 to 2,000 |
| Major issues that will affect insulation performance | 92% | 20,700 to 21,900 |
| Minor issues that may affect performance over time | 1% | up to 600 |
| Pass | 1% | up to 400 |

Summary paragraph 11: "98% of homes with external wall insulation have major issues requiring remediation (between 22,000 and 23,000 homes)." The 6% have "health and safety risks that require immediate correction, such as inadequate ventilation". Paragraph 2.2: "1% fully met the PAS 2035 standard." For internal wall insulation the figure is 29% with major issues (paragraph 11); Part 3 of this series covers it.

Three cautions when you use these figures:
- They are estimates extrapolated from audits, not counts of homes inspected.
- Paragraph 11: "They have not audited homes retrofitted from 16 January 2025 onwards", and those homes are excluded from the estimates.
- Figure 2, note 6: the wall insulation numbers "include installations on both cavity walls and solid walls".

> **Wider view: the same measure under other schemes.** The NAO also reported results from audits of two other government schemes (Summary paragraph 17; paragraph 2.9; Figure 9). DESNZ commissioned these audits of the Social Housing Decarbonisation Fund (wave 2.1) and the Home Upgrade Grant (phase 2), using a random sample of 188 wall insulation measures. They found major issues in 12% of external wall insulation. The NAO describes these audits as comparable to the ECO4 and GBIS audits. Like the ECO figures, they relate to installations before the audit dates, not to current rates.
>
> **What it means for your work:** the same measure can turn out very differently depending on how it is designed, installed and checked. The scheme an installation came through is useful context to record, and so is any evidence you are shown of checks made before the work was covered. Neither tells you the condition of the wall in front of you; your own observations do.

Use them to explain why you look carefully at ECO-era EWI and why you mention the audit route. Do not use them to tell a householder their installation is defective. A scheme-wide estimate says nothing certain about the house in front of you.

On who pays, paragraph 15 says "The original installer is liable for all the cost of remediating non-compliant installations" and that "DESNZ has stated that no household with a faulty installation should have to pay to fix the issues". Paragraph 2.20 says that where the installer has ceased to trade, the 25-year guarantee TrustMark requires should cover remediation, and that TrustMark said these guarantees should cover costs up to £20,000.

### Later developments

HM Treasury's Treasury Minutes (CP 1522, April 2026, paragraphs 1.3 and 2.2) record the government's position that "all EWI installed under ECO4 and GBIS will be checked by an independent, trained and accountable auditor", that TrustMark "reached full operational delivery of the find-and-fix programme in late January 2026", and that the programme is intended to complete by May 2027.

On 18 June 2026, alongside a consultation on consumer protection for home upgrade schemes, the government announced higher repair cover. MoneySavingExpert reported that those affected "will now be covered for repair costs up to £25,000 – up from the previous limit of £20,000". The National Residential Landlords Association reported that the Installation Assurance Authority (IAA) will cover repairs to ECO4 and GBIS solid wall insulation up to £25,000 "where these are within the terms of the original, still-valid guarantee". The IAA announced it was "retrospectively increasing" its maxima for ECO4 and GBIS solid wall insulation guarantees from £20,000 to £25,000, and described itself as the only consumer protection provider to have done so; the cover available may therefore depend on who issued the guarantee. These sources report the change as in effect, not proposed; the gov.uk page was not opened. If a householder asks about cover, point them to TrustMark and their guarantee provider rather than quoting a figure.

### The TrustMark free audit route

If a householder says they had solid wall insulation installed under ECO4 or GBIS:
- **Citizens Advice** ("Get help with faulty solid wall insulation", Wales page, read October 2026) says the free check "is for wall insulation installed inside and outside your home", where the work was "carried out between April 2022 and January 2025" with help from ECO4 or GBIS, and that if problems are found "You will not have to pay anything". It gives TrustMark's number, 0333 555 1234, and the Ofgem helpline, 0808 169 4447.
- **TrustMark's Find & Fix booklet** (5 February 2026) is headed "Act now if you have had External Wall Insulation installed under ECO4 or GBIS schemes"; the audit "will focus on safety and technical standards" and problems are fixed "at no cost to you". TrustMark's "Free Find & Fix audit offer" page was updated on 23 October 2026.
- **MoneySavingExpert** (18 June 2026) advises asking TrustMark, when it makes contact, to provide the Unique Measure Reference Number shown on the householder's Ofgem letter, as a check that the contact is genuine.

TrustMark's materials mention external wall insulation; Citizens Advice includes internal. No opened source mentions cavity wall insulation in this route, and none sets out how eligibility is decided in a particular case. Tell the householder to contact TrustMark rather than relying on your reading of eligibility.

### Recording and referral

Use the structure in MER-ALL-018 (location, extent, pattern, context, construction) and add the zone. Avoid "defective", "non-compliant", "failed", "confirmed" and "caused by", and any instruction to strip, cut, drill, fill or open up anything.

A worked record: "Rear elevation, rendered EWI, householder reports installed under ECO4 in 2023. Zone 1: roof does not appear to oversail render at rear eaves; top edge sealed with sealant bead only, cracked in places. Zone 2: rear bedroom window sill appears flush with render, no end caps visible; green staining below both sill ends. Zone 3: render continues to patio level, no visible break at DPC level. Inside: mould at ceiling line and on reveals in rear bedroom. System build-up not inspected. Householder advised of TrustMark's free audit for solid wall insulation installed under ECO4 or GBIS (0333 555 1234) and recommended to get professional advice on the moisture observed."

Recommend professional advice, in writing, for: any gas concern (follow your safety procedure); exposed cabling (qualified electrician); bulging or bowed areas, or cracks continuing a line in the old wall (structural engineer or building surveyor); internal damp or mould that lines up with an external edge defect (appropriately qualified surveyor, plus the TrustMark route where relevant); EWI on a building with a storey at or above 11 m, or any fire question.

Never tell anyone to open up a wall. If something needs opening up to be understood, the recommendation is professional advice, and for ECO4 or GBIS work, TrustMark.

Left, worked-numbers chart: one horizontal stacked bar for EWI homes from NAO Figure 6: 6% immediate health and safety, 92% major performance issues, 1% minor, 1% pass; a bracket over the first two segments labelled "98%: 22,000 to 23,000 homes"; note "estimates extrapolated from 758 audited projects; installed before 16 January 2025". Right, referral route: "EWI observed" → "ECO4 or GBIS reported?" → yes: "record by zone; give TrustMark contact"; both paths → "Any Red trigger (gas, electrics, structure, building 11 m or more)?" → yes: "follow safety procedure / recommend professional advice in writing"; no: "record, and recommend professional advice on any damp or mould observed".

## Key Takeaways

- An EWI system is adhesive, insulation boards, mechanical fixings, mesh in a basecoat and a finish, adding 70 to 160 mm to the wall (Welsh Government guide). Every feature on the old face has to be re-detailed.
- Failures cluster at six edge zones: eaves and verges, openings, the base, the render surface and board joints, penetrations and fixtures, and boundaries and corners.
- Look for a second line of defence at every edge. INCA says sealant must not be the sole means of weather protection.
- At the roofline, INCA calls an extended roof "the most robust solution". The NAO cites eaves "not ... effectively extended to fully cover the insulation" as a major performance issue.
- INCA's details require sills to project at least 40 mm from the render face (designers to consider more, 50 mm in its oversill details, in Zone 4/very severe exposure), with end caps, and reveal insulation of at least 0.6 m²K/W.
- LABC Warranty says EWI "must not bridge any horizontal damp proof course".
- The NAO reports estimates that 98% of ECO4 and GBIS EWI homes installed before 16 January 2025 have major issues (22,000 to 23,000 homes), 6% with immediate health and safety risks, and 1% fully meeting PAS 2035.
- Citizens Advice says householders with solid wall insulation installed under ECO4 or GBIS between April 2022 and January 2025 can get a free check; TrustMark arranges it (0333 555 1234). In June 2026 the Installation Assurance Authority raised its guarantee maxima for this work from £20,000 to £25,000; check cover with TrustMark and the guarantee provider.
- Recognise and record by zone, inside and out. Do not diagnose, condemn, probe or tell anyone to open up a wall.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** According to INCA's weathering and thermal bridge control guide, what is the minimum projection of a new extended window sill from the face of the render, in moderate exposure?

A) 10 mm
B) 25 mm
C) 40 mm
D) 75 mm

**Correct answer: C.** Detail WRD-WS001 requires a minimum 40 mm projection from the face of the render; designers should consider greater projection (50 mm in INCA's oversill details) where exposure is Zone 4/very severe (Section 4).

---

**Q2.** What does LABC Warranty's EWI technical update say about the damp proof course?

A) EWI should always continue to ground level for thermal continuity
B) The EWI must not bridge any horizontal damp proof course
C) The DPC can be ignored if the insulation is mineral wool
D) A new DPC must be injected before EWI is installed

**Correct answer: B.** LABC Warranty says the EWI "must not bridge any horizontal damp proof course"; its rendering update says the DPC "must form a break in the render system" (Section 5).

---

**Q3.** According to the NAO report of October 2025, what proportion of homes with external wall insulation under ECO4 and GBIS (installed before 16 January 2025) were estimated to have major issues requiring remediation?

A) 29%
B) 52%
C) 92%
D) 98%

**Correct answer: D.** Summary paragraph 11 gives 98% (between 22,000 and 23,000 homes): 92% with major performance issues and 6% with immediate health and safety risks. 29% is the internal wall insulation figure (Section 8).

---

**Q4.** At a gable end, you see the render running up to the roof edge with no visible roof overhang, and the top of the insulation closed only by a cracked bead of sealant. What is the appropriate record?

A) "Verge failed; water entering the wall; strip and replace"
B) "Verge detail non-compliant with PAS 2030"
C) "Roof does not appear to oversail the EWI at the gable; top edge protected by sealant bead only, cracked in places", with any internal mould at the same location recorded alongside
D) No record, because the roof is outside the scope of a wall assessment

**Correct answer: C.** Record what you see, by location and zone, and link internal symptoms by location without naming a cause. INCA's guidance says sealant must not be the only line of defence (Sections 3 and 8).

---

**Q5.** A householder says their EWI was installed under GBIS in 2024, and you see a gas meter box partly covered by the new render. What should you do?

A) Cut the render back from the meter box door
B) Tell the householder the installation is dangerous and must be removed
C) Record it, follow your organisation's gas safety procedure if there is any gas concern, and tell the householder about TrustMark's free audit for solid wall insulation installed under ECO4 or GBIS
D) Advise the householder to contact the gas supplier to move the meter

**Correct answer: C.** INCA's detail requires a 25 mm access gap at surface-mounted gas meter boxes. The assessor records, follows the safety procedure where needed, and signposts the TrustMark route; the assessor does not alter the system or prescribe the fix (Sections 7 and 8).
