## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- Describe how an internal wall insulation (IWI) system is built up, and the different jobs done by the insulation, the air barrier, the vapour control layer, the service void and the board.
- Explain the difference between moisture-closed and moisture-open IWI, and why traditional solid walls need particular care.
- Explain interstitial condensation in plain terms, and where in an insulated solid wall the risk concentrates.
- Recognise the common IWI defect zones and record what you see at each.
- Place your findings within the designer's moisture risk assessment, using the PAS 2035:2023 clauses and the standards they name, and signpost the free TrustMark audit route for solid wall insulation installed under ECO4 or GBIS.

## Introduction

Internal wall insulation is the measure most likely to hide its own problems. External wall insulation tends to fail where it can be seen. IWI fails behind a painted board: in the cold layer between the insulation and the old wall, in the end of a joist you cannot see, and in the corner of a reveal that the householder wipes every winter morning.

The scale is now on record. The National Audit Office (NAO) estimated in October 2025 that 29% of homes given IWI under ECO4 or the Great British Insulation Scheme (GBIS) before 16 January 2025, or between 9,000 and 13,000 homes, have major issues requiring remediation (Energy efficiency installations under the Energy Company Obligation, paragraph 2.3). The figure is an estimate extrapolated from a sample audit. Section 7 sets out what that figure covers.

This is Part 3 of the Field Work insulation series. It assumes the building physics in MER-ALL-018 (Damp, Condensation and Thermal Bridging: Reading the Moisture), which covers dew point, surface humidity, the temperature factor and moisture meters, and does not repeat those calculations. It does not train you to design IWI, to carry out a hygrothermal assessment, or to judge whether an installation complies. On site you recognise and record. Where something could be a moisture or structural problem, you say it is possible or likely, record it precisely, and recommend professional advice.

### Where this course applies

This course is written for England and Wales:
- The government's IWI best practice guide says that "In England and Wales, the Building Regulations apply to most buildings and alterations to existing buildings" (paragraph 14).
- The Approved Documents cited are the England editions: Approved Document C (2004 edition incorporating 2010 and 2013 amendments) and Approved Document L Volume 1 (2021 edition incorporating 2023 amendments). Wales publishes its own; the Welsh editions were not opened, so check gov.wales before relying on these paragraph numbers in Wales.
- The Historic England guidance is written for England. Its building physics is not nation-specific; its advice on consents is.
- The NAO report covers ECO4 and GBIS and does not split its audit findings by nation. Citizens Advice's page on faulty solid wall insulation was read in its Wales version.

PAS 2035:2023 is a BSI specification and is cited by clause.

## Section 1: How Internal Wall Insulation Is Built Up

### The main guidance

The most detailed government source is "Retrofit Internal Wall Insulation: Guide to Best Practice" (September 2021), written by technical authors from Enhabit and published on gov.uk on 13 September 2021 by the Department for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy (now also listed under the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero) as "Guidance for designers, surveyors, project managers and installers about retrofit internal wall insulation." This course calls it the IWI best practice guide. It sets no single standard build-up, because the right one depends on the wall; read together, its sections give an IWI system up to five jobs.

### The layers and their jobs

**1. The insulation.** Rigid boards, an insulated plaster, or insulation held in a frame. Board joints "should be tightly butted", and gaps over 10 mm between boards should be "properly packed out" (paragraph 88). Metal-framed systems should be avoided unless their thermal bridging is mitigated (paragraph 34).

**2. The fixing to the wall.** "Ideally a continuous layer of adhesive should be applied to the wall", and "Dot and dab approaches have the highest risk of interstitial condensation and should be avoided as they leave air gaps behind the insulation" (paragraph 92). An air gap behind the board is a route for room air, and its moisture, to reach the cold face of the old wall.

**3. The air barrier.** "An air barrier shall be installed in all internal wall insulation systems on the warm side", and "This is not the same as a vapour control layer (VCL)" (paragraph 36). It must "continue around junctions, edges and corners", using airtightness tapes, adhesives or paints appropriate to the situation; the guide names duct tape among products that "do not form robust and reliable air barriers" (paragraph 38).

**4. The vapour control layer, where one is used.** The glossary defines a VCL as "A building material (usually a membrane) with properties that control the passage of vapour through it." Where specified, it "should be on the warm side of the insulation" (paragraph 83), and in a moisture-closed system "this must be sealed at all junctions, edges and corners" (paragraph 82). Whether to use one at all depends on the wall (Section 2).

**5. The service void and the board.** "If possible, a separate service cavity should be provided in front of the insulation, air barrier and VCL" (paragraph 66). The board or plaster finish goes in front of that, so cables, back boxes and pipes can run without cutting the layers behind.

### Warm side and cold side

In IWI the warm side is the room side of the insulation and the cold side is the wall side. Once insulation is fixed inside a solid wall, the old masonry is on the cold side: it is no longer warmed by the room. Everything in this course starts from that.

From the room outwards, a moisture-closed board system that follows the guide reads:

| Position | Layer | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Room face | Board or plaster finish | Finish |
| | Service void | Room for cables, boxes and pipes |
| Warm side of insulation | Air barrier and VCL (may be one layer) | Limit air and vapour reaching the cold side |
| | Insulation | Reduce heat flow; everything outside it gets colder |
| Cold side of insulation | Ventilated cavity (Section 2) | Let the old wall dry |
| | Existing solid wall | Now colder and slower to dry |

A moisture-open system differs: capillary-active insulation bonded to the wall with vapour-open adhesive, an air barrier and a vapour-open finish, with no vapour barrier (paragraph 44).

Highlight the face where the insulation meets the old wall as the critical point, labelled "now on the cold side".

## Section 2: Moisture-Closed and Moisture-Open, and Why Solid Walls Need Care

### Two strategies

The IWI best practice guide's glossary defines:
- "Moisture closed – A building construction that prevents moisture (vapour & liquid) from moving in an out of it."
- "Moisture open – A building construction that allows moisture (vapour & liquid) to freely move in an out of it."

Modern buildings tend to be moisture closed, while "traditional buildings have a moisture open approach" (paragraph 41), and the lowest-risk design matches the building's existing moisture strategy (paragraph 44). PAS 2035:2023 agrees: "The materials specified should match the moisture strategy of the existing building" (clause 8.2.10, note). The guide adds: "Note that moisture closed is not the same as airtight" (paragraph 79). Every system needs an air barrier; not every system should have a vapour barrier.

### How a traditional solid wall manages water

Historic England explains: "Most traditional buildings are made of permeable materials", which "act as a buffer for environmental moisture", taking it up when humidity is high and releasing it when the air is dry; moisture reaches solid walls from rain, the ground and the occupants (Energy Efficiency and Historic Buildings: Insulating Solid Walls, HEAG081, version 1.1, April 2016, section 1.2). A solid wall has no cavity to stop rain reaching its inner face. It copes by getting wet and then drying, outwards in good weather and inwards to the heated room.

Approved Document L Volume 1 says energy efficiency in historic and traditional dwellings should be improved "only if doing so will not cause long-term deterioration of the building's fabric or fittings", particularly in vapour-permeable constructions (paragraph 0.10).

### What IWI changes

Fix insulation inside that wall and, in this course's reading of the sources, three things change. The wall is colder, because the room's heat no longer reaches it. It dries more slowly, because the inward drying route is partly or wholly blocked. And any room air that reaches the old wall's inner face meets a cold surface. The guide warns that if its recommendations are ignored, "a high risk of interstitial condensation and mould growth might be present", and that "moisture management is therefore a major consideration" (Introduction).

### Why the old answer changed

"The addition of a vapour barrier inside internal insulation (IWI) on solid walls used to be regarded as essential", but "in many cases, this may cause more harm than good" (paragraph 45). A vapour barrier keeps room moisture out of the wall, but also stops the wall drying inwards; if the wall is wet from rain or a defect, the water has fewer ways out. So for solid walls the guide recommends either:
- "a moisture open internal wall insulation build-up" (paragraph 45), preferably with capillary-active insulation, vapour-open adhesives and finishes and an air barrier, without a vapour barrier (paragraph 44); or
- "a moisture closed system with a ventilated cavity (of at least 25mm) on the cold side" (paragraph 45), in which "the ventilation is essential to reducing the risk of interstitial condensation" (paragraph 49).

A moisture-closed system "could be considered" for walls with impervious external cladding or cavity walls (paragraph 46).

Historic England reaches the same position. Impermeable materials such as closed-cell plastic insulation, plastic vapour barriers, cement or acrylic renders and cement pointing can "seriously exacerbate existing problems" (section 2.2), and each layer should become "progressively more permeable from the interior to the exterior" (section 3.3). The STBA said in 2015 that IWI standards and advice for solid walls "are currently wrong or misleading" (Planning responsible retrofit of traditional buildings, section 3); the 2021 guide and PAS 2035:2023 both post-date that view.

### Thin is lower risk, not no risk

The guide says thin, vapour-permeable, capillary-active IWI "will help to reduce moisture risks" (paragraph 47). The Loughborough report modelled a solid brick wall with two systems: wood-fibre board with lime plaster, and expanded polystyrene (EPS) with an air and vapour control layer. Its executive summary found:
- "installing IWI always led to an increased moisture risk, even when only thin levels of insulation were applied";
- "Increasing the thickness of the IWI increased the relative moisture risk in all cases";
- "The wood-fibre vapour permeable IWI produced a relatively lower risk" than the EPS system;
- "Homes with a higher internally generated moisture load will have a higher moisture risk."

Removing the air and vapour control layer from the EPS system "reduces the moisture risk to some extent" (section 5.3.1), which the authors read as the wall drying to the inside. The report notes that its simulation is one-dimensional, that joints are "not explicitly modelled" (section 3) and that results were "sensitive to assumptions about indoor RH" (executive summary). It does not model junctions or workmanship.

### On site

You will not choose the strategy. You can record the facts that tell the designer which strategy the wall has and whether its drying routes are intact: wall material, thickness and finishes inside and out; external condition; any existing IWI and its finish; and the room's moisture sources and ventilation.

Right, moisture-open: vapour-open finish, air barrier, capillary-active insulation bonded to the wall with no gap, solid wall; drying arrows in both directions. Highlight the cold-side face in each.

## Section 3: Interstitial Condensation in Plain Terms

### The definition

Approved Document C (England) defines interstitial condensation as deposition of liquid water from a vapour "occurring within or between the layers of the building envelope", and surface condensation as occurring "on visible surfaces within the building" (paragraph 0.12). "Interstitial condensation may cause damage to the structure" (paragraph 0.6). Walls should "be designed and constructed so that their structural and thermal performance are not adversely affected by interstitial condensation", and should "not promote surface condensation or mould growth, given reasonable occupancy conditions" (paragraph 5.2(e) and (f)).

### The mechanism, without equations

MER-ALL-018 sets out the numbers. In plain terms:
1. Room air in winter carries water vapour from cooking, washing, breathing and drying laundry.
2. Cooled air cannot hold as much vapour; cool it far enough and some turns to liquid water.
3. Across an insulated solid wall the temperature falls steeply through the insulation. The coldest place room air can reach is the cold side of the insulation, where it meets the old wall.
4. Room air gets there through gaps in the air barrier, punctures in the VCL, unsealed back boxes, or air gaps behind dot-and-dab boards.
5. The same face also receives rainwater soaking inwards through the solid wall.

Historic England calls condensation on a surface or "between layers of the building fabric" the most significant risk (Introduction, Technical risks). You cannot see that face. What may show later are its consequences: mould at the edges of the system, staining at skirting level, a smell, a soft floor, or nothing for years. The guide warns that many moisture problems stay hidden for a long time, so completion "should not be considered to occur at practical completion of works"; it should be when the building is in equilibrium, which in some situations will be after two years and in others considerably longer (paragraph 102).

### Air leaks matter

Vapour diffuses through materials slowly; air carries it much faster. That is why the air barrier is mandatory in every system (paragraph 36), why dot-and-dab should be avoided (paragraph 92), and why holes in the VCL or air barrier must be "properly sealed with appropriate tape" (paragraph 93). Historic England says "Effective vapour control is very difficult to achieve in practical terms", that building users can damage membranes by making fixings or altering electrical fittings, and that "All penetrations can allow moisture vapour through" (section 4.3).

### Wind-driven rain

The Loughborough report found moisture risk "was higher in Manchester than in London" (section 6), which it placed in wind-driven rain exposure categories 2 (moderate) and 1 (sheltered) respectively (section 3). The guide asks whether external finishes suit "the severity of wind-driven rain exposure", with a hygrothermal risk assessment as the alternative (Table 1). PAS 2035:2023 names "solid walls with internal wall insulation (IWI) proposed in exposed locations" as walls where a standard interstitial condensation assessment "might not be sufficient" (clause 4.3.3, note 1).

So, in this course's reading, a blocked gutter, failed pointing or cracked render on a wall with IWI is not only an external defect: it feeds water into a wall the IWI has made colder and slower to dry.

## Section 4: The Defect Zones

This section walks round an insulated room. For each zone: what the sources say, then, as this course's practical reading, what you may see and record.

### Services puncturing the air barrier and VCL

**Sources.** All services ("plug sockets, light switches, pipes, radiators, etc.") must be considered and reinstated (paragraph 65). Penetrations "should be kept to a minimum and be properly sealed" (paragraph 66). "Any penetrations of the air barrier or the vapour control layer that cannot be avoided should be sealed appropriately" (paragraph 94), and the design should include "A schedule of penetrations" (paragraph 95).

**What you may see and record.** Sockets, switches, radiators, pipes or a flue on insulated walls; whether a service void appears to be present, judged from the faceplate depth without removing it; any draught at a faceplate; any staining or mould around fittings. Do not remove faceplates. Water near an electrical fitting is a referral trigger in its own right (Section 8).

### Window and door reveals

**Sources.** "Using a thin insulation (<15mm) returned into the reveals is a good way to reduce thermal bridging" (paragraph 31), and "All thermal bridges should be mitigated to ensure a surface temperature factor (Frsi) > 0.75" (paragraph 33). The STBA notes that IWI's effectiveness can be reduced by heat loss through reveals that are not also insulated (section 4).

**What you may see and record.** With the wall face warmed, an uninsulated reveal becomes the coldest surface in the room. Mould tends to show as a band down the jamb or along the head, often worst in the bottom corners where condensation from the glass wets the sill. A step between a thick lining on the wall and a thin or bare reveal is a clue. Record lining thickness at the frame if visible, mould location and extent, and condensation on glass and frame.

### Floor joist ends in the external wall

**Sources.** Historic England says floor-joist ends built into external walls are "at increased risk of decay from condensation" (section 2.3). The guide says "the lowest risk option is to entirely remove the joists from the wall" (paragraph 53), that thin vapour-permeable IWI under 40 mm "will help reduce moisture risks in joist ends" (paragraph 55), and that "Joist ends embedded in walls should be made airtight using vapour open products" (paragraph 56). The Loughborough report names "the rotting of structural timber floor joists that are embedded in the wall" as a moisture risk of IWI (section 1).

**Why this is structural (course reading).** The joist end sits in masonry that is now colder and dries more slowly. If it stays wet long enough, the timber may decay. Decay at a bearing is a structural matter.

**What you may see and record.** Usually nothing. Possible signs: a floor that feels soft, springy or out of level near the external wall; skirting on an external wall that is stained, swollen or loose; a musty smell at floor level; mould at the ceiling-to-wall line in the room below. Record floor construction where known and any of these signs, with location. Any sign of possible decay at a floor is a referral to a structural engineer or building surveyor (Section 8). Do not lift boards or ask the householder to.

### Wall-to-ceiling and party wall junctions

**Sources.** Thermal bridge junctions include places "Where internal wall insulation is interrupted by internal walls, party walls" and "Where internal wall insulation meets ceilings/roofs"; insulation should be returned "along thermal bridge junctions by a minimum of 400mm" (paragraph 31). Footnote 3 warns: "Care needs to be taken at party walls when returning insulation along the party wall to mitigate against thermal bridging. Installing insulation and party walls can make the party wall colder and therefore increase the risk of condensation and mould growth in the neighbouring property if it is not suitably heated or ventilated." Historic England says insulation must extend "onto party walls, other internal walls, floors and ceilings" (section 4.2).

**What you may see and record.** A strip of mould along the ceiling edge of an insulated wall, often darkest in the corners; a vertical mould line where the insulated wall meets an uninsulated party or internal wall; a visible lining return along the party wall. Record location and length, whether a return is visible, and whether loft insulation reaches the wall head. If the householder says the neighbour has mould on the party wall, record it as reported; do not inspect next door.

### Skirting and ground floor junctions

**Sources.** Thermal bridge junctions include "Where internal wall insulation meets ground floors" (paragraph 31); if the design shows boards meeting the floor or ceiling, "this should be adhered to" (paragraph 90). The suitability table asks whether "the external ground level [is] higher than the internal floor finish level in rooms in which IWI is being installed" and whether there is "a functioning damp proof course (DPC)" (Table 1).

**What you may see and record.** Mould or staining along the skirting of an insulated wall, worst in corners and behind furniture; outside, ground or a path at or above internal floor level. Record location, extent, whether it is on insulated or uninsulated walls, and external ground level where you can see both sides. A low-level band of damp cannot be attributed from sight: the guide says rising damp is "quite uncommon in well-built, traditional buildings in the UK" and that other causes should be looked for, with specialist advice if none is found (paragraph 96). Record and refer.

### Partial coverage

**Sources.** "There is a surface condensation risk if IWI is installed on only part of an external wall", because "the external wall will become cold where the IWI is installed as it has been isolated from the room heating", and "Where the IWI finishes the cold exposed wall is more susceptible to surface condensation" (paragraph 60). Among the conditions for leaving rooms uninsulated: "only specific whole rooms, not parts of rooms may be left uninsulated", and those rooms "must have a safe heating system that can achieve a minimum of 18 degrees C" (paragraph 63). "If the above points are not followed then partial IWI installation should not proceed" (paragraph 64). PAS 2035:2023 requires a stage-by-stage design to "include provisions for mitigating the risks relating to cold surfaces, including condensation and mould" (clause 8.2.17).

**What you may see and record.** Insulation that stops part way along a room, behind kitchen units or a fitted wardrobe; a line of mould at that edge. Map which walls and rooms are insulated and where the IWI stops. A coordinator cannot assess partial coverage without that map.

### Impermeable systems on breathable walls

**Sources.** Historic England's warning on impermeable materials (section 2.2) applies here. The guide asks whether "the existing internal finish of the external walls [is] vapour closed", identifies a "Risk of moisture trapped between insulation and wall", and advises removing or scarifying "all moisture closed layers (e.g. vinyl wall finished, wallpapers, gloss paint and gypsum plasters)" before installation (Table 1). It flags "incompatible cement-based pointing" (Table 1), and the STBA lists "The addition of modern impermeable cement renders" among alterations to traditional walls (section 3).

**What you may see and record.** A stone or old brick wall with cement render or pointing outside and foil-backed or plastic-faced boards inside may have limited drying in both directions. You cannot tell from the room face what is behind the board. Record wall material, external finish and pointing type where identifiable, and any product information the householder holds. Write "unknown" where you do not know. Do not call a system moisture-open or moisture-closed without documentary evidence.

## Section 5: Reading the Signs

### The twins in an insulated home

The guide offers one distinction: "Large areas of mould growth or condensation may indicate poor ventilation or underheating", whereas "Focused areas of fungus or mould growth may indicate thermal bridging or water ingress from elsewhere" (Table 1). The rest of this table is this course's practical reading of Sections 2 to 4. It lists reasons to record and refer, not tests that settle a cause.

| What you see | Possible explanation A | Possible explanation B | Detail worth recording |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mould on a reveal of an insulated wall | Insulation not returned into the reveal | Water entering at the frame or sill | Worse in cold weather with condensation on glass, or after rain below a visible gap in the seal? |
| Mould line at the ceiling edge | Junction not insulated or air barrier not continuous | Loft insulation short of the wall head, or a roof or gutter leak | Loft insulation at the eaves if safely visible; external roof and gutters |
| Staining at skirting level | Floor junction cold bridge or air leakage behind the board | Ground moisture or water from an external defect | External ground level; gutters and downpipes; confined to insulated walls? |
| Mould around a socket | Room air reaching the cold side through an unsealed box | Local cold spot where insulation was cut back | Service void visible? Draught? Extent |
| Soft or springy floor near an external wall | Possible decay at a joist bearing | Other floor defects | Location and wall; a referral either way |
| General mould across all walls | High humidity, poor ventilation or underheating | More than one cause | Extract fans and whether they run; trickle vents; heating in use |

### Questions that sharpen the record

- **Weather or life inside?** Ask when it is worst; record the answer as reported.
- **Insulated or uninsulated?** Where the IWI stops, and where the problem sits relative to that edge, is one of the most useful facts you can record.
- **Junction or field?** A problem at a junction points to a local detail; one spread across the wall points to room conditions.
- **Was it there before?** If the householder says mould appeared after the insulation went in, record it as reported.

### Ventilation

"No insulation should be installed without assessing the ventilation system" (paragraph 57), and "Sufficient, controlled ventilation is key to managing internal humidity levels" (paragraph 10). The guide also says "Positive Input Ventilation (PIV) should not be installed with internal wall insulation" (paragraph 58). The NAO's examples of performance defects include "Inadequate ventilation, leading to increased levels of moisture and pollutants" (Figure 6). Record extract fans, whether they run, trickle vents and any PIV unit, factually. A PIV unit in a home with IWI is a fact to record, not a finding of non-compliance.

### Instruments

MER-ALL-018 Section 5 covers the limits of moisture meters. With IWI there is a further limit, in this course's reading: a meter on the board measures the board, at its surface. It tells you nothing reliable about the masonry face behind the insulation, where the risk sits. Do not report a meter reading on an IWI board as evidence about the wall.

## Section 6: Moisture Risk Assessment, and Where It Sits

### Who does it

The moisture risk assessment for IWI is design work. PAS 2035:2023 requires the retrofit design to "include provision for the management of moisture within the construction, in line with BS 5250" (clause 8.2.10), and construction details "for the corners, junctions and edges" of affected elements (clause 8.2.15). Your part is the evidence the designer starts from: "an appraisal of the dwelling's heritage, architectural features, structure, construction and condition" (clause 7.3.2 b)) and "identification of the location and severity of any existing construction defects" and "of any condensation and/or mould growth in the dwelling" (clause 7.3.2 d)).

### The standards named

- **BS 5250:2021, Management of moisture in buildings. Code of practice.** BSI lists it as published 31 October 2021, current and under review. PAS 2035:2023 notes that guidance on diagnosing dampness in existing buildings is in its Annex C (clause 7.3.2, note 4). The IWI best practice guide says hygrothermal modelling "should be used to assess moisture risk as advised in BS5250" (paragraph 42), citing the standard's pre-2021 title in a footnote.
- **BS EN ISO 13788.** PAS 2035:2023 clause 4.3.3, note 1: "In order to conform to BS 5250, the assessment of interstitial condensation (i.e. to BS EN ISO 13788) might not be sufficient as the basis for a moisture risk assessment for certain types of walls, e.g. solid walls with internal wall insulation (IWI) proposed in exposed locations." The note does not say what to use instead.
- **Glaser and dynamic simulation.** The guide fills that gap: "The simple Glaser method is only suitable for moisture closed and weathertight constructions", and dynamic hygrothermal simulation, using software such as WUFI or Delphin, should be used for moisture-open constructions exposed to wind-driven rain (paragraph 42).
- **BS EN 15026** (numerical simulation of moisture transfer). Not opened. The Loughborough report used "dynamic, one-dimensional, hygrothermal simulation, in line with BS EN 15026:2007" and notes that "BS EN 15026:2007 does not include any criteria" (section 2).

The STBA warned in 2015 that modelling "is often not possible due to lack of good data or skills" (section 7), and Historic England that calculations are "based upon idealised, homogenous walls" (section 1.1). Modelling a traditional wall is specialist work with real uncertainty: a reason for your record to be precise about the wall, not for you to model it.

### The temperature factor at junctions

PAS 2035:2023 clause 8.2.16 requires details "to maintain the continuity of the three-dimensional insulated envelope". For a detail that is not a published good-practice detail, "it shall be shown by calculating it in accordance with IP1/06" that "the temperature factor (fRsi) of each detail is not less than 0.75." The guide's paragraph 33 uses the same value. MER-ALL-018 Section 2 explains what fRsi means and what 0.75 does and does not promise. Reveals, ceiling junctions, party wall returns and floor junctions are the details clause 8.2.16 is aimed at.

### What the Building Regulations ask of a renovated wall

Approved Document L Volume 1 (England) treats IWI on an existing wall as renovating a thermal element. The result should be "no worse than that of the element before it was renovated" (paragraph 4.11). Table 4.3 gives, for "wall – internal or external insulation", a threshold U-value of 0.70 W/(m²·K) and an improved value of 0.30 W/(m²·K). Where the improved value is not "technically or functionally feasible" or would not achieve a simple payback of 15 years or less, the element should be upgraded to the lowest U-value that meets both tests (paragraph 4.13). A lesser standard may be appropriate if meeting the improved value would reduce the room's floor area "by more than 5%" (Table 4.3, footnote 6), and "may be acceptable where work complies with Part C" on condensation (paragraph 4.13).

Set that beside the Loughborough finding that "the relative moisture risk increased more rapidly below a U-value of about 0.8 W/(m2·K)" in its models (section 6). On a traditional wall the improved value and the moisture risk can pull in different directions. Resolving that is design work; recording the wall well is yours.

**Visual:** Referral route as a flow: assessor records wall, condition, defects, mould, services, ventilation (PAS 2035 7.3.2) → coordinator → designer: moisture management in line with BS 5250 (8.2.10), junction details (8.2.15), fRsi not less than 0.75 or a published detail (8.2.16) → specialist hygrothermal assessment where needed (4.3.3 note 1; IWI guide paragraph 42). Mark the assessor's box "observe and record; no modelling".

## Section 7: What the National Audit Office Found

### The figures

The NAO report "Energy efficiency installations under the Energy Company Obligation" (HC 1334, 14 October 2025) rests on site visits by Ofgem's auditors between June and August 2025 to "a statistically representative random sample of 758 homes" (paragraph 2.4): 380 with IWI and 378 with external wall insulation (EWI) (Figure 6, notes). It estimated:
- **IWI:** "29%, or between 9,000 and 13,000 homes" have major issues requiring remediation (paragraph 2.3).
- **EWI:** "98%, or between 22,000 and 23,000 homes" (paragraph 2.2).

The ranges are 95% confidence intervals (paragraph 2.2, note 11). They cover homes retrofitted before 16 January 2025; later homes were not audited (summary, paragraph 11).

### What "major issues" means for IWI

| Audit outcome, IWI (Figure 6) | Share | Estimated homes |
|---|---|---|
| Major issues posing immediate health and safety risks | 2% | 300 to 1,400 |
| Major issues affecting the insulation's performance | 27% | 8,300 to 11,600 |
| Minor issues that may affect performance over time | 35% | 11,200 to 14,700 |
| Pass | 35% | 11,200 to 14,700 |

The 27% are "often creating the risk of condensation and mould" (paragraph 2.3). The immediate health and safety issues include inadequate ventilation and poor electrical safety (summary, paragraph 11). **The four shares shown sum to 99%, not 100%.** Figure 6 also has a category for minor issues not materially affecting performance, whose IWI value could not be read in the copy opened; rounding may also account for the gap. "Pass" means the installation fully met PAS 2035 (paragraph 2.3). For EWI, the 98% comprises 92% performance issues, often creating "the risk of water ingress and mould", and 6% immediate health and safety risks (paragraph 2.2).

### Context

To the end of March 2025, about 42,500 homes received IWI under ECO4 and about 2,700 under GBIS (Figure 2). TrustMark's own audit of 2,674 IWI homes, which "aimed to target the riskier projects", gave similar results (paragraph 2.5). The department "believes that the non-compliance rate will be lower" for installations from 16 January 2025, but has no current estimate (paragraph 2.6). Advice from TrustMark to the department suggests most IWI remediation "should cost from £250 to £6,000 per property" if done before major damage occurs (paragraph 2.19). "The original installer is liable for all the cost of remediating non-compliant installations" (summary, paragraph 15), and the department has said "no household with a faulty installation should have to pay to fix the issue" (paragraph 2.21).

On the NAO's estimate, roughly three in ten ECO4 and GBIS IWI homes installed before 16 January 2025 have a major issue. Those homes are in the stock you assess. A householder who says the walls were insulated "on a grant" is a reason to look carefully at the Section 4 zones and to know the route below. It is not a reason to assume the installation in front of you is faulty.

### The free audit route

The sources do not say quite the same thing:
- **TrustMark's leaflet** is headed "Act now if you have had External Wall Insulation installed under ECO4 or GBIS schemes". TrustMark "will arrange a free audit of your home", and "Any problems found will be fixed at no cost to you". Contact: 0333 555 1234 (Option 1), onsitebookings@trustmark.org.uk. TrustMark's "Free Find & Fix Audit Offer" page (updated 23 October 2026) also describes a "Free external wall insulation audit".
- **Citizens Advice, "Get help with faulty solid wall insulation"** (Wales version, no update date shown, read 27 October 2026), says the free check "is for wall insulation installed inside and outside your home", for work between April 2022 and January 2025 under ECO4 or GBIS where the household got help with the cost; if problems are found, TrustMark should arrange the fix and "You will not have to pay anything". It gives TrustMark's number, 0333 555 1234 (charges apply), and Ofgem's helpline, 0808 169 4447, for households unsure which scheme they used.
- **The NAO** says the department "is encouraging households contacted for an audit of external or internal wall insulation installed under ECO4 and GBIS to allow an inspection" (summary, paragraph 12).

So Citizens Advice and the NAO include internal wall insulation; TrustMark's own leaflet and page mention only external. Whether a particular home qualifies is for TrustMark or Ofgem to confirm, not you. Your role is to signpost: give the householder the contact details and let them make the call.

## Section 8: Recording, Referring and the Traffic Light

### The vocabulary

Recognise and record. Say what is possible or likely. Recommend professional advice. Do not call an installation defective, non-compliant or safe, or name a cause as certain. Do not tell a householder to remove boards, lift floors, take off faceplates, drill or strip anything: if something needs opening up to be understood, recommend professional advice.

PAS 2035:2023 asks for the "location and severity" of defects, condensation and mould (clause 7.3.2 d)), not their cause. Where defects are found, the assessment report "shall include identification of such defects in two categories": "defects that need to be repaired before any retrofit work can proceed" and "defects that are recommended but not an essential prerequisite to retrofit" (clause 7.4.2). Categorising a defect by what you can see is part of your report; naming its cause is not. Where you cannot tell which category applies, say so and refer.

### The traffic light

This is this course's recommended way of sorting what you see in a home with IWI. It is not a legal or scheme classification.

**Green: monitor.** IWI present; no visible mould, staining or condensation at any defect zone; ventilation present and working. Record the extent of the IWI, the zones you looked at, ventilation, and "no visible mould at time of visit". A visual inspection cannot rule out hidden moisture; say so.

**Amber: investigate.** Mould at reveals, ceiling edge, party wall return or skirting; mould around sockets on insulated walls; IWI stopping part way along a wall; external defects on a wall with IWI; a PIV unit, or no working extract. Record location, extent, pattern and context, and recommend that the coordinator or designer reviews the detail. If the householder reports ECO4 or GBIS solid wall insulation, signpost the TrustMark route.

**Red: get professional advice.** A soft, springy or sagging floor near an external wall; swollen, stained or loose skirtings with a musty smell; water near or tracking through electrical fittings, or exposed cabling; extensive mould, or mould in a home with vulnerable occupants; active water entry; cracking, bulging or other signs of structural movement. Record promptly and in writing, recommend the named professional, and warn occupants not to use affected electrical fittings.

### Referral triggers

These are this course's recommended practice, not legal requirements.
- **Possible timber decay at a floor, or structural movement:** a structural engineer or building surveyor.
- **Electrical:** urgent advice from a qualified electrician. The NAO's examples include live cabling left exposed (Figure 6), and its IWI health and safety findings include "poor electrical safety" (summary, paragraph 11).
- **Gas and flues:** follow your organisation's gas safety procedure and the emergency arrangements in force. Do not investigate.
- **A low-level band of damp you cannot account for:** a specialist damp survey (IWI best practice guide, paragraph 96).
- **Grant-funded solid wall insulation:** signpost TrustMark (Section 7).

### What to photograph

PAS 2035:2023 clause 7.4.1 requires "a photographic record of all the recorded features of the building and of any identified defects". For IWI, this course recommends: each insulated wall, showing where the IWI stops; each defect zone you looked at, problem or not; sockets on insulated walls, faceplates in place; reveals, with lining thickness at the frame; the external face of each insulated wall; and ventilation.

The guide asks installers to photograph the work before and during installation, including the stripped wall and joists, any VCL, the air barrier and its taping, insulation thickness and services (paragraph 104). If the householder holds such photographs or handover documents, record that they exist and what they show: they are better evidence of what is behind the board than anything you can see.

### A worked record

A two-storey solid brick terrace, IWI on front and rear walls, December visit. The householder reports it was installed in 2023 "on a grant".

"Rear bedroom, north-east external wall, IWI present (lining approx. 70 mm visible at window frame; reveals approx. 10 mm lining). Black mould spotting on both reveals and the bottom corners of the sill, approx. 300 mm high each side. Condensation on the inside of the glass at time of visit. Mould line approx. 1.5 m along the ceiling-to-wall junction on the same wall, darkest in the corner by the party wall; no lining return visible along the party wall. Two double sockets on the insulated wall, faceplates flush with board, no service void evident, no staining. Floor firm near the external wall; skirting sound. Room 16°C, 71% RH at 09:40 (single spot reading). Bathroom extract runs; no trickle vents in bedroom; PIV unit in loft. Outside: rear downpipe disconnected at the shoe, wall below stained; pointing eroded at first-floor level. Pattern consistent with possible condensation at cold junctions (reveals, ceiling edge) and possible water loading on the rear wall from the downpipe. Recommend the coordinator and designer review the reveal and ceiling details and the ventilation before any further work. Householder given TrustMark's contact details for the free audit route for solid wall insulation installed under ECO4 or GBIS; eligibility for TrustMark to confirm."

It does not say the IWI is defective or that the PIV caused the mould, gives no meter reading, and tells no one to open anything up. It locates, measures, gives context, uses "possible", and refers.

**Visual:** Traffic light with three panels. Green "Monitor": IWI present, no visible mould at any defect zone, ventilation working. Amber "Investigate": mould at reveals, ceiling edge, party wall return or sockets; partial IWI; external defects; PIV or no working extract. Red "Get professional advice": soft or sagging floor near an external wall, water near electrics, extensive mould or vulnerable occupants, active water entry, structural movement. Under each panel the action: record; record and refer to the coordinator; record and recommend the named professional.

## Key Takeaways

The points to carry away:
- An IWI system has up to five jobs: insulation, fixing to the wall, an air barrier on the warm side (always), a VCL on the warm side (only where the strategy calls for one), and a service void so services do not puncture the layers behind (IWI best practice guide, paragraphs 36, 66, 83).
- Once insulated internally, a solid wall is on the cold side: colder and slower to dry. The face where insulation meets masonry is where interstitial condensation risk concentrates.
- For solid walls the guide recommends a moisture-open build-up, or a moisture-closed one with a ventilated cavity of at least 25 mm on the cold side (paragraph 45). PAS 2035:2023 says materials "should match the moisture strategy of the existing building" (8.2.10, note).
- Modelling for DESNZ found IWI "always led to an increased moisture risk", rising with thickness, with the wood-fibre vapour-permeable system relatively lower risk (DEEP Report 6.03).
- The defect zones are services, reveals, joist ends, ceiling and party wall junctions, floor and skirting junctions, partial coverage and impermeable systems on breathable walls. The guide advises returning insulation at least 400 mm along junctions and warns that party wall returns can make the neighbour's wall colder.
- PAS 2035:2023 clause 4.3.3 note 1 says a BS EN ISO 13788 assessment "might not be sufficient" for solid walls with IWI in exposed locations; clause 8.2.16 requires fRsi not less than 0.75 for details that are not published good-practice details. The moisture risk assessment is design work.
- The NAO estimated that 29% of ECO4 and GBIS IWI homes installed before 16 January 2025, or 9,000 to 13,000 homes, have major issues requiring remediation: 27% affecting performance, often with a risk of condensation and mould, and 2% posing immediate health and safety risks.
- Recognise and record; say "possible" or "likely"; recommend professional advice; treat possible joist decay as structural; never tell a householder to open up walls. For solid wall insulation installed under ECO4 or GBIS, signpost TrustMark; eligibility is for TrustMark or Ofgem to confirm.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** According to the IWI best practice guide, where should the air barrier and any vapour control layer be placed?

A) On the cold side of the insulation, against the masonry
B) On the warm side of the insulation
C) Only around windows and doors
D) On the external face of the wall

**Correct answer: B.** An air barrier "shall be installed in all internal wall insulation systems on the warm side", and any VCL "should be on the warm side of the insulation" (Section 1).

---

**Q2.** For insulating a traditional solid wall internally, what does the IWI best practice guide recommend?

A) A polythene vapour barrier fixed to the wall in every case
B) Dot-and-dab foil-backed boards to leave an air gap
C) A moisture-open build-up, or a moisture-closed system with a ventilated cavity of at least 25 mm on the cold side
D) Tanking the inner face of the wall before boarding

**Correct answer: C.** Paragraph 45 recommends a moisture-open build-up for solid walls, or a moisture-closed system with a ventilated cavity of at least 25 mm; dot-and-dab "should be avoided" (paragraph 92) (Sections 1 and 2).

---

**Q3.** What does PAS 2035:2023 clause 4.3.3 note 1 say about solid walls with internal insulation in exposed locations?

A) They must never be insulated
B) An interstitial condensation assessment to BS EN ISO 13788 might not be sufficient as the basis for a moisture risk assessment
C) The assessor must carry out a BS EN 15026 simulation
D) They are exempt from BS 5250

**Correct answer: B.** The note says the BS EN ISO 13788 assessment "might not be sufficient as the basis for a moisture risk assessment" for walls such as "solid walls with internal wall insulation (IWI) proposed in exposed locations" (Section 6).

---

**Q4.** The NAO estimated what share of homes given internal wall insulation under ECO4 or GBIS before 16 January 2025 have major issues requiring remediation?

A) 2%
B) 29%, or between 9,000 and 13,000 homes
C) 35%
D) 98%, or between 22,000 and 23,000 homes

**Correct answer: B.** Paragraph 2.3 gives 29%, or 9,000 to 13,000 homes, for IWI: 27% performance issues and 2% immediate health and safety risks. The 98% figure is for external wall insulation (Section 7).

---

**Q5.** In a solid-walled house with IWI, the floor near the external wall of a first-floor bedroom feels soft and springy and the skirting is stained and loose. What is the appropriate response?

A) Lift a floorboard to check the joist end
B) Advise the householder to remove a section of the board to inspect the wall
C) Record the location and signs, treat it as a possible structural matter, and recommend advice from a structural engineer or building surveyor
D) Take a moisture meter reading on the board and report it as the moisture content of the joist

**Correct answer: C.** Joist ends in internally insulated solid walls are at increased risk of decay; possible decay at a floor is a red, structural referral. The assessor records and refers, and never opens up or tells the householder to (Sections 4 and 8).
