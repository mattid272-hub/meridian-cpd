## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:
- Tell a cold loft, a warm (rafter-level) roof, a flat roof and a room in the roof apart, and say where the moisture risk sits in each.
- Recognise the site evidence of roof-space condensation, roof leaks, plumbing leaks and blocked ventilation, and record it without naming a cause.
- Explain how loft insulation, hatch sealing and room-in-roof work change the temperature and ventilation of a roof space, including the effect on tanks, pipes, cables, downlighters and ducts.
- Get into a loft safely or decide not to, and record what you could and could not see.
- Record findings within PAS 2035:2023, refer each to the right person, and name TrustMark first where the insulation was funded under ECO4 or the Great British Insulation Scheme.

## Introduction

The loft is where most homes get their first insulation measure, and where many assessors spend the least time. You put your head through the hatch, measure the depth, take a photograph and climb down. This course is about the other things visible from that hatch: the felt, the eaves, the tank, the cables, the stains on the timbers. Each says something about how the roof space handles moisture, and each can change when insulation goes in.

Part 1 of this series, **MER-ALL-018**, covered condensation physics, the four kinds of damp, the "twins", moisture meters, and the mould band a cold eaves junction leaves on a top-floor ceiling. None of that is repeated here. Part 2 covers floors. Data entry stays in **MER-DEA-008** (roof insulation in RdSAP 10) and **MER-DEA-004** (room-in-roof classification); fire detail in **MER-ALL-004**; ventilation assessment in **MER-RA-008**.

This course does not train you to design a roof, specify ventilation, or judge whether timber is decayed. You recognise, record and refer.

### Safe access first

The government's room-in-roof guide reminds assessors and installers of "the Work at Height Regulations 2005" (BEIS, *Retrofit Room in Roof Insulation*, January 2022, para 143), and notes that roof work can involve "tight and confined spaces" (para 148). The RdSAP conventions ask you to measure loft insulation only "Where safe and practicable access to the loft is possible" (convention 3.04).

Meridian's practice, stated plainly:
- Treat loft access as work at height, every time.
- Do not go into a loft you judge unsafe: no secure ladder, a damaged hatch, no light, or no boards or joists you can see to stand on.
- Stand and kneel only on boards or joists. Historic England notes the risk of "injury caused by stepping between joists" (HEAG077, s. 3.9, p. 13).
- Do not lift insulation, boards or felt. Look from where you can stand safely and record what you could not see.
- If the hatch "could not be used on the date of the site visit", for example because of an "obstruction preventing access for health and safety reasons", record "access, loft insulation unknown" (convention 3.04).

## Section 1: Four Kinds of Roof, Four Places for Moisture

**In short:** Before you look for moisture, work out what kind of roof you are in. A cold loft has insulation at ceiling level and an unheated space above. A warm roof has insulation at the rafters, so the roof volume is inside. Flat roofs differ by where their insulation sits. A room in the roof combines heated rooms with cold pockets around them. Each puts the cold, moisture-sensitive surfaces in a different place.

### Cold roof and warm roof

Historic England's guide to rafter-level insulation gives the two definitions (HEAG070, *Insulating Pitched Roofs at Rafter Level*, v1.3, January 2016, p. 7):
- A **cold roof** has its insulation at the ceiling of the top floor, "leaving an unheated roof space (attic or loft) above the insulation".
- A **warm roof** has insulation "between or just under or over the sloping rafters", so the whole volume under the roof can be heated and used.

The same guide puts the risk in one line: "The most significant risk is that of creating condensation" (p. 1).

RdSAP draws its heat loss boundary at the ceiling and records only joist insulation where joists and rafters are both insulated, because the roof U-value "greatly depends on the air changes per hour in the unheated loft space" and "RdSAP assumes that the loft space is ventilated" (RdSAP 10 Specification v4.0, 5.11.1). The energy model, too, treats a ventilated loft as normal.

### Flat roofs

The design guidance for flat roofs was not opened, so they appear here only briefly. Approved Document L's Table C1 flat roof rows say "Also see BS 6229 for design guidance" (ADL 2021 and 2026). The BEIS guide says warm flat roofs, with insulation over the joists, "do not require ventilation" (Table 1, p. 22). From below you usually cannot tell which kind you have. Record the ceiling, any staining and any vents at the roof edge, and say what you could not see.

### Rooms in the roof

A room in the roof is a set of elements: sloping ceilings ("skeilings"), flat ceilings, dwarf stud walls, gables, dormer cheeks and dormer roofs (BEIS para 22). Around them sit spaces you may never see: "Roof spaces above flat ceilings and behind dwarf walls have long been used for storage" (para 19).

For ECO4, the "residual area" of a room-in-roof measure is "the loft area in the same continuous air space as the room-in-roof" (ECO4 Delivery v4.0, 5.33); classification is in 5.31 and MER-DEA-004. For moisture, "same continuous air space" is the useful phrase: the pockets behind the dwarf walls and above the flat ceiling are one roof space.

### Where the cold surfaces sit

| Roof type | Insulation | Cold, moisture-sensitive surfaces | What the opened sources say about ventilation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold loft | Ceiling joists | Roof timbers, felt or membrane, tanks and pipes, in a colder space (HEAG077 s. 3.6) | Keep insulation clear at the eaves; ventilation through eaves, gables, ridge or coverings (HEAG077 s. 3.5) |
| Warm roof, over rafters | Above the rafters | Structure on "the warm, dry side" is the aim (HEAG070 p. 9) | Design question; not covered here |
| Warm roof, between rafters | Between, possibly under, rafters | Timbers and membrane next to the insulation | Air path below the roof finish, "50mm is recommended" (HEAG070 p. 14, Fig. 12); otherwise a "carefully considered moisture strategy" (BEIS para 85) |
| Flat roof | Over, between or under joists | Depends on type; often not visible | Warm flat: "do not require ventilation" (BEIS Table 1); others: BS 6229 (not opened) |
| Room in roof | Mixed | Pockets behind dwarf walls and above flat ceilings; sloping sections | Ventilating enclosed loft spaces is "critical to managing moisture" (BEIS para 83) |

## Section 2: Where Loft Moisture Comes From, and How It Leaves

**In short:** Moisture reaches a roof space mainly as warm, moist air from the rooms below, through gaps at the hatch, around pipes and cables, and through ceilings. Tanks without lids add more. Rain gets in through defects in the roof covering. Ventilation at the eaves, ridge, gables or through the covering carries moisture out. Insulation pushed into the eaves can reduce that, and impervious felt can trap moisture. Record the felt, the eaves, the hatch and the penetrations.

### From below: air from the rooms

Historic England's cold roof guide names the main route. Gaps in the ceiling "should be sealed to prevent moist air from the habitable accommodation entering the roof space", "especially from areas of high humidity such as bath and shower rooms", and "All cracks and holes, particularly around pipes and cables where they pass through the ceilings, should be sealed" (HEAG077, *Insulating Pitched Roofs at Ceiling Level*, v1.1, April 2016, s. 3.11, p. 14).

The hatch is a weak point: "Gaps at the perimeter will allow heat loss to simply by-pass any amount of insulation" (s. 3.10, p. 13). The same gap lets moist air through. In an existing house the plaster ceiling usually serves as the air barrier, because "It is rarely possible to install an airtight membrane above an existing ceiling" (Checklist, p. 7). Every hole in it matters.

The BEIS guide says the same for room-in-roof work: "Air leakage into roof voids can carry a high amount of moisture", and "An air barrier should form part of all RIRI construction on the warm side" (para 74).

### From within, and from above

A cold water tank is a moisture source: "Tight fitting lids to water tanks will assist in removing a potential source of water vapour in the roof space" (HEAG077 s. 3.7, p. 11). A tank with no lid, or a loose one, is worth recording.

Rain gets in where the covering has failed. The BEIS survey table asks surveyors to "Identify any loose, damaged or missing tiles or slates" and whether "gutters, flashings and external pipework" are in good condition (Table 1). The government's damp and mould guidance lists "missing or broken roof finishes" among its external checks (updated 1 April 2026). From the ground you may see slipped slates or a missing ridge tile; from inside, daylight. Record both.

### How it leaves: ventilation

A cold loft is normally ventilated "by open eaves or by vents in gable walls, ridges or through the roof coverings" (HEAG077 s. 3.5, p. 10). Historic England is direct about what happens if that changes: "Reducing the ventilation can cause problems of condensation", "particularly where the roof is provided with an impervious roofing/sarking felt", and "It is important that the insulation is kept free at the eaves to allow for ventilation" (s. 3.5).

Its Figure 4 shows the failure: "Insulation here is in contact with an impermeable felt and is also blocking the ventilation path to the open eaves." Its Figure 3 shows one way to keep the path open, a "Proprietary spacer to provide 25mm minimum ventilation gap at eaves". That is a diagram label, not a rule. This course gives no eaves ventilation area, because the sources that set one (Approved Document C Section 6 and BS 5250) were not opened.

### Felt and membranes

The BEIS guide explains that from around the 1930s the older, vapour-permeable layer under roof coverings gave way to bituminous felt, and that timbers in these roofs "are at higher risk of moisture problems because the impervious roofing felt traps moisture" (para 9, p. 9). Vapour-permeable membranes appeared in the 1990s (para 13), but "Vapour permeable membranes do not all have the same ability to deal with air borne moisture" (para 15).

You cannot tell a membrane's properties by looking. Record what you see: black bituminous felt, a plastic-looking membrane, sarking boards, or no underlay. If a product name is printed on it, photograph it.

### What you record

- Underlay type as seen, or "not visible".
- Eaves: insulation clear, pushed in, or not visible; any vents at eaves, gables, ridge or tiles, photographed.
- Hatch: insulated, sealed at the edges, or neither.
- Penetrations: pipes, cables, flues and fan ducts through the ceiling, sealed or open.
- Tank: lid present and fitting, or not.

## Section 3: What Insulation Does to the Loft

**In short:** Ceiling insulation keeps heat in the rooms, so the loft gets colder, and pipes, tanks, felt and timbers sit closer to the conditions for condensation and freezing. Most of the heat saving comes from the first 100 mm; going on to 270 mm saves far fewer watts but cools the loft further. Approved Document L asks for roof condensation risk to be assessed and, where appropriate in existing homes, for continuous insulation at the eaves. Both are design tasks.

### A colder loft

Historic England puts the trade-off simply: "the insulation at ceiling level will make the roof space colder thus increasing the risk of freezing", and "The cold surfaces of pipes and tanks are also common areas for condensation to occur" (HEAG077 s. 3.6, p. 11). Where moisture is absorbed into roof timbers, the risk is "active timber decay" (p. 6).

No source opened for this course gives a figure for how much colder a loft gets. The worked numbers below show the heat flow that insulation cuts off, which is why the loft cools. They do not give a loft temperature.

### Worked numbers: heat loss through a 45 m² ceiling

Inputs: RdSAP 10 Specification v4.0, Table 16, column (1), insulation at joists, for mineral wool or expanded polystyrene; the table allows for joists and "the sheltering effect of unheated roof space". Heat loss coefficient = U-value × area.

| Insulation at joists | U-value (W/m²K) | × 45 m² = heat loss (W/K) |
|---|---|---|
| None | 2.3 | 103.5 |
| 50 mm | 0.68 | 30.6 |
| 100 mm | 0.40 | 18.0 |
| 125 mm | 0.35 | 15.75 |
| 200 mm | 0.21 | 9.45 |
| 270 mm | 0.16 | 7.2 |
| 400 mm or more | 0.11 | 4.95 |

The steps:
- None to 100 mm: 103.5 − 18.0 = 85.5 W/K saved.
- 100 mm to 270 mm: 18.0 − 7.2 = 10.8 W/K saved.
- 270 mm to 400 mm: 7.2 − 4.95 = 2.25 W/K saved.
- At a 20 K difference between rooms and loft, the 100 to 270 mm step cuts heat flow into the loft by 10.8 × 20 = 216 W.

On Table 16, 125 mm gives 0.35 W/m²K, the ADL threshold, and 270 mm gives 0.16, the improved value. These are model inputs showing the shape of the curve, not a design or a measured loft.

The first 100 mm does most of the work. Every millimetre after that saves less heat, but still makes the loft a little colder. A top-up is still worth doing; it is also the moment the cold surfaces in the loft start to matter more.

Outside the regulations, the government's damp and mould guidance says "Improving loft insulation to a minimum of 270mm is a particularly effective means of keeping homes warmer" (updated 1 April 2026).

### Continuity and ventilation at the eaves

Two pieces of advice meet at the eaves. ADL asks for loft insulation to carry on over the wall head without thinning. Historic England asks that "the insulation is kept free at the eaves to allow for ventilation" (HEAG077 s. 3.5). Where insulation runs up a short sloping ceiling, it adds that "Physical contact between the roof and the insulation would increase the risk of condensation", and uses the 50 mm gap for new buildings "as a benchmark for existing buildings" (s. 4.2, p. 21).

Both can be met, as the spacer in Historic England's Figure 3 shows, but how is a design matter. For you, the eaves is a place to look closely, photograph, and record: insulation thin or missing over the wall head, or pushed into the eaves. The first can leave the ceiling-edge mould band described in MER-ALL-018 Section 3. The second can reduce ventilation.

### Boarded lofts

Historic England notes that walkways "will reduce the risk of damage to ceilings and of injury caused by stepping between joists", that "Even lightweight goods can compress insulation", and that a ventilated space, "ideally 50mm or so", should be left between crawl boards and insulation (s. 3.9, p. 13).

You do not lift boards. Where insulation is fully obstructed, "boarded or obscured by items stored", convention 3.04 gives "pitched, access, loft insulation unknown" unless there is documentary evidence, the householder lifts the boards, or you can evidence insulation at several points through gaps or from the edges. Record boarding and storage as findings: they affect any future top-up.

## Section 4: Telling Roof Moisture Apart

**In short:** Water in a roof space can come from condensation, a roof leak, a plumbing or tank leak, or condensation on cold pipes; on the ceiling below, a cold eaves junction can add a mould band. They can look alike and coexist. Record where the wetting or staining is, its extent, and what is above, below and outside it. Do not name the cause: confirming the source is for a roofer, plumber or surveyor.

### Why they get confused

All of these leave staining, wet timber or mould. A roof leak and condensation can both wet the felt and drip onto insulation. A tank overflow and a slipped slate can both stain a bedroom ceiling. MER-ALL-018 set out the government guidance's four kinds of damp (Section 1) and the twins (Section 4); in a roof space they become the grid below.

### The grid

The "What the sources say" column is quoted from the sources named. The "Where it tends to show" column is this course's practical reading of them, not a quotation. Treat it as a reason to look closely and record, never as proof.

| Possible source | What the sources say | Where it tends to show (practical reading) | What to record |
|---|---|---|---|
| Condensation in the roof space | Moist air from rooms enters the roof space (HEAG077 s. 3.11); impervious felt traps moisture (BEIS para 9); "spots of mould growth" and staining or fungal growth on timbers (HEAG077 p. 6, s. 3.1) | Spread across the underside of felt or sarking and timbers, often over bathrooms or near the hatch; droplets on cold felt | Extent; underlay; eaves; vents; hatch and penetrations; bathroom below |
| Roof leak | "loose, damaged or missing tiles or slates"; gutters and flashings (BEIS Table 1); "missing or broken roof finishes" (DM guidance) | Localised, under a defect you can see outside or as daylight inside: chimney, valley, abutment, ridge | Position against the outside defect; photographs inside and from the ground |
| Plumbing or tank leak | Traumatic damp includes "burst pipes or defective water storage vessels" (DM guidance) | Around the tank, valves, pipe runs or overflow; stain follows the pipe | Positions; wet insulation; ceiling stain below |
| Condensation on cold pipes and tanks | "The cold surfaces of pipes and tanks are also common areas for condensation to occur" (HEAG077 s. 3.6) | Drips below bare pipes or a tank in a cold loft | Pipes and tank insulated or not; drip marks |
| Cold eaves junction (from below) | Eaves insulation "without any reduction in thickness" (ADL 2021 4.17(i)(ii)); MER-ALL-018 Section 3 | Mould band along the top-floor ceiling edge on external walls, darkest at corners | Ceiling pattern; insulation over the wall head, as far as safely visible |

### Recording habits that help

- **Position.** Tie each stain to something nameable: "under the bathroom", "below the valley", "at the chimney stack on the party wall".
- **Above, below and outside.** Look at the ceiling below a loft stain, the loft above a ceiling stain, and the roof from the ground, where you can do so safely.
- **When.** Ask whether it appears after rain, in cold weather or after a bath, and record the answer as the householder's report.
- **More than one.** Two causes can share one stain. Record what you see without choosing.

MER-ALL-018 Section 5 covers moisture meters. No source opened for this course sets a moisture content for roof timbers, so do not report a meter reading on a rafter or joist as one.

## Section 5: Rooms in the Roof and Warm Roofs

**In short:** A room in the roof has heated rooms surrounded by cold pockets, and insulation between rafters leaves little room for air. The BEIS guide says ventilating the enclosed loft spaces is "critical to managing moisture", and that 50 mm should be kept between insulation and membrane. Rafter depth limits what insulation can achieve without extra layers, which is why this is design work. Record the pockets, the ventilation you can see and the chimneys.

### The cold pockets

The space over the flat ceiling and behind each dwarf wall is a small cold loft, often with only a storage door. The BEIS guide says "Appropriate ventilation of enclosed loft spaces surrounding the RIR is critical to managing moisture in these spaces" (para 83, p. 34), and asks surveyors to "Check loft hatches and storage doors to uninsulated spaces are properly insulated and airtight" (Table 1).

From the room you can see the doors, their condition, and whether the space behind is used for storage. You may be able to look in. Meridian's practice: do not crawl in; these are the "tight and confined spaces" of para 148.

### The sloping sections

Where a sloping ceiling follows the rafters, the BEIS survey table says "A minimum of 50mm ventilation gap should be retained between insulation and membrane" (Table 1, p. 21), and asks surveyors to "Note whether ventilation is continuous between rafters" and to inspect eaves, ridge and tile ventilation "and take photos to establish total ventilation sizes" (pp. 21 to 22).

Where rafters are filled completely, "Constructions that fully insulate between rafters need a carefully considered moisture strategy" (para 85, p. 35), because without a ventilated cavity moisture can become trapped and rot timbers. Any product between rafters should not "block any part of the ventilated cavity" (para 86). If vapour control layers are specified, "these should be on the warm side of the insulation" (para 124).

Historic England adds a caution for older buildings: of vapour barriers, it says it is "very difficult to install them in historic buildings", and that it is "preferable within historic buildings to use vapour permeable insulation" (HEAG070 p. 18). Over-rafter insulation leaves the structure on the warm, dry side (p. 9); warm, moist air crossing a cold thermal bridge will condense there (p. 17). MER-RA-010 covers what you record at junctions.

### Worked numbers: rafter depth

Writing about short sloping ceilings, Historic England says "The maximum insulation thickness is typically 50mm less than the depth of rafters" (HEAG077 s. 4, p. 15), which leaves the air path. RdSAP Table 16, column (2), gives U-values for "Insulation at rafters but U-value is attributed to the ceiling area", for mineral wool or expanded polystyrene. That column models a pitched roof insulated at rafters over a loft; roof rooms use Table 17 (not used here). So these figures show the depth problem, not a room-in-roof U-value.

| Rafter depth | Insulation between (depth − 50 mm) | Table 16 col. (2) U-value (W/m²K) |
|---|---|---|
| 100 mm | 50 mm | 0.88 |
| 150 mm | 100 mm | 0.54 |
| 225 mm | 175 mm | 0.32 |

The BEIS guide's targets are 0.16 W/m²K at ceiling level and 0.18 for flat or vaulted roofs (para 58, p. 29). None of the three reaches 0.18 with insulation between the rafters alone. Historic England's worked example needs an insulating sarking board "of about 70 mm thickness" over 150 mm between the rafters to reach 0.18 (HEAG070 p. 20).

ADL anticipates this: where the roof standard "would limit head room", a lesser standard may be appropriate, with insulation depth plus any air gap "at least to the depth of the rafters" (ADL 2021 Table 4.3 note 3; ADL 2026 Table 3.3 note 3 says "a higher U-value", with the same condition). Table C1's room-in-roof row asks for condensation risk to be assessed "(particularly interstitial condensation)". These are decisions about layers and moisture, which belong to the designer.

### Chimneys and dormers

The BEIS guide says "Decommissioned masonry chimneys on gable ends or party walls should generally be insulated", but "In use masonry chimneys on gable or party walls should not be insulated" (para 100, p. 36). Record whether each chimney breast in the roof serves an appliance, if the householder knows, and any staining around it. Dormer cheeks count as wall and dormer roofs as roof in ADL's U-value table (2021 Table 4.3 note 2; 2026 Table 3.3 note 2); record them as separate elements.

### Investigation is not your call

The BEIS guide says "Retrofit Assessors, Designers and Installers must do the necessary and sometimes destructive investigation work" (para 18). PAS 2035:2023 notes that identifying defects might need invasive assessments, "including opening up of the building fabric" (7.3.2 d), NOTE 1), and that where invasive tests are appropriate, "the retrofit design can specify" them (7.3.2, NOTE 11). Meridian's practice: the assessor does not open up, lift boards or remove linings. Record what you could see and what you could not, so that the Retrofit Coordinator and Designer can decide what investigation to commission.

## Section 6: Tanks, Pipes, Cables, Downlighters and Ducts

**In short:** When a loft gets colder, the services in it matter more. Tanks and pipes in a cold loft should be insulated, with the area under a tank left clear unless the tank is raised. Cables can overheat under insulation. Recessed downlighters bring fire and air leakage risks. Intake and exhaust ducts through a loft should be insulated, with a thickness set in ADF 2026 from March 2027. Record each item, and refer anything needing an electrician, plumber or gas engineer.

### Tanks and pipes

Historic England says plumbing and tanks in a cold roof should be insulated against freezing, and "the area below water tanks should be kept free of insulation, unless the water tank is raised well above the joists" (HEAG077 s. 3.6 and 3.7, p. 11). The BEIS guide says that where roof spaces will become colder, "Water tanks and pipes must be properly insulated" (p. 27). The damp and mould guidance lists "insulating hot water tanks and pipes" among energy efficiency measures.

Record: tank present, lid on, tank insulated, area beneath it insulated or clear, pipes lagged or bare, overflow visible.

### Cables and downlighters

"Electric cables give off heat when in use and may overheat where they are covered by thermal insulation" (HEAG077 s. 3.12, p. 14). The BEIS guide agrees: "If wiring is encapsulated in, or directly behind insulation, there can be a risk of overheating" (para 132), and where installations are a concern, "a qualified electrician should be instructed to assess and make them safe" (para 150). It also says "Recessed lights present fire and air leakage risks in roof insulation systems" (para 105).

Record cables on top of insulation, cables buried, any damaged cable, scorch mark or uncovered junction box, and the backs of downlighters, with or without covers and with insulation against or over them. Do not move cables or insulation. MER-ALL-004 covers the fire detail.

### Ducts

Fan and ventilation ducts often cross a loft. Approved Document F deals with their insulation in two ways:
- **ADF 2021 (England), Appendix C, Part 2b.2**, for continuous extract and MVHR: "Have the heat recovery unit and all ductwork been effectively insulated and sealed for all heated and unheated spaces?" No thickness was found in the 2021 text searched.
- **ADF 2026 (England), 1.83, from 24 March 2027**: intake and exhaust ductwork through "unheated areas (e.g. loft spaces and sub-floor voids)" insulated with the equivalent of at least 25 mm at 0.04 W/(m·K) or less; through heated areas, at least 50 mm, "fully vapour sealed to minimise the risk of condensation forming".

The Welsh 2026 edition was not checked for this paragraph. Record ducts as lagged, unlagged, crushed or disconnected, and where they end.

### Flues, asbestos and wildlife

Where roof work could affect a combustion appliance or its ventilation, the BEIS guide says "Assessor should take expert advice" (Table 1, p. 24). It notes asbestos may be found in roofs, including in plumbing and electrical systems, fire proofing and textured coatings (para 149). Historic England's checklist asks, before work, for checks for asbestos and for bats or nesting birds (HEAG077 Checklist, p. 7). Do not touch or sample anything you suspect.

### What the Retrofit Coordinator will look for later

PAS 2035:2023 gives, as an example, what loft insulation photographs should show after installation (9.2, NOTE 2), including "ventilation safeguarded in all loft areas", "insulated tanks and pipes", and "insulated and airtight loft hatch, downlighters and high-power cables safeguarded". Your pre-works photographs of the same items give the designer and coordinator their starting point.

Amber ("record; refer to the Retrofit Coordinator"): bare pipes or tank; insulation under a tank that is not raised; no tank lid; cables buried; insulation over downlighters; ducts unlagged, crushed or disconnected; eaves blocked; open-flued appliance below. ## Section 7: Recording and Referring

**In short:** Record what you saw, where, and what you could not see, with photographs. Under PAS 2035, roof defects, leaks, condensation and mould go into the assessment and then into one of two categories: repair before retrofit, or recommended. Refer each finding to the person who can act: roofer, plumber, electrician, damp and timber specialist, structural engineer, or the Retrofit Coordinator and designer. Where loft or room-in-roof insulation was funded under ECO4 or GBIS, name TrustMark first.

### What PAS 2035 asks you to record

PAS 2035:2023 7.3.2 asks the assessment to include an appraisal of "structure, construction and condition" (b); "the location and severity" of defects or leaks, and of any condensation and/or mould growth (d); and the main materials, U-values and moisture properties of "exposed floors, walls and roofs" (g). The assessment, with "a photographic record", goes to the Retrofit Designer (7.4.1). Defects, leaks, condensation or mould are reported in two categories (7.4.2): those that "need to be repaired before any retrofit work can proceed", and those "recommended but not an essential prerequisite". MER-RA-002 covers the reporting detail.

Record clearly enough for the coordinator and designer to place each finding. Which category it falls in, in a given house, is their decision.

### Photographs

Use the PAS 2035 9.2 NOTE 2 list as a mirror. Before works, from a safe position, photograph: the hatch, open and closed; insulation depth with a rule in several places (convention 3.04); each eaves you can see, and any vents; the underlay; tank, pipes and overflow; cables, junction boxes and downlighter backs; ducts and where they end; any staining, wetting or mould, close and wide; and the roof from the ground.

If ECO4 loft insulation has been installed, a signed "pre-existing loft declaration" should be left in every loft space, "close to the loft hatch" (ECO4 Delivery v4.0, 5.26 and 5.27). If you see one, photograph it.

### Grant-funded loft or room-in-roof insulation: TrustMark first

If the householder says their loft or room-in-roof insulation was installed under ECO4 or the Great British Insulation Scheme (GBIS), point them to TrustMark first. Under ECO4, compliance with PAS is evidenced through the TrustMark Quality Assurance Framework, and PAS measures "should be installed by or under the responsibility of a TrustMark registered installer" (ECO4 Delivery v4.0, 4.19). PAS 2035:2023 names TrustMark as an applicable quality assurance regime (9.3.2, NOTE 2).

## Key Takeaways

- Enter a loft only where access is safe and practicable; stand on boards or joists, lift nothing, and record "access, loft insulation unknown" where you could not go in (RdSAP convention 3.04).
- Know the roof type first. Cold lofts put timbers, felt, tanks and pipes in a colder space; warm roofs bring the structure inside; rooms in the roof surround heated rooms with cold pockets in one air space.
- Moisture reaches a loft mainly as moist air through the hatch, ceiling penetrations and ceilings, plus open tanks; ventilation carries it out. Insulation pushed into the eaves can reduce that, and impervious felt can trap moisture.
- Ceiling insulation makes the loft colder. On RdSAP Table 16, the first 100 mm saves 85.5 W/K over a 45 m² ceiling; 100 to 270 mm saves 10.8 W/K.
- ADL asks for roof condensation risk to be assessed (Table C1) and, in its new-dwelling guidance applied "where appropriate" to existing homes, insulation continuous at the eaves; Historic England asks for insulation to be kept free at the eaves for ventilation. Reconciling the two is design work.
- Condensation, roof leaks, plumbing leaks and cold eaves junctions can look alike and coexist. Record location, extent and what is above, below and outside; do not name the cause.
- In rooms in the roof, ventilating the cold pockets is "critical to managing moisture" (BEIS para 83), and rafter depth limits what insulation alone can achieve.
- Record tanks, pipes, cables, downlighters and ducts, and refer to the right trade. For ECO4 or GBIS loft or room-in-roof insulation, point the householder to TrustMark first and let TrustMark say which route applies.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** Why does Historic England say that insulating a loft at ceiling level increases the risk of freezing?

A) Insulation absorbs water from the tank
B) The insulation keeps heat in the rooms below, so the roof space becomes colder
C) Ventilation at the eaves is always closed off by the installer
D) Loft insulation lowers the water pressure in the pipes

**Correct answer: B.** HEAG077 s. 3.6 says "the insulation at ceiling level will make the roof space colder thus increasing the risk of freezing", which is why tanks and pipes in a cold loft should be insulated and the area below a tank left clear unless the tank is raised (Sections 3 and 6).

---

**Q2.** Using RdSAP 10 Table 16, column (1), how much does the heat loss coefficient of a 45 m² ceiling fall when insulation is increased from 100 mm to 270 mm?

A) 85.5 W/K
B) 10.8 W/K
C) 2.25 W/K
D) 18.0 W/K

**Correct answer: B.** 0.40 × 45 = 18.0 W/K at 100 mm; 0.16 × 45 = 7.2 W/K at 270 mm; 18.0 − 7.2 = 10.8 W/K. The 85.5 W/K saving is from none to 100 mm (Section 3).

---

**Q3.** In a cold loft you find droplets and mould spots on the underside of black bituminous felt over the bathroom, insulation pushed into the eaves, and an open gap around the bathroom fan duct where it passes through the ceiling. What is the appropriate record?

A) "Condensation caused by bathroom; fit a new fan"
B) "Roof leak above bathroom"
C) A description of the droplets, mould, felt, eaves and duct gap, with photographs, referred to the Retrofit Coordinator for the designer
D) Nothing, because the loft is outside the assessment

**Correct answer: C.** PAS 2035:2023 7.3.2 d) asks for the location and severity of condensation and mould. You describe what you saw and refer; you do not name the cause or specify the remedy (Sections 2, 4 and 7).

---

**Q4.** What does the BEIS room-in-roof guide say about ventilation in a room-in-roof retrofit?

A) Rooms in the roof do not need any ventilation once insulated
B) A minimum 50 mm ventilation gap should be kept between insulation and membrane, and ventilating the enclosed loft spaces around the room is critical to managing moisture
C) Ventilation gaps should be filled to improve the U-value
D) Only flat ceilings need ventilating

**Correct answer: B.** Table 1 (p. 21) gives the 50 mm gap, and para 83 says ventilation of enclosed loft spaces surrounding the room in roof "is critical to managing moisture" (Section 5).

---

**Q5.** A householder says their loft insulation was installed under ECO4 last year and asks what to do about wet insulation and a musty smell. What should you do?

A) Tell them TrustMark's free Find & Fix audit covers loft insulation
B) Lift the insulation to find the source
C) Record what you saw, recommend professional advice, and point them to TrustMark first, on 0333 555 1234, to ask which route applies
D) Tell them to complain to the energy supplier only

**Correct answer: C.** Under ECO4, compliance with PAS is evidenced through TrustMark (ECO4 Delivery v4.0, 4.19). The free audit pages opened for this course describe external wall insulation, so you should not say it covers lofts; the householder should ask TrustMark which route applies. You do not lift insulation (Introduction and Section 7).
