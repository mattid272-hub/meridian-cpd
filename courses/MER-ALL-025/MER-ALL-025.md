## Learning Objectives

By the end of this course you should be able to:

- Describe where homes in Great Britain sit on airtightness, using the DESNZ 2025 baseline study, and explain why "less airtight" and "highly airtight" are ADF terms for new work, not labels for the stock.

- Work out the whole dwelling ventilation rate, extract rates, background ventilator areas, door undercut and purge openings for a sample house, with the arithmetic shown.

## Introduction

Ventilation comes up on almost every assessment visit. A householder asks whether the new windows have made the house stuffy; a retrofit package includes cavity wall insulation, new windows and a sealed loft hatch, and somebody has to say whether the home will still get enough fresh air. To answer, you need a clear picture of what ventilation is for, how much air ADF expects, and how existing homes are actually ventilated.

Part 1 of the Ventilation Series gives you that picture. Part 2 covers the systems as you meet them on site: what each looks like, how each goes wrong, and what you record. Part 3 covers commissioning, flow measurement and the 2027 changes.

This course does not train you to design a ventilation system. Under PAS 2035:2023 the Retrofit Designer confirms whether existing ventilation is adequate when the package or the assessment calls for it (Annex C, C.2.1). Your job is to know what the guidance expects, so that your record is useful to the designer and accurate for the householder.

This course does not repeat three related Meridian courses:
- **MER-RA-008, Ventilation Assessment in Retrofit:** the PAS 2035 clauses, Table 3.1 in detail, and Annex C.
- **MER-DEA-003, Ventilation in RdSAP 10:** how ventilation is entered in RdSAP.
- **MER-ALL-018, Damp, Condensation and Thermal Bridging:** relative humidity, dew point, surface temperature and the moisture physics behind condensation.

## Section 1: Four Ventilation Jobs

**In short:** Think of ventilation as four separate jobs. Extract removes moisture and pollutants at the source, in kitchens, bathrooms and toilets. Whole dwelling ventilation supplies fresh air at a low, steady rate. Purge clears a room quickly when needed. Infiltration is the uncontrolled air that leaks through gaps and cracks. ADF says infiltration should be counted as part of an existing home's ventilation, so sealing gaps can reduce ventilation even when no vent is touched.

### What ADF asks ventilation to do

ADF sets out its ventilation strategy as a combination of three things (2021 paragraph 1.9; 2026 paragraph 1.5):
- **Extract ventilation** from rooms "where water vapour or pollutants are likely to be released", such as kitchens and bathrooms, to limit their spread through the house.
- **Whole dwelling ventilation** "to provide fresh air to the building" and to dilute and remove what extract does not catch.
- **Purge ventilation** to remove high concentrations of pollutants and water vapour from occasional activities, with "fumes from painting" as ADF's example.

ADF's glossary (Appendix A) calls whole dwelling ventilation "nominally continuous" at "a relatively low rate", and purge "manually controlled" at "a relatively high rate".

### The fourth job: infiltration

ADF defines infiltration as "the uncontrolled exchange of air" between inside and outside "through gaps and cracks" (Appendix A). Nobody designs it, but in existing homes it does a large share of the work. Two passages make the point, in the same words in the 2021 and 2026 editions:
- Paragraph 3.6 opens: "Many existing dwellings are ventilated through infiltration rather than purposeful ventilation."
- The note to paragraph 3.2 says ventilation through infiltration "should be considered to be part of the ventilation provision". It adds that reducing infiltration "might reduce the indoor air quality" below the standards in ADF Appendix B.

PAS 2035:2023 Annex C, C.1, reports that infiltration and air leakage provide "approximately three-quarters" of the required ventilation in UK dwellings, citing BRE research. So energy efficiency work can change ventilation without anyone touching a fan or a vent: draught-proofing, new windows, a sealed floor or a blocked chimney all reduce the uncontrolled air.

### The four jobs side by side

| Job | What it does | What you see on site | Where ADF sets the numbers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extract | Removes moisture and pollutants where they are made | Kitchen fan or ducted cooker hood, bathroom and WC fans, continuous extract units | Table 1.1 (intermittent), Table 1.2 (continuous) |
| Whole dwelling | Supplies fresh air at a low, steady rate to habitable rooms | Background ventilators (trickle vents), continuous mechanical systems | 0.3 l/s per m² and Table 1.3; Table 1.7 for vent areas |
| Purge | Clears a room quickly after an occasional activity | Openable windows and external doors, or a mechanical system | Four air changes per hour per room; Table 1.4 for opening sizes |
| Infiltration | Uncontrolled air through gaps and cracks | Nothing designed: gaps at floors, frames, hatches, service holes, open chimneys | No ADF rate for existing homes; Appendix B assumes 0.15 air changes per hour where the Section 1 guidance for "less airtight" dwellings is followed |

### Worked numbers: how much air ADF assumes infiltration supplies

ADF Appendix B sets out the assumptions behind its rates. Where the guidance for less airtight dwellings is followed, dwellings "are assumed to have an infiltration rate of 0.15 air changes per hour" (B7); for highly airtight dwellings the assumed rate is zero (B8). Both editions say the same.

Take a two-storey house with 90 m² of internal floor area and, as an assumption for this example only, a 2.4 m ceiling height:
- Volume: 90 m² × 2.4 m = 216 m³.
- Infiltration at 0.15 air changes per hour: 216 × 0.15 = 32.4 m³/h.
- In litres per second: 32.4 ÷ 3.6 = 9.0 l/s.

Section 4 shows that a three-bedroom house of this size needs a whole dwelling rate of 31 l/s. The 0.15 figure is an assumption behind ADF's Section 1 rates, not a measurement of any existing home. It does show why ADF treats a "highly airtight" dwelling differently: with assumed infiltration at zero, purpose-provided ventilation does all the work.

### Why the separation matters on site

Each piece of equipment does one or two jobs, not all four. A bathroom fan extracts while it runs but supplies no fresh air to the bedrooms. A trickle vent supplies whole dwelling air but will not clear a steamed-up kitchen. A window can purge a room, but ADF does not count a window on its night latch as background ventilation (2021 note to 1.52). A record that says only "ventilation present" does not tell the designer which jobs are covered.

ADF states the purpose: without adequate ventilation, "mould and internal air pollution might become hazardous to health" (1.1, both editions). MER-ALL-018 covers the moisture physics.

## Section 2: How Airtight Is the Existing Stock?

**In short:** Use the 2025 DESNZ baseline when you discuss how leaky homes in Great Britain are. It measured 5,125 dwellings, weighted to represent the GB stock: median air permeability 8.1 m³/(h·m²) at 50 Pa, middle half 6.5 to 10.2. You should not treat ADF's "less airtight" and "highly airtight" lines as a stock benchmark: they are framed around design and as-built figures for building work. Air permeability measures leakiness, not how much air a home actually gets.

### The DESNZ baseline, 2025

The most recent large measured study is *Baseline airtightness of the GB housing stock* (DESNZ Research Paper 2025/002, September 2025), by Roberts, Li, Allinson and Lomas of the Building Energy Research Group at Loughborough University. It used the low-pressure pulse method. From 12,277 test results at 8,933 addresses, it cleaned the data to 5,125 dwellings, which were then weighted to represent the GB stock (pp. 5, 36).

The headline results, all in m³/(h·m²) at 50 Pa (pp. 36 to 37):
- Mean 8.6 ± 3.3.
- Median 8.1.
- Interquartile range 6.5 to 10.2, so half the sample sat between those two figures.

The report's own summary is that "GB dwellings are more airtight than previous studies suggest" (p. 5).

| Group (Table 6, p. 37) | Mean air permeability, m³/(h·m²) at 50 Pa |
|---|---|
| Built before 1919 | 8.9 |
| 1919 to 1944 | 8.7 |
| 1945 to 1964 | 8.1 |
| 1965 to 1980 | 7.9 |
| 1981 to 1990 | 8.6 |
| After 1990 | 9.3 |
| Flats | 7.3 |
| Bungalows | 7.6 |
| Detached | 8.7 |
| Terraced | 9.1 |
| Semi-detached | 9.2 |

The spread between age bands is small, and the newest band has the highest mean. These are group means; they give you no basis for predicting any one house.

### What the study does not tell you

Two limits are stated in the report itself:
- **It measured leakiness, not ventilation.** The report says: "There was no evidence for the ventilation rate of the existing UK housing stock" (p. 11).
- **An often-quoted model estimate is not a finding.** The report cites an estimate that "up to 79% of the current English housing stock could require additional purpose-provided ventilation", but describes the model behind it as "unvalidated" (p. 30). Do not quote the 79% as a measured result.

### Air permeability is not a ventilation rate

ADF defines air permeability as the air leakage rate per hour per m² of envelope area "at the test reference pressure differential of 50Pa or 4Pa" (Appendix A). It describes how easily air passes through the fabric under test, not how much air the home gets in ordinary weather.

Watch the units. PAS 2035:2023 Annex C, C.1, quotes an average of "11.5 ach at 50 Pa" from earlier BRE research. Air changes per hour depend on the volume of the home; the DESNZ figures are per m² of envelope area. Do not put the two side by side.

### ADF's airtightness terms

ADF uses two labels, defined in Appendix A:
- **Highly airtight dwellings** achieve a design air permeability lower than 5 m³/(h·m²) at 50 Pa, or an as-built air permeability lower than 3. (The 2026 edition reads "one or both of the following".)
- **Less airtight dwellings** are "those dwellings that are not highly airtight dwellings".

These labels decide which system types ADF's guidance covers (Section 3). They are framed around design and as-built figures for building work, not around a survey of existing homes.

### Worked numbers: the stock against ADF's line

- Median 8.1 ÷ design line 5 = 1.62, so the median is about 62% above the design line.
- The lower quartile boundary, 6.5, is also above 5.

So most homes in the sample sit on the "less airtight" side of the design line. The parts of the report opened for this course do not give the share below 5 or below 3, so none is stated. A home's own figure can only come from a test of that home (MER-DEA-021 covers RdSAP entry).

Retrofit work moves a home along this scale. ADF 3.6 says energy efficiency measures "might reduce infiltration and cause the dwelling to become under-ventilated". Section 5 shows how ADF estimates that effect without a test.

## Section 3: The Systems ADF Names, and What Sits Outside Them

**In short:** Learn the three systems in ADF Table 1.6: natural ventilation (background ventilators with intermittent extract fans), continuous mechanical extract, and mechanical ventilation with heat recovery (MVHR). Natural ventilation is covered only for less airtight dwellings; the other two cover all dwellings. PIV, passive stack and single-room heat recovery units are not Table 1.6 systems. ADF says other systems may be acceptable if they meet an equal level of performance, and that expert advice should be sought outside the table.

### Table 1.6

ADF Table 1.6 lists three types of ventilation system and the dwellings each one covers. The list is the same in the England 2021 and 2026 editions and in the Welsh 2022 and 2026 editions.

| System | What it consists of | Dwellings covered | England paragraphs (2021 and 2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural ventilation | Background ventilators, intermittent extract fans in wet rooms, windows for purge | Less airtight dwellings only | 1.47 to 1.59 |
| Continuous mechanical extract ventilation | A central extract unit, individual room extract fans, or both, running all the time; background ventilators in habitable rooms | All dwellings | 1.60 to 1.66 |
| Mechanical ventilation with heat recovery | Supply to every habitable room and extract from every wet room, with no background ventilators | All dwellings | 1.67 to 1.73 |

The 2026 England edition spells out "All dwellings" as "both less airtight dwellings and highly airtight dwellings". In the Welsh 2026 edition the same three systems are covered at paragraphs 1.56 to 1.83.

### Natural ventilation

ADF uses "natural ventilation" narrowly: it "refers to a ventilation strategy using background ventilators and intermittent extract ventilation" (Appendix A). A house with trickle vents and switched kitchen and bathroom fans is, in ADF's terms, naturally ventilated. The guidance is headed "guidance suitable only for less airtight dwellings"; where such a home tests as highly airtight, expert advice should be sought or continuous mechanical extract installed (1.46).

### Continuous mechanical extract ventilation

ADF 1.60 says a continuous mechanical extract system "could consist of" a central extract system, individual room extract fans (labelled "decentralised" in 2026), or both. Background ventilators are still needed, but not in wet rooms (1.64(a)). The 2021 edition notes that "an intermittent extract fan may be replaced" with a decentralised continuous fan (note to 1.46); the 2026 edition does not repeat that note there. MER-DEA-003 covers the RdSAP entries.

### Mechanical ventilation with heat recovery

With MVHR, each habitable room should have mechanical supply (1.67), each wet room should have extract at the Table 1.2 high rate (1.70), and the system should be designed so that moist air from wet rooms does not recirculate to habitable rooms (1.71). ADF says background ventilators "should not be installed" with MVHR, "to avoid unintended air pathways" (1.72). Where MVHR is added to an existing home after energy efficiency work, existing background ventilators "should be covered or sealed shut" (3.11(c) and 3.12(c), both England editions; Welsh 2026 3.11).

### What sits outside Table 1.6

ADF does not say that only three systems are allowed. Paragraph 1.11 (2021; 1.7 in 2026) says the systems in the document are examples, and "other ventilation systems may be acceptable" if they meet "an equal level of performance". The note under Table 1.6 says that outside the table's scope, "expert advice should be sought" for design, sizing and position. ADF's glossary gives examples of people able to give expert advice, including a chartered or professional engineer and a building services specialist.

**Positive input ventilation (PIV).** A search of the full England 2021 and 2026 texts finds no mention of positive input ventilation. It is not one of the Table 1.6 systems, so ADF's general provisions for other systems apply: 1.11 (2021) or 1.7 (2026), and the expert advice note. RdSAP does recognise it: convention 7.03e lists "PIV from outside" and "PIV from loft" in its order of precedence for homes with two systems (RdSAP 10 conventions v12.2). MER-DEA-003 covers how PIV is entered, and Part 2 covers what the evidence on PIV does and does not show.

**Passive stack ventilation.** The England 2021 and 2026 texts do not mention passive stack ventilators. The Welsh editions do: in a kitchen or bathroom refurbishment, existing fans "or passive stack ventilators should be retained or replaced" (Wales 2022 and 2026, 3.30). In RdSAP, where vents other than air bricks are present and mechanical ventilation cannot be confirmed, the vents are entered as passive stack (convention 7.03a). Older reports may use system numbers from earlier ADF editions; the 2010 edition was not opened, so they are not explained here.

**Single-room heat recovery ventilators.** ADF names these for two situations in existing homes, not as a whole dwelling system: an added habitable room, with its share of the whole dwelling rate set by room volume (3.18(a)); and an added wet room, at the Table 1.2 high rate with 50% as the continuous rate and no background ventilator needed in that room (3.25(c), 3.26). Exhaust air heat pumps are left to the Heating Series.

**How common they are.** Van Rooyen and Sharpe (2024) analysed 1,861 self-reported responses to a YouGov survey of adults in England, Wales and Scotland in June 2022 (p. 3). Of those, 3% (n = 55) reported MVHR and 2% (n = 42) PIV (p. 5); the bathroom section gives a different PIV count (n = 48, p. 6). Treat the figures as a sign that both are uncommon, not as a count.

### Combustion appliances: one safety line

ADF warns that fans "might cause combustion gases to spill from open-flued appliances", even when fan and appliance are in different rooms (0.17), and that combustion appliances "must operate safely whether or not fans are running" (0.18, both editions). Record any open-flued appliance and any extract fan in the same home; Part 2 covers referral.

## Section 4: Rates and Areas: Working the Numbers for a Sample House

**In short:** Work out ADF's numbers in order. The whole dwelling rate is the higher of 0.3 l/s per m² of floor area and the Table 1.3 figure for the number of bedrooms. Extract rates come from Table 1.1 for intermittent fans and Table 1.2 for continuous ones, and the two tables are not interchangeable. Background ventilator areas come from Table 1.7 or the continuous extract rules. Door undercuts and purge openings complete the picture.

### The sample house

The worked examples use one house throughout:
- Two-storey, three-bedroom semi-detached house.
- Internal floor area 90 m² across both floors.
- Rooms: living room, separate kitchen, three bedrooms, bathroom with WC.
- Habitable rooms: four (the living room and three bedrooms). ADF's glossary excludes a room that is "solely a kitchen" from the habitable room definition, so the kitchen is counted separately.

### Whole dwelling ventilation rate

ADF says the minimum whole dwelling rate should meet both of two conditions (2021 1.24; 2026 1.20; Wales 2022 1.24; Wales 2026 1.29):
- At least 0.3 litres per second per m² of internal floor area, adding all floors together.
- At least the figure in Table 1.3 for the number of bedrooms.

| Bedrooms | Table 1.3 minimum (l/s) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 19 |
| 2 | 25 |
| 3 | 31 |
| 4 | 37 |
| 5 | 43 |

Table 1.3 adds 6 l/s for each bedroom beyond five, and uses 13 l/s where a dwelling has only one habitable room. The values are the same in all four editions covered here.

**Worked numbers, sample house:**
- Floor area condition: 0.3 × 90 = 27 l/s.
- Bedroom condition: 31 l/s.
- Both conditions should be met, so the higher figure governs: **31 l/s**.

**The same house after a rear extension takes the floor area to 120 m², still with three bedrooms:**
- Floor area condition: 0.3 × 120 = 36 l/s.
- Bedroom condition: 31 l/s.
- The higher figure governs: **36 l/s**.

**Where the two conditions cross.** Each Table 1.3 figure ÷ 0.3 gives the floor area above which floor area governs: 19 ÷ 0.3 = 63.3 m² (1 bedroom); 25 ÷ 0.3 = 83.3 m² (2); 31 ÷ 0.3 = 103.3 m² (3); 37 ÷ 0.3 = 123.3 m² (4); 43 ÷ 0.3 = 143.3 m² (5).

**What 31 l/s means as air changes.** With the 2.4 m ceiling height assumed in Section 1:
- 31 l/s × 3.6 = 111.6 m³/h.
- 111.6 ÷ 216 m³ = 0.52 air changes per hour.

The ceiling height is an assumption. ADF does not express the whole dwelling rate in air changes, and a different ceiling height gives a different answer.

### Extract rates

ADF says extract to the outside should be provided in kitchens, utility rooms, bathrooms and sanitary accommodation (2021 1.17; 2026 1.13). Rates differ depending on whether the fan runs only when needed or all the time.

| Room | Intermittent, Table 1.1 (l/s) | Continuous high rate, Table 1.2 (l/s) |
|---|---|---|
| Kitchen, cooker hood extracting to outside | 30 | 13 |
| Kitchen, no hood or hood not extracting to outside | 60 | 13 |
| Utility room | 30 | 8 |
| Bathroom | 15 | 8 |
| Sanitary accommodation (WC) | 6 (or purge by window instead) | 6 |

For continuous systems, Table 1.2 also says the sum of all extract on its continuous rate should be at least the whole dwelling rate from Table 1.3. A recirculating cooker hood "on its own does not provide a means of ventilation" that complies with Part F (note to Diagram 1.2).

**Worked numbers, the sample kitchen with no ducted hood:**
- Intermittent: 60 l/s × 3.6 = 216 m³/h while the fan runs.
- Continuous high rate: 13 l/s × 3.6 = 46.8 m³/h.

The intermittent figure is 4.6 times the continuous high rate (60 ÷ 13). The tables answer different questions, so when you record a fan, record which kind it is.

**Worked numbers, the sample house on continuous extract:** kitchen high rate 13 + bathroom high rate 8 = 21 l/s. The continuous total should still reach the whole dwelling rate of 31 l/s (Table 1.2 and 1.62), so the high rates are a per-room floor and the continuous total a separate whole-house check.

### Positions and controls

Positional rules you will meet on site (2021 numbering; 2026 in brackets where different):
- Extract terminals should be as high as practicable and no more than 400 mm below the ceiling, cooker hoods excepted (1.20; 1.16).
- Background ventilators should be at least 1700 mm above the floor but easy to reach (1.34; 1.30), with equivalent area marked (1.16; 1.12), and at least 500 mm from a fan in the same room (1.55).
- Humidity controls should not be used for sanitary accommodation, "where odour is the main pollutant" (1.36; 1.32).
- In a room with no openable window, an intermittent fan should run on for at least 15 minutes (1.51).

ADF says background ventilators are intended to be left open (2021 note to 1.34 adds "normally"; 2026 note to 1.30). The damp and mould guidance for rented housing (MHCLG, DHSC and UKHSA, updated 1 April 2026) says trickle vents "should be controllable by the tenant, rather than permanently open". The two fit together, since ADF also asks for controllable ventilation (1.33; 1.29). Part 2 looks at how vents are used.

### Background ventilator areas

ADF gives sizes as equivalent area in mm², "not as a free area" (2021 1.15; 2026 1.11), so the marked figure matters more than the size of the slot.

**Natural ventilation (Table 1.7; the same values in the England 2021 and 2026 editions and the Welsh 2026 edition):**

| Room | Dwellings on more than one floor | Single-storey dwellings |
|---|---|---|
| Habitable room | 8,000 mm² | 10,000 mm² |
| Kitchen | 8,000 mm² | 10,000 mm² |
| Bathroom | 4,000 mm² | 4,000 mm² |
| Utility room, sanitary accommodation | No minimum | No minimum |

There should be at least five ventilators in the habitable rooms and kitchen together (four in a one-bedroom home) (1.57). Table 1.7 is "not appropriate" where the home has only one exposed façade, at least 70% of its openings on one façade, or a kitchen with no window or external wall for a ventilator; in those cases expert advice should be sought (note 1 to Table 1.7).

**Worked numbers, sample house on natural ventilation:**
- Four habitable rooms × 8,000 = 32,000 mm².
- Kitchen 8,000 mm².
- Bathroom 4,000 mm².
- Total at least 44,000 mm².
- Count in habitable rooms and kitchen: five rooms with one ventilator each gives five, which meets 1.57.

**Continuous mechanical extract (1.64, both England editions):** background ventilators should not be in wet rooms, should give at least 4,000 mm² in each habitable room, and should number at least the bedrooms plus two.

**Worked numbers, sample house on continuous extract:**
- Area: four habitable rooms × 4,000 = at least 16,000 mm², with no ventilators in the kitchen or bathroom.
- Number: 3 bedrooms + 2 = at least 5 ventilators.
- With four habitable rooms and five ventilators, at least one room carries two.

**MVHR:** none, per 1.72.

### Door undercuts

Internal doors should give a free area equivalent to "a 10mm undercut in a 760mm wide door" (2021 1.25; 2026 1.21; Wales 2026 1.30). The undercut should be 10 mm above a fitted floor finish, or 20 mm above the floor surface if the finish is not yet fitted.

**Worked numbers:** 10 mm × 760 mm = 7,600 mm² of free area per door: the path from supply in habitable rooms to extract in wet rooms.

### Purge openings

Each habitable room should have a means of purge ventilation (2021 1.26; 2026 1.22) capable of "at least four air changes per hour per room directly to the outside" (2021 1.27; 2026 1.23). Where windows or doors provide it, Table 1.4 sets the minimum opening area:

| Opening type | Minimum total opening area |
|---|---|
| Hinged or pivot window opening 15° to 30° | 1/10 of the room's floor area |
| Hinged or pivot window opening 30° or more | 1/20 of the room's floor area |
| Opening sash window | 1/20 of the room's floor area |
| External door | 1/20 of the room's floor area |

Hinged or pivot windows opening less than 15° "are not suitable for purge ventilation" (2021 1.31; 2026 1.27). The Table 1.4 values rest on stated assumptions from BS 5925: single-sided ventilation, an urban setting, a 2.1 m/s wind and a 3°C temperature difference (2021 1.29).

**Worked numbers, a 12 m² bedroom:**
- Casement opening 30° or more: 12 ÷ 20 = 0.6 m² of opening.
- Casement opening between 15° and 30°: 12 ÷ 10 = 1.2 m².
- Casement restricted to under 15°: not suitable for purge, whatever its size. Note restrictors for that reason.

## Section 5: Work to Existing Dwellings, in One Page

**In short:** When energy efficiency work reduces infiltration, ADF expects someone to check what extra ventilation is needed. The simplified method counts past and planned measures as major or minor, then reads a category, A, B or C, from Diagram 3.1. Category A should need no further provision; B and C should. ADF notes that following PAS 2035 is an adequate way to show compliance with its paragraph 3.6. The designer decides the upgrade; you record what is there.

### The starting point

ADF Section 3 separates two kinds of work (2021 and 2026, 3.1 and 3.2):
- Work that **includes** ventilation, such as adding a wet room or replacing extract fans, should meet the relevant standards.
- Other work that **affects** ventilation, such as replacing windows or doing energy efficiency work, should leave ventilation either meeting the standards or "not ... less satisfactory than before the work was carried out".

For energy efficiency work, 3.6 says building work "should not reduce the ventilation provision" unless what remains still meets F1(1). Paragraph 3.7 then says an assessment should decide what additional ventilation, if any, is needed. It names two routes: the simplified method in 3.8 to 3.13, or expert advice, "which may include" an air permeability test under Approved Document L. Both England editions and the Welsh 2026 edition use the same structure, and all three carry the note that "following BSI PAS 2035 is considered to be an adequate means" of showing compliance with 3.6.

The simplified method has a built-in assumption. It is designed for a dwelling "assumed to have adequate means of ventilation" before the work, through purpose-provided ventilation and infiltration together. If the home differs significantly from that, 3.6 still has to be met, through expert advice or another suitable method (note before 3.8).

### Counting measures

Paragraph 3.8 says to count, using Table 3.1, both the measures fitted "since the original dwelling was constructed" and the measures planned. Past work counts. A home that had cavity wall insulation ten years ago and new windows five years ago starts with those already on the tally.

Table 3.1 sorts eleven measures into minor and major. MER-RA-008 teaches the table in full; the examples below use four of them:
- Cavity wall insulation: minor.
- Renewing loft insulation with edge sealing: minor.
- Replacing more than 30% of windows or door units: major.
- External or internal wall insulation to more than 50% of the external wall area: major.

Diagram 3.1 turns the count into a category. The grid below was read from the page images of the England 2021 and 2026 editions, which are identical. The Welsh diagrams were not viewed.

What the categories mean (3.10 to 3.12, both England editions):
- **Category A:** the measures have likely not reduced ventilation below F1(1), "so no further ventilation provision is necessary".
- **Category B:** ventilation has likely been reduced below F1(1). Further provision should be made by natural ventilation to 1.47 to 1.59 (assuming existing purpose-built ventilators work and have not been compromised), continuous mechanical extract, or MVHR.
- **Category C:** ventilation has likely been reduced "significantly". Natural ventilation is then an option only by "following expert advice" on design, sizing and position; otherwise continuous mechanical extract or MVHR.

### Worked categories

- **Cavity wall insulation and renewed loft insulation:** 0 major, 2 minor → **A**.
- **Add replacement of more than 30% of the windows:** 1 major, 2 minor → **B**.
- **External wall insulation over 50%, sealing a suspended timber floor, and sealing a chimney:** 3 major, 0 minor → **B**.
- **The same three, plus renewed loft insulation:** 3 major, 1 minor → **C**.

One minor measure moved the last package from B to C, which is why a complete record of past work matters.

### Windows and refurbishments

Replacing windows has its own route (3.14 to 3.16): ventilators no smaller than before and controllable; where there were none, 8,000 mm² in habitable rooms and kitchens and 4,000 mm² in bathrooms, or 4,000 mm² per habitable room with continuous extract (3.15). England and Wales treat window-only work differently (Section 6). In a kitchen or bathroom refurbishment, existing fans "should be retained or replaced" and retained devices checked (3.30).

### Where PAS 2035 takes over

Under PAS 2035:2023, if the package of existing and specified measures falls into Category B or C, or the assessment finds condensation or mould, the Retrofit Designer "shall confirm the adequacy" of the existing ventilation and, if necessary, specify an upgrade (Annex C, C.2.1, via clause 8.2.13).

The assessor's part is the inspection and review of the existing ventilation in clause 7.3.2 f), reported to the designer as part of the whole dwelling assessment (7.4.1). PAS 2035 also warns that "the existence of a ventilation system is not proof" that a building is adequately ventilated (C.1). MER-RA-008 covers these clauses and Annex C in full. ADF's Appendix D checklist may also be used before work starts (3.13).

## Key Takeaways

- Ventilation does four jobs: extract at source, whole dwelling ventilation at a low steady rate, purge when needed, and uncontrolled infiltration. ADF says infiltration should be counted as part of an existing home's ventilation.
- ADF's design assumption, where its guidance for less airtight dwellings is followed, is 0.15 air changes per hour of infiltration; for highly airtight dwellings it is zero. That is an assumption, not a measurement of any home.
- The DESNZ 2025 baseline found a GB median air permeability of 8.1 m³/(h·m²) at 50 Pa (interquartile range 6.5 to 10.2). It measured leakiness, not ventilation, and its 79% figure comes from an unvalidated model.
- ADF Table 1.6 names three systems: natural ventilation (less airtight dwellings only), continuous mechanical extract and MVHR (all dwellings). PIV, passive stack and single-room heat recovery units sit outside the table; other systems may be acceptable at an equal level of performance.
- The whole dwelling rate is the higher of 0.3 l/s per m² and Table 1.3. For the sample three-bedroom, 90 m² house that is 31 l/s; at 120 m² it is 36 l/s.
- Intermittent (Table 1.1) and continuous (Table 1.2) extract rates answer different questions: 60 l/s against 13 l/s for a kitchen with no ducted hood.
- Diagram 3.1 turns a count of past and planned measures into Category A, B or C. Under PAS 2035, B or C, or condensation or mould, brings in the designer's adequacy check.
- England moves to the 2026 edition on 24 March 2027 and Wales on 4 March 2027. Rates are unchanged; paragraph numbers, some Welsh Table 3.1 wording and testing rules differ, so always cite the edition.

## Self-Assessment Questions

**Q1.** A two-storey, three-bedroom house has 120 m² of internal floor area. What is the minimum whole dwelling ventilation rate under ADF?

A) 27 l/s
B) 31 l/s
C) 36 l/s
D) 67 l/s

**Correct answer: C.** ADF asks for both 0.3 l/s per m² (0.3 × 120 = 36 l/s) and the Table 1.3 figure for three bedrooms (31 l/s) to be met, so the higher, 36 l/s, governs. D adds the two together, which ADF does not do (Section 4).

---

**Q2.** Which statement about infiltration matches ADF's guidance on existing dwellings?

A) Infiltration is excluded from ventilation because it is uncontrolled
B) Ventilation through infiltration should be considered part of the ventilation provision, and reducing it might lower indoor air quality
C) Infiltration only matters in homes built before 1919
D) ADF sets a minimum infiltration rate for existing homes

**Correct answer: B.** This is the note to paragraph 3.2 in both England editions. Paragraph 3.6 adds that many existing dwellings are ventilated through infiltration. ADF sets no infiltration rate for existing homes; Appendix B assumes 0.15 air changes per hour only as a design assumption where the Section 1 guidance for less airtight dwellings is followed (Section 1).

---

**Q3.** What did the DESNZ 2025 airtightness baseline find, and what does it not show?

A) A median of 8.1 m³/(h·m²) at 50 Pa; it gives no evidence on the ventilation rate of the existing stock
B) A median of 3 m³/(h·m²) at 50 Pa; most homes are highly airtight
C) That 79% of English homes need extra ventilation, as a measured result
D) That homes built before 1919 are three times leakier than newer homes

**Correct answer: A.** The weighted median was 8.1, with an interquartile range of 6.5 to 10.2. The report says there was no evidence for the ventilation rate of the existing stock, and calls the model behind the 79% estimate unvalidated. Means by age band range only from 7.9 to 9.3 (Section 2).

---

**Q4.** A planned package includes external wall insulation to more than 50% of the walls, sealing a suspended timber floor and sealing a chimney. The home already has renewed loft insulation with edge sealing. Using Diagram 3.1 (England), which category results?

A) Category A
B) Category B
C) Category C
D) Diagram 3.1 does not apply because the loft work was done earlier

**Correct answer: C.** That is 3 major and 1 minor measure, which Diagram 3.1 places in Category C. Paragraph 3.8 says past measures fitted since construction are counted with the planned ones, so D is wrong. Without the loft work, 3 major and 0 minor would be Category B (Section 5).

---

**Q5.** In Wales, which point differs from England in the guidance covered by this course?

A) Wales uses a whole dwelling rate of 0.5 l/s per m²
B) In a kitchen or bathroom refurbishment, Wales also says existing passive stack ventilators should be retained or replaced
C) Wales does not recognise MVHR
D) Wales has no Table 1.7

**Correct answer: B.** The Welsh 2022 and 2026 editions add passive stack ventilators to paragraph 3.30. Wales also treats replacing more than 30% of windows as major only when carried out with another measure, and requires flows to be measured on notifiable ventilation work in existing dwellings. In the Welsh 2026 edition as read, the rates, Table 1.6 systems and Table 1.7 areas match England (Sections 3, 4 and 6).
